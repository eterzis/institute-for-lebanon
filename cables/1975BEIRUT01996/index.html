Beirut, February 12, 1975 — 3:47 PM GMT

The Meeting Taqla Was “Delighted” To Survive

Fourteen days before Sidon erupted. Six weeks before King Feisal was assassinated. Eight weeks before the civil war began. Lebanon’s Foreign Minister sat down with the American Ambassador and described how he had just prevented the Arab League from sponsoring the militarization of southern Lebanon — and how Syria had kept its Palestinian proxy on a leash. The cable is candid, the confidence is high, and by early March nothing it describes would hold.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT01996 SECRET / EXDIS → UNCLASSIFIED

On the evening of February 11, 1975, U.S. Ambassador George McMurtrie Godley met privately with Lebanese Foreign Minister Philippe Taqla for forty minutes. Taqla had just returned from the Cairo Arab League Defense Council Meeting (February 5–7). He was delighted. Lebanon had successfully declined Arab military personnel, declined massive military assistance, and avoided substantive discussion of the PLO in Lebanon. A Libyan proposal for Qaddafi-financed fortified villages in southern Lebanon — dual-use positions for Lebanese defense and fedayeen operations — had been deflected into a study commission. The Saudi offer to reconstruct Kafr Shuba had been politely declined. Syria had kept Zuhayr Muhsin, the troublesome Saʿiqa leader, on his best behavior. Taqla was taking a Cabinet readout that day and a Parliamentary readout the next. He asked Godley to urge Israeli restraint in southern Lebanon. Godley, writing at 3:47 PM GMT, doubted Taqla would survive the parliamentary debate and declined to promise any American action with Israel. Within two weeks Sidon would erupt. Within six weeks Feisal would be dead. Within eight weeks the civil war would begin.

What this document reveals
  • Libya proposed — on the record at a formal Arab League meeting — to finance fortified villages in southern Lebanon that would serve as both Lebanese defense positions and fedayeen operational bases. Taqla deflected by promising to study the matter. The Libyan admission documented here — that the proposed villages would be dual-use — is the clearest possible evidence that senior Arab states were proposing to formalize PLO-armed presence on Lebanese soil with external Arab funding.
  • Syria kept Zuhayr Muhsin, the Saιiqa leader, silent. Taqla reports: “his Syrian masters had told him to behave himself.” This is direct, real-time documentation of Syrian operational control over Saιiqa — a control that would reverse within weeks, when Muhsin reappeared in the March 1975 Sidon Crisis cables as disruptive rather than quiescent. The same figure, opposite postures, three weeks apart.
  • Hafez al-Assad had sent Lebanon a “personal charitable offering” of $1 million. Taqla describes this explicitly as Assad’s personal gift rather than Syrian state aid — the exact distinction that let Damascus embed itself in Lebanese reconstruction politics without formal state-to-state commitment. Taqla’s handling of the Saudi offer (“if King Feisal... desired to make a similar charitable offer, it would be graciously received”) preserved the personal-gift framing while inviting parallel Saudi generosity.
  • The Arab League’s only position on the PLO in Lebanon was an informal, unrecorded consensus that fedayeen should stop raiding Israel from Lebanon. Not formalized. Not in the communiqué. Not binding. Two weeks later the Sidon Crisis would confirm that informal consensus had no operational value.
  • Taqla successfully prevented Arab military personnel from being deployed to Lebanon. Had the February 5–7 meeting resulted in formal Arab troop deployment to support Lebanon against Israeli incursions, the subsequent civil war would have been fundamentally different. The refusal — courteous, quietly firm — preserved Lebanese sovereignty against Arab-solidarity framings that were not being offered in Lebanon’s interest.
  • Taqla asked Washington to urge Israel to abstain from further military actions in southern Lebanon. Since Lebanon had no direct diplomatic relations with Israel, the American Embassy functioned as an indirect Lebanese-Israeli back-channel on restraint messaging. Godley’s reply — he would report the views to the Department and the Secretary but could give no indication of possible U.S. action — is characteristic of Kissinger-era American positioning on the Lebanese-Israeli border.
  • Godley’s editorial aside — “I personally believe this might be difficult” — refers to Taqla’s optimism about managing the Lebanese parliamentary debate. Within two weeks, Sidon erupted and consumed Lebanese politics. The Cairo results became irrelevant before the Parliament had finished debating them.

Key figures

Who was involved

G. McMurtrie Godley
U.S. Ambassador to Lebanon (Dec 1974 – Mar 1976)
Godley had presented credentials in Beirut on December 31, 1974. This February 12, 1975 cable, filed six weeks into his ambassadorial tenure, is among his earliest substantive Beirut reports. His practice of long private sessions with Foreign Minister Taqla immediately after major Arab events would recur throughout the year — most notably in the Sidon Crisis reporting of March 1975 (1975BEIRUT03203_03256).
P
Philippe Taqla
Foreign Minister of Lebanon (1974–1975)
Greek Orthodox veteran of Lebanese diplomacy, Foreign Minister under Prime Minister Rashid Solh. A Chehabist-aligned figure whose defensive Arab policy — accept symbolic solidarity, reject concrete militarization — shaped Lebanon’s posture at the Cairo meeting. Would leave office with the Solh cabinet after the April outbreak. His candor with Godley in this cable is exemplary of the Lebanese-American back-channel during the Kissinger shuttle era.
H
Public domain. Official portrait.
Hafez al-Assad
President of Syria (1971–2000)
The author of the “personal charitable offering” of $1 million to Lebanon — a technique that threaded Syrian influence through personal patronage while avoiding formal state-to-state commitment. Also the ultimate author of Zuhayr Muhsin’s cooperative posture at Cairo: Muhsin’s “Syrian masters had told him to behave himself”. The plasticity of Assad’s instructions — cooperative in February, disruptive by March — is one of the through-lines of the 1975 cable record.
K
King Feisal
King of Saudi Arabia (1964–1975)
The ruling Saudi monarch whose “press treatment” had produced the diplomatic contretemps Taqla had to smooth over with Saudi representatives at Cairo. His assassination by a nephew six weeks after this cable (March 25, 1975) would remove the Saudi architect of Saudi-Lebanese policy through the 1970s. Succeeded by King Khalid.
I
Ismail Fahmi
Egyptian Foreign Minister (1973–1977)
Sadat’s principal foreign-policy interlocutor during the Kissinger shuttle period. At the Cairo meeting, Fahmi was prepared to intervene helpfully on Taqla’s behalf against the Libyan when the exchange over fortified villages heated up. Fahmi’s resignation in November 1977 in protest of Sadat’s Jerusalem trip would mark the endpoint of the Egyptian-Arab coherence this cable documents.
A
Abdul Halim Khaddam
Syrian Foreign Minister (1970–1984)
Assad’s principal handler of Lebanon policy through the 1970s. Taqla’s claim that he had secured Saιiqa’s good behavior at Cairo by pre-arrangement with Khaddam — in Damascus in late January 1975 — documents the bilateral Lebanese-Syrian channel through which the Palestinian question was actually managed, outside Arab League deliberations.
Z
Zuhayr Muhsin
Secretary-General, Saιiqa (1971–1979)
The Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction leader whose “Syrian masters had told him to behave himself” at Cairo. Three weeks later he would reappear in the Sidon Crisis record (cable 1975BEIRUT03203_03256) playing the opposite role — disruptive rather than quiescent. Assassinated in Cannes, France, on July 25, 1979, by unknown assailants, possibly Mossad. One of the most notorious PLO-aligned operatives of the pre-war period.

President of Lebanon (1970–1976)
The Maronite President under whose authority Taqla conducted the Cairo mission. Frangieh’s government would fall to the civil war in 1976, but the February 1975 posture — preserving Lebanese sovereignty against Arab-sponsored militarization — was consistent with his long-standing position against expanded fedayeen rights on Lebanese territory (documented in the 1973 Melkart Protocol cable series).
The Record

What Taqla achieved in Cairo

The Cairo Arab League Defense Council Meeting of February 5–7, 1975 had a potentially transformative agenda. In the wake of Israeli incursions into southern Lebanon through late 1974, Arab states were offering to provide Lebanon with military personnel, substantial military aid, and formal Arab League sanction for Palestinian armed operations from Lebanese territory. A Libyan proposal envisioned Qaddafi-financed fortified villages along the border — dual-use positions for Lebanese defense and fedayeen operations.

Foreign Minister Philippe Taqla went to Cairo to make sure none of that happened.

He succeeded. Arab military personnel were not committed to Lebanon. Massive aid was not formalized. The PLO question was finessed. The Libyan proposal was deflected into a Lebanese study. The Saudi reconstruction offer for Kafr Shuba was declined with the nicety that Assad’s $1 million personal gift would be the model for any parallel Saudi charitable offer. The only concrete outcome was an informal consensus — not formalized in the communiqué — that fedayeen raids from Lebanon into Israel should stop.

Taqla returned to Beirut on the evening of February 8 pleased with his work. Three days later, he sat down with the new American Ambassador for a 40-minute private conversation. This cable is the product.

The Libyan position, on the record

The Libyan offer deserves particular attention for its historical documentary value. The Qaddafi regime proposed to finance “a series of fortified villages in southern Lebanon.” These, according to the Libyans and in Godley’s reporting, would be “kibbutz-like military strongholds” that would “oppose further Israeli incursions and would constitute positions from whence the fedayeen might operate.”

The Libyan admission — preserved verbatim in the cable — is historically unambiguous: a senior Arab state proposed, at a formal Arab League meeting, to construct Lebanese border villages configured explicitly as both Lebanese defensive positions and Palestinian offensive bases. This is the architecture of Lebanese sovereignty erosion, proposed on the record as Arab solidarity.

Taqla’s procedural deflection — “a Lebanese decision” to be studied on return — was an elegant no. It preserved the Lebanese government’s right to refuse without publicly humiliating Libya in front of the assembled Arab defense ministers.

The Syrian management of Saιiqa

One of the most striking passages in the cable concerns Zuhayr Muhsin, the Secretary-General of Saιiqa — the Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction. At the July 1974 predecessor meeting, Muhsin had been disruptive. At the February 1975 meeting, Taqla reports, he had “little to say and it was obvious his Syrian masters had told him to behave himself.”

Taqla attributes this to his own January 1975 Damascus talks with Syrian Foreign Minister Abdul Halim Khaddam. The cable thus documents, with the specificity of a single figure’s demeanor at a single meeting, the Syrian capacity to flip Palestinian faction behavior on command.

Three weeks later, that flip would reverse. In the March 12–14, 1975 Sidon Crisis cable (1975BEIRUT03203_03256), the same Zuhayr Muhsin appears in a very different posture — actively disruptive, Syria’s interests now diverging from Lebanon’s. The February cable captures the control mechanism; the March cable captures its reversal. Together, they document Syrian policy fluidity with unusual precision.

The request Godley didn’t promise to fulfill

Taqla closed the forty-minute meeting with a request: bring the Cairo results to the Israeli government’s attention, and urge Israeli restraint in southern Lebanon. Lebanon had no direct diplomatic relations with Israel; the American Embassy in Beirut was therefore functioning as an indirect Lebanese-Israeli channel on restraint messaging.

Godley’s response — he would report Taqla’s views to the Department and the Secretary, but could give no indication of possible U.S. action — is characteristic of the Kissinger-era American position. The United States was engaged in shuttle diplomacy in the Sinai (Kissinger would be in the region again in March) and wanted neither to commit to Lebanese-Israeli restraint enforcement nor to decline the role of conduit. The cable reflects that exact equipoise.

The short distance to the civil war

Reading this cable with the knowledge that the civil war began eight weeks later is disorienting. Taqla is confident. Godley is skeptical of the parliamentary politics but not of the Arab diplomatic achievement. The Arab League’s only concrete Lebanese outcomes — defensive weapons commitments and informal consensus against fedayeen cross-border raids — look reasonable. A Saudi-Lebanese press-coverage contretemps is being smoothed over.

Within two weeks of this cable, Sidon erupts (cable 1975BEIRUT02611_02682). The informal consensus against cross-border fedayeen raids proves unenforceable. Within six weeks, King Feisal — the Saudi architect whose press-coverage sensitivities had occupied a paragraph in this cable — is assassinated. Within eight weeks, the civil war has begun.

The Cairo meeting’s principal result — Lebanon’s successful defense of its sovereignty against Arab-sponsored militarization — would be irrelevant by May. The country would be fragmenting along the very fissures the Arab solidarity framework was supposed to paper over.

Taqla’s “delight”, captured here at 3:47 PM GMT on February 12, 1975, is the last recorded moment of Lebanese diplomatic confidence in the pre-war record.

Summary.
Foreign Minister Taqla[1] is delighted with the Cairo Arab League Defense Ministers Meeting (February 5–7, 1975)[2] in that Lebanon was able to dodge accepting Arab military personnel or massive Arab military assistance. He was also able to finesse the entire question of the PLO in Lebanon, except for a general admonition to the PLO to curtail terrorism from Lebanon into Israel.[3]

2.
There has been a great deal of speculation in the Lebanese and other press regarding the results of the Cairo meeting of February 5–7. Last evening I[4] had a 40-minute private conversation with Foreign Minister Taqla, who returned late on February 8 from Cairo. Taqla said he was extremely pleased with the results, which came out as he had planned. The meeting's atmospherics were cordial but businesslike, with little or no acrimony. The Arabs were generous in their offers but did not appear to be resentful when the Lebanese turned down offers of personnel and relatively sophisticated military equipment. No financial figure was reached, but the conference concluded that Arab states that could would supply the Lebanese Army with some artillery and anti-aircraft weapons.[5] Taqla was vague as to precisely what was involved, but stated the “soldiers had exchanged lists” and the Lebanese Army was relatively satisfied as to what it would get. Financial support for purchases abroad, Taqla said, was promised if Arab states did not have the required weaponry in their arsenals.

3.
Assistance for southern Lebanon was offered but, according to Taqla, courteously rejected as a Lebanese responsibility. At a meeting prior to the conference with the Saudi representatives, the latter offered large sums for the reconstruction of Kafr Shuba[6] and other villages, citing that they understood the GOL had accepted one million dollars from President Asad. Taqla responded that the million dollars was a personal charitable offering by President Asad[7] and that if King Feisal[8] or other Saudis desired to make a similar charitable offer, it would be graciously received.

4.
I asked Taqla where he stood with the Saudis (Embtel 1712 NOTAL), noting I had heard of a serious contretemps over Lebanese press treatment of King Feisal.[9] Taqla confirmed this was indeed the case and was one of the first things he had to straighten out, but was relieved that none of the contretemps was reflected in the attitude of the Saudi representatives at Cairo (believed to be the Acting Minister of Foreign Affairs and Deputy Minister of Defense). These men had been most cooperative and helpful on all occasions.

5.
As might be expected, Taqla said the only serious problem he really had to handle in Cairo was a Libyan offer to finance and help construct a series of fortified villages in southern Lebanon.[10] These kibbutz-like military strongholds, according to the Libyans, would oppose further Israeli incursions and would constitute positions from whence the fedayeen might operate.[11] Taqla responded that he did not share all the Libyan views on this question and, as it was a Lebanese decision, he would study the matter upon return to Beirut. The exchange with the Libyan heated up a bit, and Egyptian Foreign Minister Fahmi[12] was about to make a helpful intervention when Taqla was able to cut off further debate.

6.
Taqla indicated he was particularly happy that he was able to avoid any discussion of the PLO at the Cairo meeting. Unlike the July 1974 meeting[13] when there had been a good deal of discussion of Arab states supplying weaponry to the PLO, this subject was never broached. Except as mentioned above, Taqla said the only reference to the PLO was a broad informal consensus that the most helpful thing for Lebanon would be a curtailment of fedayeen raids from Lebanon into Israel.[14] This view was not formalized but, according to Taqla, came through loud and clear.

7.
Taqla was also delighted with the attitude of his Arab colleagues. The Syrians were particularly helpful, not only in the discussions but also in controlling Zuhayr Muhsin[15], who was one of the more troublesome elements in the July 1974 meeting. This time he had little to say and it was obvious his Syrian masters had told him to behave himself. Taqla attributed the Syrian attitude to his visit to Damascus and his talks with Khaddam[16] late last month.

8.
I pressed Taqla on the cash he had obtained from his Arab friends. He reiterated what he had said above (para 2) — that no amount was specified, although the representatives of wealthy states repeatedly took the position that money was available if required. Taqla will be presenting to the cabinet today a report on the Cairo meeting and tomorrow will be appearing before Parliament. While there is considerable local political pressure for a complete public report of the Cairo meeting, Taqla implied he will be able to avoid this, and said he was relatively optimistic that for the time being he could satisfy local political appetites for more parliamentary discussion on the military situation in the south. I personally believe this might be difficult.[17]

9.
Taqla summarized our discussion, pointing out that the Cairo meeting had really not changed the local Lebanese military situation. The weapons the Army is seeking are purely defensive and he doubts they will be arriving for some time. In concluding, he requested that we bring the foregoing to the attention of the GOI and try to convince the GOI to abstain from further military actions in the southern Lebanon.[18] I told the Minister I would of course report his views to the Department and the Secretary but could give him no indication as to what action we might be able to take.

GODLEY[19]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]Philippe Taqla (فيليب تقلاFilib Taqla): Greek Orthodox Christian, Lebanese Foreign Minister in 1974–1975 under Prime Minister Rashid Solh. A veteran of several earlier Lebanese cabinets (he had served as FM in the early 1960s), Taqla was a member of the independent political establishment closely aligned with the Chehabist legacy. His tenure in this period spans the April 1975 outbreak of the civil war; he left office with the Solh government in May 1975. His characterization of the Cairo meeting in this cable exemplifies the Lebanese Foreign Ministry’s defensive Arab policy: accept symbolic solidarity, reject concrete militarization of Lebanese territory.
[2]Cairo Arab League Defense Council Meeting (February 5–7, 1975): convened in the aftermath of a December 1974 Israeli incursion into southern Lebanon, ostensibly to respond with Arab military solidarity. The meeting’s practical significance lies less in what was decided than in what Lebanon successfully resisted: the deployment of Arab military personnel on Lebanese soil, and formal Arab League sponsorship of PLO militarization from Lebanese territory. The follow-up Cairo meeting fourteen days later (referenced throughout this cable as the July 1974 predecessor) had gone much further in both directions. This cable is the Lebanese Foreign Minister’s candid readout to the American Ambassador, on the record.
[3]"General admonition to the PLO to curtail terrorism from Lebanon into Israel": Taqla’s framing of the only PLO-related outcome of the meeting. The language “terrorism” — rather than “operations” or “resistance” — is the Lebanese diplomat’s own framing in an EXDIS cable to Washington. The “admonition” was informal, not formalized in the meeting’s communiqué (see paragraph 6). Its practical effect would be nil: the February 26, 1975 Sidon outbreak (cable 1975BEIRUT02611_02682), three weeks after this cable, would be followed by continuing fedayeen cross-border raids throughout the year.
[4]"I": Ambassador George McMurtrie Godley, who had presented credentials in Beirut on December 31, 1974. This 40-minute private conversation with Taqla, on the evening of February 11, 1975 (cabled Feb 12 at 1547Z), is one of Godley’s earliest substantive Embassy cables. His professional practice — a long private session with the Foreign Minister immediately after major Arab-world meetings — recurs throughout his 1975 reporting, including the Sidon Crisis cable series.
[5]Artillery and anti-aircraft weapons: the Arab League’s concrete commitment was confined to defensive systems. Critically, the meeting did NOT sanction supply of offensive weapons (tanks, aircraft, missiles) to Lebanon, and it did not sanction Arab military personnel deployment inside Lebanon. Taqla’s phrase “soldiers had exchanged lists” captures the quartermaster-level nature of the commitment: lists of available stocks, not operational or combat support.
[6]Kafr Shuba (كفر شوباKafr Shuba): a border village in southeastern Lebanon (Hasbaya district), subject to repeated Israeli reprisals for fedayeen cross-border raids from the surrounding Arqub zone. Its reconstruction was a symbolic priority after Israeli incursions. The Saudi offer to finance Kafr Shuba reconstruction was a soft-power gesture aligned with Saudi strategic interests (supporting a moderate pro-Western Lebanon without deepening Arab military footprint). Taqla’s polite refusal preserved the principle that Lebanese territory would be rebuilt by Lebanese decision.
[7]Hafez al-Assad’s $1 million "personal charitable offering" to Lebanon: notable as the paradigmatic example of how Assad threaded Syrian influence through personal patronage channels in parallel to formal state channels. By framing it as a personal gift (rather than Syrian state assistance), Assad avoided Lebanese resistance to institutional Syrian influence while still embedding Damascus in Lebanese reconstruction politics. Taqla’s rhetorical parallel — inviting Feisal to make "a similar charitable offer" — is diplomatic elegance: accepting Syrian generosity without conceding its political framing.
[8]King Feisal of Saudi Arabia (1906–1975): ruling monarch since 1964, a principal architect of the conservative pan-Islamic axis. His assassination by a nephew on March 25, 1975 — just six weeks after this cable — would remove the Saudi statesman who had shaped Saudi-Lebanese relations through the 1970s. The contretemps over Lebanese press treatment of him, referenced here, would be overtaken by events.
[9]The "serious contretemps over Lebanese press treatment of King Feisal" references Lebanese newspaper coverage that had offended Saudi sensibilities, likely in early 1975. The Embassy’s cable 1712 (referenced but not publicly available) documented the Saudi diplomatic démarche. The willingness of the Saudi representatives at Cairo to continue working with Taqla notwithstanding this dispute was important Lebanese diplomatic intelligence: it signaled that the Saudis distinguished between press-driven irritations and substantive bilateral cooperation.
[10]Libyan proposal for fortified villages in southern Lebanon: the single most consequential proposal at the meeting, from Lebanon’s perspective. The Qaddafi regime’s offer would have financed Lebanese border villages configured explicitly as defensive military positions doubling as fedayeen operational bases. This would have formalized — with external Arab funding — the very dual-use arrangement Lebanon was trying to unwind. Taqla’s deflection (study-the-matter-on-return) is a classic Lebanese diplomatic technique for saying no without saying no: procedural delay into oblivion.
[11]"Kibbutz-like military strongholds... positions from whence the fedayeen might operate": this is the explicit acknowledgment, in the Libyan position, that the fortified villages were intended as both defensive Lebanese positions and offensive PLO bases. The Libyan admission, preserved in the cable, is significant historically: it documents on the record that a senior Arab state was proposing exactly the kind of Palestinian-armed presence on Lebanese soil that the Lebanese government had spent the post-Cairo-Agreement years trying to circumscribe. Taqla’s rejection was therefore a defense of Lebanese sovereignty against the very possibility being offered in the name of Arab solidarity.
[12]Ismail Fahmi (إسماعيل فهميIsmail Fahmi): Egyptian Foreign Minister (1973–1977). A career diplomat, Fahmi was Sadat’s principal foreign-policy interlocutor during the Kissinger shuttle period. His posture in Cairo during this meeting — helpful to Taqla, prepared to intervene against the Libyan — fits the broader Egyptian-Lebanese alignment documented across the 1973–1975 cable record. Fahmi would famously resign in November 1977 in protest of Sadat’s Jerusalem trip.
[13]July 1974 Arab Defense Council meeting: documented in the Embassy’s cable 1974BEIRUT07889 (“Arab Defense Council Meeting”). Taqla’s reference here to the July 1974 precedent is the specific connection. The 1974 meeting had discussed Arab states supplying weaponry to the PLO in considerable detail; the February 1975 meeting, by Taqla’s design, did not. The contrast is the cable’s central diplomatic point.
[14]The "broad informal consensus" against fedayeen raids from Lebanon into Israel is paradoxically the strongest outcome of the meeting from the Lebanese perspective. Had it been formalized in the communiqué, it would have committed the PLO’s Arab sponsors publicly; had the Israelis understood it as a binding guarantee, they might have reduced cross-border reprisals. As an informal consensus, it was practically worthless — and events two weeks later (Sidon Crisis) would confirm as much.
[15]Zuhayr Muhsin (زهير محسنZuhayr Muhsin, 1936–1979): Secretary-General of Saιiqa — the Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction — and one of the most notorious PLO-aligned operatives of the pre-war period. His docility at the February 1975 Cairo meeting (“his Syrian masters had told him to behave himself”) is documented here as direct evidence of Syrian operational control over Saιiqa. Muhsin reappears in this project’s March 1975 Sidon Crisis cables (1975BEIRUT03203_03256), where he plays the opposite role — disruptive rather than controlled. The contrast across three weeks reveals the plasticity of Syrian direction over its Palestinian proxies.
[16]Abdul Halim Khaddam (عبد الحليم خدامAbd al-Halim Khaddam, 1932–2020): Syrian Foreign Minister (1970–1984), later Vice President. Khaddam handled the Syrian side of Lebanon policy for Hafez al-Assad through the 1970s. Taqla’s claim to have secured Saιiqa’s good behavior through personal talks with Khaddam in Damascus “late last month” (January 1975) documents the bilateral Lebanese-Syrian channel through which much of the Palestinian question was actually managed — outside formal Arab League deliberations.
[17]"I personally believe this might be difficult": Godley’s editorial aside on Taqla’s optimism about managing the Lebanese parliamentary discussion. The Ambassador’s skepticism proved justified — within two weeks of this cable, the Sidon Crisis (February 26, 1975) would erupt and consume Lebanese parliamentary debate for the next six weeks. The Cairo meeting’s results would be irrelevant by the time Parliament finished debating the southern military situation.
[18]The request to Washington to convey Taqla’s views to the Israeli government and urge Israeli restraint in southern Lebanon is noteworthy: it treats the U.S. as the Lebanese-Israeli back-channel. The Lebanese government had no direct diplomatic relations with Israel; the Embassy in Beirut was therefore functioning as an indirect conduit for Lebanese restraint messages to the Israelis. Godley’s reply — noncommittal, promising only to convey the views — is characteristic American fence-sitting on an issue where U.S. interests (Israeli security, regional stability) were not fully aligned.
[19]George McMurtrie Godley (1917–1999): U.S. Ambassador to Lebanon (December 1974 – March 1976). A career Foreign Service officer whose prior postings included Lao P.D.R. as Ambassador. Godley’s Beirut tenure coincided almost exactly with the first phase of the Lebanese civil war (April 1975 – 1976). The American Embassy was evacuated in April 1976; Godley was one of the last ambassadors to maintain a fully functioning mission through the war’s opening phase. This February 12, 1975 cable is among his earliest substantive reporting from Beirut.
