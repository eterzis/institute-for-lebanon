Beirut, March 6–7, 1975

The Day Maarouf Saad Died

Two cables filed March 6 and March 7, 1975 document the 24 hours in which the Sidon crisis became a national confessional rupture: Saad dies at mid-day March 6 at AUB Hospital; the Savoy Hotel attack in Tel Aviv saturates Lebanon with fear of Israeli reprisal; fedayeen fire on Lebanese planes thinking they are Israeli; Beirut is under general strike on March 7; the Higher Islamic Council has demanded the cabinet’s resignation; Israeli aircraft sonic-boom over Beirut during the funeral; the U.S. Embassy sends non-essential staff home at 1030 local.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT03028_03064 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

On the evening of March 5, 1975, the Solh government had survived a cabinet confidence vote. The Sidon crisis — though unresolved in substance — appeared to be stabilizing. One cabinet minister (Malik Salam) had resigned, Christian counter-demonstrations had boosted the regime’s confidence, and the Embassy believed a “typically Lebanese” solution of deferring decisions indefinitely was taking hold. Two events on March 5–6 destroyed that equilibrium within 24 hours. First, on the night of March 5, eight Fatah fighters landed on the Tel Aviv waterfront and attacked the Savoy Hotel, killing eleven Israelis. Second, at mid-day on March 6, Maarouf Saad died at AUB Hospital of the wounds he had received on February 26. The Embassy’s February 27 prediction — “If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions” — was validated precisely. PLO offices in Beirut were evacuated. Fedayeen in the Tripoli camps, believing Israeli retaliation was imminent, opened fire on overflying Lebanese Air Force planes. By mid-day March 7, Beirut was under a general strike. The Corniche Mazraa area was paralyzed by armed fedayeen from the nearby camps. Israeli aircraft were creating sonic booms over the city. The Higher Islamic Council had formally demanded the Solh cabinet’s resignation, naming the President, the Army leadership, and the Prime Minister by name. The Embassy sent its non-essential American staff home at 1030 local.

What this document reveals
  • Saad died at AUB Hospital at mid-day on March 6, 1975. The Embassy’s February 27 prediction that his death would produce manifestations of serious proportions was validated within hours. The cable logged “palpable atmosphere of public nervousness and foreboding” before the sun had set on the day of his death.
  • PLO offices in Beirut were evacuated within hours of Saad’s death. The response was not to the death itself but to the anticipated Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack of the previous night. The simultaneity of the two events — Fatah raid in Tel Aviv, Saad death in Beirut — created a political shockwave that no single mechanism could have produced.
  • Fedayeen fired on Lebanese Air Force planes north of Tripoli, mistaking them for Israeli. An inadvertent but consequential incident: Palestinian fighters were operating in a posture of expecting Israeli attack hours after Saad’s death. The confusion of nationality of aircraft is a miniature portrait of the breakdown in state-fedayeen coordination.
  • The Higher Islamic Council demanded the Solh cabinet resign on March 6, naming Frangieh, the Army leadership, and Solh himself. For the supreme body of the Sunni religious establishment to publicly call for the resignation of a Sunni Prime Minister was a confessional rupture. Solh had lost the constituency that might have sheltered him.
  • Beirut was under a general strike on March 7; Corniche Mazraa was in the hands of armed fedayeen. The road from the Embassy to the main hotel district was blocked. Israeli aircraft were sonic-booming overhead. Lebanese security authorities publicly urged all foreigners to stay indoors. The sovereignty collapse seen in Sidon on February 27 was now visible in Beirut.
  • The Embassy sent non-essential American staff home at 1030 local. The British Embassy did the same. In diplomatic practice this is a calibrated protective drawdown initiated only when streets are judged unsafe for routine circulation. The Embassy’s own security posture confirms the actual threat level — five weeks before Ain al-Rummaneh.

Key figures

Who was involved

M
Maarouf Saad
Sunni Nasserist ex-deputy — died March 6, 1975
Leader of the Popular Nasserist Organization, shot by Lebanese Army fire on February 26, 1975 during the fishermen’s demonstration in Sidon. Died at mid-day March 6 at AUB Hospital. His funeral on March 7 produced the countrywide general strike, the Palestinian-Sunni political alignment, and the confessional framing that the Embassy now describes as the dominant political mode. Historians identify his death as the precipitating event of the chain of incidents that produced the Lebanese Civil War on April 13.
R
Rashid Solh
Prime Minister of Lebanon — publicly named by Higher Islamic Council
Sunni Prime Minister, October 1974 to May 15, 1975. On March 6 the Higher Islamic Council publicly demanded his resignation, naming him personally alongside Frangieh and the Army leadership. For the Sunni religious establishment to withdraw its confidence from a sitting Sunni Prime Minister was unprecedented and politically terminal; his May 15 resignation was, in substance, written on March 6.

President of Lebanon — publicly blamed by Higher Islamic Council
Maronite Christian President. The Higher Islamic Council’s March 6 declaration named him first, blaming him for “the current state of affairs.” His refusal to consider army-command reform throughout the crisis was the specific grievance. His presidency would end in 1976 amid the civil war that began six weeks later.

Founder of the Progressive Socialist Party and leading figure of the Lebanese left. The Sunni-leftist-Palestinian mobilization of March 6–7 — the general strike, the funeral procession, the Corniche Mazraa barricades — was the coalition Jumblatt would formally lead within weeks as head of the Lebanese National Movement. The ‘ticklish position’ of late February had resolved itself: he was now on the other side.

The Record

The equilibrium of March 5

To understand what happened on March 6, 1975, it is necessary to understand what the Embassy believed was happening on March 5. Cable 03028’s opening paragraph describes a situation that had, after nine days of escalation, begun to stabilize.

The country-wide demonstrations in support of the Army and government on March 5 had gone off without serious violence. Christian political parties — principally the Kataeb and the NLP — had mobilized their street constituencies in defense of the state. The cabinet meeting that evening had produced the decision that the Solh government would continue in office. Malik Salam’s resignation had been absorbed; the first cabinet-level casualty of the crisis had not brought down the government. An agreement had been reached to refer Sunni-leftist demands for army-command reform to a cabinet study committee, to satisfy the Sidon fishermen economically, and to transfer the army’s southern district commander to “other posts” — a formula that addressed the Sunni complaint without conceding the structural issue of army command.

The Embassy’s characterization is precise: “thanks to a typically Lebanese solution in which no problems were really solved but were merely to be brushed under the rug for the time being.” The crisis had been managed, not resolved. The arrangement depended on nothing external disturbing it.

Two external disturbances arrived within fourteen hours.

The Savoy Hotel attack

On the night of March 5, 1975, eight Fatah fighters from the Black September Organization — operating under Fatah’s internal designation Force 17 — landed by rubber dinghy on the Tel Aviv seafront. They were led by Mohammed Yousef al-Najjar’s deputy and were briefed, according to Israeli reconstruction, to take as many civilian hostages as possible and exchange them for imprisoned Palestinian fighters.

They came ashore at the Savoy Hotel on HaYarkon Street, a beachfront tourist hotel in central Tel Aviv, and stormed the building. Eleven guests were taken hostage. Over the next ten hours, Israeli special forces from Sayeret Matkal assaulted the hotel; all but one of the attackers were killed, along with eight Israeli civilians and three Israeli soldiers. The attack was among the deadliest inside Israel in the history of the Palestinian armed struggle to that point.

The political problem the attack created for Lebanon was not the attack itself but the geography of its origins. The fighters had launched from Lebanese territory — standard practice by this point — and the probability that Israel would retaliate against Lebanon was approximately certain. The Lebanese state could calculate, as it had calculated after every Palestinian attack from Lebanese soil since 1968, that an Israeli reprisal would come; the question was scale and target. At a moment when the Sidon crisis had been contained but not resolved, an Israeli reprisal that required the Lebanese Army to respond would convert the containment into a regional war.

Saad’s death

At mid-day on March 6, 1975, Maarouf Saad died at American University Hospital in Beirut, eight days after he had been shot by Lebanese Army fire in the fishermen’s demonstration. The Embassy’s prediction of February 27 was validated.

The cable is precise in its wording: “News at mid-day March 6 of the death in AUB Hospital of ex-deputy Maarouf Saad (who had been wounded in Feb 26 clashes in Sidon).” The death is placed on a specific cable and a specific paragraph, connected to the Feb 26 cause by a direct reference. The cable does not need to editorialize: the connection between cause and effect is in the diplomatic record across the nine days.

The immediate operational response was swift. PLO offices in Beirut — the Fatah headquarters in Fakhani, the PFLP offices in Hamra, the DFLP offices, the Sa’iqa offices — were evacuated. Personnel and materials were moved from known political addresses into the refugee camps and distributed safe houses. This was the standard PLO response to an expected Israeli air or commando operation. Fedayeen went on full alert at the fifteen refugee camps. The country was posturing for an Israeli attack that it believed was coming within hours.

The cascade that the February 27 cable had predicted — mass demonstrations, general strike, paralysis of urban life — began within hours of the death being announced. Leftist and Sunni Muslim sympathizers, accompanied in many places by fedayeen, blocked roads, burned tires, fired weapons in the air. The barricades that had been dismantled in Sidon after the March 2 truce reappeared. In Beirut, the army’s internal security forces found themselves attempting to clear crowds from arteries they had never expected to be blocked — including the road to the airport.

Fire on the Lebanese planes

In the cascade of events on the morning of March 6, one small incident stands out as a miniature portrait of the crisis. Palestinian fighters at refugee camps north of Tripoli — the Beddawi and Nahr al-Bared camps, though the cable does not specify — opened fire on aircraft overflying their positions. The aircraft were in fact Lebanese Air Force, on routine operations. The fedayeen had assumed they were Israeli.

The incident reveals the posture of expectation. Palestinian fighters were operating, hours after Saad’s death and the news of the Savoy attack, in a state of readiness for immediate Israeli assault. The confusion of nationality — Lebanese aircraft assumed to be Israeli — is a symptom of the conditions under which people take cover first and identify later. It is also a signal of how far the operational coordination between the Lebanese state and the armed groups it nominally hosted had broken down. Lebanese Air Force aircraft could not fly over Lebanese territory without being mistaken for enemy aircraft by armed groups on that territory.

What the incident would have looked like if the fedayeen had been equipped with the SAM-7s reported in Sidon’s citadel is an exercise the reader may perform. No Lebanese pilot was hit; no Lebanese aircraft went down. But the underlying fact — that Palestinian fighters in Lebanese territory had the armament and the disposition to shoot down aircraft without identifying them — was one the Lebanese state could not ignore.

The Higher Islamic Council

On March 6, the Higher Islamic Council of the Lebanese Republic — the supreme body of the Sunni religious-political establishment, headed by the Grand Mufti — issued a public declaration demanding that the Solh cabinet resign. The declaration named three men as responsible for “the current state of affairs”: President Frangieh, the Army leadership, and Prime Minister Rashid Solh himself.

In Lebanese confessional politics, the Prime Minister’s office is reserved for a Sunni Muslim. Any Sunni Prime Minister governs with the consent — or at least the non-opposition — of the Sunni religious establishment. For the Higher Islamic Council to publicly demand the resignation of a sitting Sunni Prime Minister, and to name him by name, was an act of confessional abandonment. It meant that Solh’s own religious community had formally withdrawn its political confidence from him. No Sunni Prime Minister could govern without the Council behind him. The Council had moved.

Solh survived in office for another ten weeks. But his May 15 resignation was not a new event when it arrived; it was the formal acknowledgment of a political reality that had already taken shape on March 6. The Council’s declaration was the turning point at which the confessional framing of the Sidon crisis became institutional. From this point forward, the crisis was not about a fishing concession or an army operation; it was about the balance of confessional power in the Lebanese state.

March 7: a city under general strike

Cable 03064 is the Embassy’s late-morning report from March 7, filed from a city under general strike. “Right now Beirut is generally quiet as the result of a general strike,” the cable opens — but the sentence ends in its own qualification: “groups of men (some of them armed) can be observed at roadblocks scattered throughout the city.”

The strike was effective in the Sunni and mixed commercial districts. It was not observed in Christian commercial districts. The resulting geography was itself politically eloquent: the districts that closed and the districts that stayed open constituted, in real time, a map of the political coalition forming in response to Saad’s death. What the cable describes as “Beirut generally quiet” is a city in which half of commercial life has stopped and the other half has not, with the line between the two corresponding to the confessional fault lines of the country.

In the Corniche Mazraa area — adjacent to the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps and to the Fakhani PLO headquarters district — “armed fedayeen from nearby camps much in evidence.” This was not a discrete political demonstration; it was the practical deployment of fedayeen forces to hold a major Beirut artery against Lebanese state authority. The road to the airport was blocked by “milling demonstrators.” The road from the U.S. Embassy in Ain el-Mreisseh to the main hotel district in Ras Beirut was blocked. The Lebanese security authorities publicly urged all foreigners to stay off the streets — a measure that concedes, in the language of public-order announcements, that the authorities could not guarantee the safety of foreigners on the streets of the capital.

The Israeli sonic booms

The cable notes, in passing, that “several loud explosions (apparently sonic booms caused by overflying Israeli aircraft) were heard during the late morning hours.” In the spectrum of Israeli pressure signals, the sonic boom is a deliberate low-end demonstration: the aircraft are there, they are visible and audible, but they are not striking. The boom is political, not military.

The timing of the overflights was precise. They coincided with the morning of the Saad funeral and the general strike. They were Israeli signaling to the Lebanese government and to the Palestinian leadership simultaneously: Israel was watching, Israel had strike capability in the airspace, a decision could be taken at any moment. Together with the fedayeen firing on Lebanese planes the previous day in the mistaken belief that they were Israeli, the overflights produced a saturated airspace in which every party was calibrating to the same imminent possibility.

No Israeli attack on Lebanon came in those 24 hours. When Israeli retaliation for the Savoy Hotel attack finally materialized — in the form of Operation Marvad / the Mount Moussa raid on December 2, 1975 — it was nine months later, the civil war had been under way for six months, and the Lebanese state had already lost the capacity to respond that the Embassy feared would be forced on it. But on March 7, 1975, the possibility of immediate Israeli reprisal was part of the air over Beirut.

The Embassy closes

The final paragraph of cable 03064 is short and operationally specific. At 1030 local on March 7, the U.S. Embassy sent its non-essential American employees home with instructions to remain there for the remainder of the day. The British Embassy did the same. In diplomatic practice, a daytime protective drawdown of this kind is initiated only when the Chief of Mission judges the streets unsafe for routine Embassy circulation.

The measure was not an evacuation. It was not an emergency action. It was a calibrated protective response: keep the people we do not need out of the streets until the event (the Saad funeral and its aftermath) has passed. But the fact that the measure was judged necessary, and the fact that it was taken in coordination with the British, is the cleanest indicator in these cables of the Embassy’s actual real-time assessment of the threat level on the streets of Beirut on March 7, 1975.

The cable closes with the Embassy’s acknowledgment that the confessional turn had set in: “Events over the past nine days have tended increasingly to assume confessional overtones, with Sunni Muslims displaying growing and open dissatisfaction over what they regard as Christian monopolization of power within the GOL and the country at large.” And: “Christians, in return, have tended more and more to react hostilely to these pressures, attributing them to leftist and Palestinian ‘subversion’ and manipulation of the Sunni population.”

The framing was now in place. The civil war would begin five weeks and one day later, on April 13, 1975, when Phalange gunmen attacked a bus of Palestinian workers on their way home to the Tel al-Zaatar refugee camp in Ain al-Rummaneh. The fuse lit on February 26 in Sidon, confirmed by Saad’s death on March 6, institutionalized by the Higher Islamic Council’s declaration of the same day, and deployed on the streets of Beirut on March 7, had five weeks and one day left to burn.

Archival Note — Merged Entry

This is a merged archival entry combining two companion cables filed on consecutive days, March 6 and March 7, 1975, documenting the cascade that followed Maarouf Saad’s death. Cable 1975BEIRUT03028 (061547Z) is the same-day report: Saad has died at AUB Hospital at mid-day March 6; PLO offices in Beirut have been evacuated; fedayeen are on full alert; fedayeen fire on overflying Lebanese Air Force planes thinking they are Israeli; Beirut streets are blocked by leftist-Sunni demonstrations. Cable 1975BEIRUT03064 (071050Z) is the next morning’s report: general strike across Lebanon, Beirut paralyzed, Corniche Mazraa in the hands of armed fedayeen from nearby camps, Israeli sonic booms overhead, the Higher Islamic Council has demanded the Solh cabinet resign, and the Embassy has sent its non-essential staff home. The two cables together document the precise 24-hour period in which the crisis shifted from a South Lebanon incident to a nationwide confessional rupture.

Cable 1 of 2 — 1975BEIRUT03028 — 061547Z Mar 75

Subject: Renewal of Tension in Sidon and Elsewhere in Lebanon · CONFIDENTIAL · Ref: BEIRUT 2894 and previous

1.
Tensions generated throughout Lebanon over the past week by clashes between the Lebanese Army and “armed elements” in Sidon had begun to lessen perceptibly late on March 5, following country-wide manifestations by citizens[1] (mostly Christians but some Shi’a) demonstrating in support of the Army and GOL. Demonstrations went off more or less peacefully, with only one person reported killed (accidentally by stray bullet). The Council of Ministers meeting the evening of March 5 resulted in a decision by the Solh cabinet (backed by Pres Frangie) to continue in office, although Min of Water and Electricity Malik Salam carried out his threat to resign and walked out of the meeting. The stage thus seemed set for further diminution of tensions and a progressive return to normality on March 6, thanks to a typically Lebanese solution in which no problems were really solved but were merely to be brushed under the rug for the time being.[2]

2.
Unfortunately, widespread apprehension re possible Israeli retaliation against Lebanon for last night’s terrorist operation in Tel Aviv,[3] followed by news at mid-day March 6 of the death in AUB Hospital of ex-deputy Maarouf Saad[4] (who had been wounded in the Feb 26 clashes in Sidon (see Beirut 2611)), have changed all this. PLO offices in Beirut apparently have been evacuated, and fedayeen are reportedly on full alert in and around the country’s 15 refugee camps, including those north of Tripoli where trigger-happy elements this morning actually opened fire on overflying Lebanese Air Force planes, probably on the assumption they were Israeli.[5] As news of Saad’s death spread during the afternoon, other disruptive incidents began occurring at various points in Beirut, where groups of his leftist and Sunni Moslem sympathizers (accompanied in some places by fedayeen) have taken to blocking roads, burning tires, shooting in air, etc. Sidon reportedly is again gripped by tension, with large numbers of “armed elements” (i.e., fedayeen and their leftist allies) having reoccupied street barricades and positions on rooftops. Lebanese Army and internal security forces have cancelled all leaves and units are currently engaged in trying to clear crowds and barricades from several arteries in Beirut, including the road to the airport. Most shops and businesses closed earlier than usual, and late-afternoon rush-hour traffic appears less than normal.

3.
Conditions described above have created a palpable atmosphere of public nervousness and foreboding. The general feeling seems to be that further trouble — generated internally or by the Israelis, or both — is still to come. I have taken the precaution of advising the Embassy’s American staff to avoid unnecessary movement at least during the night of March 6-7.[6]

HOUGHTON

Cable 2 of 2 — 1975BEIRUT03064 — 071050Z Mar 75

Subject: Security Situation in Lebanon · CONFIDENTIAL · Ref: BEIRUT 3028

1.
As of mid-day March 7, the tense security situation in Beirut and other major urban areas remains much as outlined reftel. The funeral of leftist leader Maarouf Saad is scheduled to take place at noon in Sidon and will be accompanied by services at mosques in Beirut and other towns having large Sunni Moslem populations.[7] Large mourning processions and demonstrations staged by leftist and Sunni elements are expected to follow this afternoon in Beirut, Sidon and Tripoli. Although Lebanese security authorities hope these will occur without major disturbances, they are not optimistic.

2.
Right now Beirut is generally quiet as the result of a general strike,[8] but groups of men (some of them armed) can be observed at roadblocks scattered throughout the city, and scattered small-arms fire (probably in air) plus several loud explosions (apparently sonic booms caused by overflying Israeli aircraft)[9] were heard during the late morning hours. The Corniche Mazraa area is paralyzed, with armed fedayeen from nearby camps much in evidence, and several main roads — including that leading to the airport — in outlying areas are blocked by milling demonstrators. The road leading from the Embassy to the main hotel district is also blocked.[10] Lebanese security authorities have taken the unusual precaution of publicly urging all foreigners to stay off the streets.

3. Comment
Although we hope that the day will pass without further trouble, the possibility of ugly incidents and violence occurring is still with us. The situation is compounded by the fact that events over the past nine days have tended increasingly to assume confessional overtones,[11] with Sunni Muslims displaying growing and open dissatisfaction over what they regard as Christian monopolization of power within the GOL and the country at large. (The Higher Islamic Council on March 6 issued a strong demand for the Solh cabinet to resign, blaming the current state of affairs entirely on Pres Frangie, the Army leadership and Primin Solh.)[12] Christians, in return, have tended more and more to react hostilely to these pressures, attributing them to leftist and Palestinian “subversion” and manipulation of the Sunni population.[13] Although many leaders on both sides are still counseling moderation, there is no telling where events of the next 12-24 hours may lead. We expect to have a better picture of the situation when the Saad funeral and its aftermath are over.

4.
In view of the foregoing, I have sent non-essential employees home at 1030 local[14] and instructed them to remain there for the remainder of March 7. The British Embassy has done the same.

HOUGHTON

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The March 5 Christian demonstrations: In the days before Saad’s death, pro-Army demonstrations took place in Christian areas of Beirut and the mountain, organized principally by the Kataeb (Phalange) Party of Pierre Gemayel and the National Liberal Party of Camille Chamoun. The demonstrations were framed as support for the state and the rule of law against “armed elements.” In confessional terms, however, they represented the first mass public mobilization of Christian street forces in response to the Sunni-leftist agitation of the previous week. The Embassy’s careful phrase — “mostly Christians but some Shi’a’ — acknowledges the confessional character while noting the limited Shi’a participation (consistent with Musa Sadr’s cautious silence during the first phase of the crisis, which would end a week later with his open agitation against the GOL).
[2]The Embassy’s “brushed under the rug” diagnosis: The March 5 cabinet meeting had resolved nothing structural. The Solh government continued in office. General Ghanem was not dismissed. The army command was not reformed. Salam’s resignation was absorbed. The Sidon insurgency remained unresolved. But the immediate political crisis had been contained by the formula of continuing consultations, a ‘study commission’ on army reform, and a promise to satisfy the fishermen — a formula that the Embassy characterizes with characteristic dryness as the “typically Lebanese solution” in which nothing is decided but everyone can go home. The phrase is diagnostic of the entire political system: compromises that defer problems rather than resolve them, producing an accumulation of unresolved issues that would collapse the system in April 1975.
[3]The Tel Aviv Savoy Hotel attack (March 5, 1975): On the night of March 5, 1975, eight Fatah fighters from the Force 17 / Black September Organization landed by rubber dinghy on the Tel Aviv seafront, stormed the Savoy Hotel on HaYarkon Street, and took 11 hostages. In the 10-hour siege that followed, Israeli special forces (Sayeret Matkal) assaulted the hotel; seven attackers, eight hostages, and three Israeli soldiers were killed. The attack was planned and executed by the mainstream Fatah wing, not by a rejectionist faction — which complicates the picture of which faction wanted what. The operation came immediately after the Sidon ambushes and was presumed by the Embassy to be likely to produce an Israeli reprisal against Lebanon. As cable 1975BEIRUT03203 would document a week later, that Israeli reprisal — if it came while the Sidon crisis was unresolved — would force the Lebanese Army to respond or lose all public credibility, potentially tipping Lebanon into open conflict with Israel at a moment when it could not afford one.
[4]Maarouf Saad’s death, March 6, 1975: Eight days after he was shot by Lebanese Army fire during the fishermen’s demonstration of February 26, Maarouf Saad died of his wounds at American University Hospital in Beirut. The prediction the Embassy had filed on February 27 — “If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions” — was validated to the day. Historians including Kamal Salibi, Fawwaz Traboulsi, and Walid Khalidi identify Saad’s death as the specific political event that tipped a regional unrest into a national confessional crisis. The Ain al-Rummaneh bus attack of April 13, widely named as the start of the civil war, was five weeks and one day away.
[5]Fedayeen fire on Lebanese Air Force planes: One of the most politically charged incidents of the cable. Palestinian fighters at refugee camps north of Tripoli opened fire on aircraft overflying the camps, mistaking them for Israeli. The aircraft were in fact Lebanese Air Force. The incident is a miniature portrait of the crisis: the armed groups were in a posture of expecting immediate Israeli attack, the population inside the camps was operating with nerves stretched taut, and the confusion of who was shooting at whom — and on what assumption — was itself a symptom of the breakdown in coordination between the state and the armed groups it could not control. That fedayeen in Tripoli were prepared to open fire without confirming the target nationality is a signal of the level of tension; that they would assume Israel was already attacking, hours after Saad’s death and the Savoy attack, is a signal of what everyone expected to happen next.
[6]Houghton’s staff-safety advisory: The specific direction to Embassy American staff to avoid unnecessary movement was the first formal staff-safety measure of the crisis. The next cable (1975BEIRUT03064) would escalate this to sending non-essential employees home at 1030 local. The measured escalation of Embassy security posture is a useful indicator of the Embassy’s own threat assessment in real time. In diplomatic practice, sending non-essential staff home is a serious measure; coordination with the British Embassy to do the same indicates that the concern was being communicated across the allied diplomatic community.
[7]The Saad funeral, March 7, 1975: The funeral of Maarouf Saad in Sidon at noon on March 7 was the largest public assembly of the Sunni-leftist-Palestinian coalition since the crisis began. Secondary sources describe the funeral as producing the image of Sidon under Palestinian armed control in a specifically political form: PLO and Sa’iqa units marched in uniform in the procession, fedayeen flags flew alongside Lebanese flags, and speeches called for the replacement of General Ghanem, the dismissal of the Solh cabinet, and army reform. Simultaneous mosque services in Beirut, Tripoli, and other Sunni cities created a coordinated national moment in which the Sunni political and religious establishment publicly aligned with the fedayeen-leftist coalition. The synchronous mosque-and-funeral structure — using the religious infrastructure of the Sunni community as the coordinating mechanism for a political mobilization — was unprecedented in the scale and coordination of its execution.
[8]The general strike of March 7: A country-wide general strike declared by the Sunni-leftist political bloc, observed substantially across Sunni commercial districts, mixed districts, and — critically — by the Palestinian camps. Christian commercial districts largely did not observe the strike, which produced its own confessional geography in real time: the commercial sectors that did and did not close on March 7 became a map of the political coalition that was forming. The explicit observation that “Beirut is generally quiet as the result of a general strike” is a careful diplomatic construction: the city is quiet in the sense that commerce has stopped, but it is not peaceful, because the cessation of commerce is itself a political act.
[9]Israeli overflights, March 7: Sonic booms from overflying Israeli aircraft over Beirut on the morning of March 7 were the Israeli political signal of the day. The overflights were not attacks; they were a demonstration of Israeli air capability over Lebanese urban territory, timed to coincide with the Saad funeral and the general strike. In Israeli operational logic this was standard pre-reprisal signaling — establishing that Israel was watching, that Israeli strike capability was present, and that a decision could be taken at any moment. The Embassy’s notation that the booms were “apparently sonic booms caused by overflying Israeli aircraft” registers both the event and its political meaning. Together with the fedayeen shooting at Lebanese planes the previous day in the mistaken belief they were Israeli, the overflights produced a saturated airspace in which the question was not whether Israel would attack but when.
[10]The blocked road from the Embassy to the hotel district: The U.S. Embassy in Beirut in March 1975 was at the corner of Rue de la France and Avenue de Paris in Ain el-Mreisseh — the building that would be destroyed by a suicide truck bomb on April 18, 1983. The “main hotel district” refers to the Phoenicia-St. George-Holiday Inn cluster in Ras Beirut. The road between these two points — which the Embassy used for routine business — passed through an area now blocked by demonstrators. The practical consequence was that the Embassy’s American staff could not circulate in their routine business areas, and that Beirut’s commercial and hotel infrastructure had been paralyzed. The observation is small but important: the Embassy itself was now constrained by the crisis, not simply observing it.
[11]The confessional turn: The single most consequential political observation in cable 03064 is that “events over the past nine days have tended increasingly to assume confessional overtones.” The Sidon crisis had begun, on February 26, as an economic grievance about fishermen and a fishing concession. By March 7, the Embassy was describing it as a Sunni-Christian confrontation with the Sunni community in religious-political mobilization against a Christian-dominated state apparatus. This reframing is not the Embassy’s invention; it is a description of the way the political actors themselves were now framing the crisis. The movement from economic grievance to confessional war over nine days is the accelerated version of a decades-long drift in Lebanese political alignment. The confessional framing, once established, was very difficult to reverse.
[12]The Higher Islamic Council demand: The Higher Islamic Council’s March 6 demand that the Solh cabinet resign, explicitly blaming President Frangieh, the Army leadership, and Prime Minister Solh himself, was a specifically religious intervention in a political crisis. The Council is the supreme body of the Sunni religious establishment in Lebanon, headed by the Grand Mufti; its political pronouncements carry the weight of Sunni religious authority. For the Council to demand the resignation of a Sunni Prime Minister and to name him personally was unprecedented. It meant that Solh had lost the Sunni religious establishment — the final confessional constituency that might have sheltered him politically. His eventual May 15 resignation was, in essence, already written on March 6.
[13]The Christian counter-narrative: Parallel to the Sunni religious-political mobilization, Christian political leaders were constructing a counter-narrative in which the Sunni agitation was not a grass-roots response to a genuine confessional grievance but a product of “leftist and Palestinian subversion and manipulation of the Sunni population.” On this reading, the Sidon crisis was a Palestinian-engineered provocation (consistent with the Embassy’s own ‘ruse’ hypothesis of February 26, though deployed differently), and the Sunni response was evidence of how deeply the Sunni community had been penetrated by Palestinian-leftist agitation. The narrative did not need to be entirely accurate to be politically effective; it provided the Christian right with a framework in which the proper response was not conciliation but defense. The Kataeb and NLP militias would organize accordingly.
[14]The 1030 Embassy closure: The U.S. Embassy in Beirut sent its non-essential employees home at 10:30 local time on March 7, 1975, with instructions to remain at home for the remainder of the day. The British Embassy did the same. In diplomatic practice, a daytime staff drawdown of this kind is initiated only when the Chief of Mission judges the streets unsafe for routine Embassy circulation. The measure was calibrated: it was not an evacuation, not a closure, not an emergency action reporting requirement — it was a protective daytime drawdown pending the outcome of a specific event (the Saad funeral). The fact that the Embassy judged this measure necessary is a useful indicator of the actual threat level at street level in Beirut on the afternoon of March 7, 1975 — days before the April 13 bus attack would begin the civil war.
