Beirut, March 12–14, 1975

The Strategic Assessment of the Sidon Crisis

Ambassador Godley&rsquo;s SECRET/LIMDIS warning of March 12, 1975: the Lebanese Army is 8:1 outnumbered in Sidon, the commander of the fedayeen force is Abu Saleh (recently returned from East Berlin), the PLO is playing a &lsquo;double game&rsquo;, Soviet Ambassador Soldatov has been feeding Arafat &lsquo;scare stories&rsquo;, Syria may have encouraged the clashes, Musa Sadr has publicly joined the agitation, and an Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack would collapse the regime. The cable was forwarded same-day to Kissinger&rsquo;s Jerusalem shuttle.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT03203_03256 SECRET / LIMDIS / NOFORN → UNCLASSIFIED

Fourteen days after the Sidon clashes began, Ambassador G. McMurtrie Godley filed the Embassy&rsquo;s strategic assessment of the crisis in two companion SECRET/LIMDIS/NOFORN cables. The first (1975BEIRUT03203, filed the evening before the decisive Council of Ministers meeting) was the retrospective analysis: the crisis had dangerously aroused confessional feelings, the GOL had been further discredited in Muslim eyes, the Lebanese Army&rsquo;s ability to control the fedayeen had been sorely weakened. Brigadier Kanaan, Deputy Chief of Staff for Operations, had briefed the Embassy: on March 1, the Army had tried to reopen the coastal road with 100&ndash;150 troops and had been opposed by over 1,000 fedayeen firing machine-guns, RPGs, and recoilless rifles from fortified positions; the Army killed 25&ndash;30 fedayeen; the commander of the fedayeen force was Fatah&rsquo;s &ldquo;leftist&rdquo; military leader Abu Saleh, recently returned from exile in East Berlin. The Shia cleric Musa Sadr, whose silence during the first two weeks of the crisis had been conspicuous, had now publicly joined the Sunni-leftist agitation. The Embassy&rsquo;s operational warning was delivered in explicit terms: any Israeli retaliation for the March 5 Savoy Hotel attack in Tel Aviv would force the Lebanese Army to respond or lose all credibility with the Lebanese public. The cable was forwarded the same day by State Department cable 057899 to Secretary Kissinger&rsquo;s traveling party in Jerusalem, where Kissinger was conducting the shuttle negotiations on what would become the collapsed second Sinai interim agreement. The second Embassy cable (1975BEIRUT03256, filed 48 hours later) recorded the compromise outcome: the Council of Ministers had referred the demand for Army-command reform to a special cabinet committee for study, had tasked the Economy Minister with satisfying the fishermen, and had quietly transferred the Army&rsquo;s Southern District commander to &lsquo;other posts&rsquo;. The Solh cabinet survived. But Kanaan&rsquo;s further briefing recorded in the second cable delivered three additional revelations: (1) the PLO leadership was playing a &lsquo;double game&rsquo;, publicly negotiating while privately doing nothing to reduce the fedayeen occupation; (2) Soviet Ambassador Soldatov and other Soviet officials had filled Arafat full of &lsquo;scare stories&rsquo; about an impending Lebanese Army &lsquo;liquidation&rsquo; operation, inducing Arafat to welcome exactly the confessional turmoil that was now destabilizing Lebanon; (3) Sa&rsquo;iqa had participated in the Sidon operation despite Syrian government instructions to withdraw &mdash; and Kanaan suspected Syria had encouraged the clashes in order to foment wider disturbances. The cables close the Sidon crisis diplomatic record. The civil war would begin four weeks later.

The Record

The retrospective assessment

Cable 1975BEIRUT03203 is Ambassador Godley’s first full strategic assessment of the Sidon crisis, filed from Beirut on the evening of March 12, 1975, three hours before the Council of Ministers convened. The cable is classified SECRET, with the additional compartments LIMDIS (Limited Distribution) and NOFORN (Not Releasable to Foreign Nationals). It was marked “Dept pass Secretary’s Party”, a routing instruction that directed the State Department to forward the cable to Kissinger’s traveling shuttle in the Middle East. The Department carried out the instruction the same day via cable STATE 057899.

The cable opens with a summary paragraph whose plain-language judgments are worth reading twice. The Sidon clashes have “dangerously aroused confessional feelings in Lebanon.” The GOL has been “further discredited in the eyes of a substantial portion of the Moslem population.” The Lebanese Army’s ability to control the fedayeen, already limited, has been “reduced significantly.” And — the operational finding of the cable — any Israeli retaliation for the March 5 Savoy Hotel attack “would force the Lebanese Army to respond or lose all credibility with the Lebanese public.”

In the voice of an Ambassador filing at the SECRET/LIMDIS/NOFORN level, these are serious judgments. Godley is not forecasting civil war; he is describing the conditions under which one would be initiated. He is also, in the closing paragraph, making a direct appeal to Israeli leadership to restrain itself — an appeal that, through the Department’s forwarding to Kissinger, would be placed in front of the Secretary at the decision point.

Musa Sadr joins the opposition

One paragraph of the cable deserves particular attention: the parenthetical noting that “This anti-GOL agitation has lately been joined by Shi’ite Imam Musa Sadr, who had remained curiously silent throughout most of last week’s troubles.”

Imam Musa al-Sadr, the Iranian-Lebanese Shia cleric who had founded the Movement of the Deprived the previous year, was the principal political organizer of Lebanon’s Shia community. His silence during the first two weeks of the Sidon crisis had been conspicuous. The fishermen at Sidon were predominantly Sunni; the fedayeen leading the occupation were Palestinians; the political mobilization around Saad’s death was Sunni-leftist. The Shia community could have stayed out of the confrontation entirely, and for most of the first two weeks Sadr had positioned them to do so.

Sadr’s decision, around March 10–11, to publicly align with the Sunni-leftist agitation changed the shape of the coalition. The organized Shia community now formally entered the anti-Frangieh opposition. Four months later, Sadr would publicly name the Amal Movement (Afwaj al-Muqawama al-Lubnaniyya) as the armed wing of the Movement of the Deprived. The trajectory from March 12, 1975 to the armed Shia politics of the later civil war runs through this moment.

Kanaan’s numerical disclosure

Paragraph 5 of the cable delivers the most operationally significant piece of intelligence in the entire Sidon diplomatic record. Brigadier Kanaan, the Lebanese Army’s Deputy Chief of Staff for Operations, had briefed the U.S. Defense Attaché’s Office on the Army’s professional assessment of what had happened on the evening of March 1.

The figures were devastating. The Lebanese Army had committed 100 to 150 troops to the operation of reopening the main coastal road at Sidon. They had found themselves opposed by over 1,000 fedayeen, firing machine-guns, RPG rockets, and recoilless rifles from rooftop and other fortified positions in and around Sidon’s Old City. The numerical disadvantage was approximately 8:1, with the defender holding the terrain and the firepower. The Army killed 25 to 30 fedayeen during the fighting; Kanaan noted that the Army’s own losses would have been heavier had its troops not been using armored personnel carriers.

The figures answered a question the Embassy had left hanging through the first two weeks of the crisis: why had the Army not returned to Sidon? The answer was now documentary. The Army had been outnumbered 8:1 in an engagement it had initiated, on terrain of its adversary’s choosing, against a force firing from fortified positions with heavy infantry weapons. The return to Sidon in force would have required a substantially larger commitment of Army resources — and a political decision to accept the all-out confrontation with the fedayeen and their Lebanese sympathizers that such a commitment would have provoked. Kanaan’s later remark (in cable 03256) that the GOL and Army leadership “never entertained any intention of ‘smashing’ the fedayeen in Sidon” is the obverse of the numerical fact: the Army had neither the force structure nor the political authority for the operation the situation would have required.

Abu Saleh identified

The second revelation in paragraph 5 was the identity of the commander of the fedayeen force. Kanaan’s briefing identified the fedayeen operation in Sidon as “leftist” fedayeen elements — not only from the “rejectionist” groups but also elements from Sa’iqa and Fatah — who had taken advantage of the Sidon affair from the very start. Overall command, Kanaan told the Embassy, had been assumed by Fatah’s “leftist” military leader Abu Saleh, recently returned from exile in East Berlin.

The identification had three significant implications. First, the Sidon operation was not a spontaneous camp-militia response to the February 26 fishermen’s demonstration but a professionally-commanded Fatah leftist operation, executed under the direction of a senior Fatah commander. Second, the commander himself had direct East German intelligence connections; East Berlin in the 1970s hosted the Fatah left, provided Stasi security-service training to Palestinian commanders, and maintained direct political relations with the faction. Third, the fact that the command was held by Fatah’s leftist wing, rather than by the rejectionist factions (PFLP, DFLP), complicated any simple explanation of the operation as a rejectionist provocation against Arafat’s moderate leadership. Fatah’s own left wing, under a recognized Fatah commander, had executed the operation.

Abu Saleh (Nimr Saleh) would later break publicly with Arafat and lead Fatah al-Intifada against him in 1983; his leftist posture in March 1975 was an early marker of that eventual rupture. For the Embassy to name him in a SECRET/LIMDIS/NOFORN cable is the Lebanese Army’s fingerprinting of the operation, preserved in the diplomatic archive.

The warning to Israel

The closing paragraphs of cable 03203 deliver the operational warning. Paragraph 6: “Israeli retaliation against Lebanon in the near future for the Mar 5 terrorist raid on Tel Aviv would place the GOL in an impossible position vis-à-vis many of its own citizens, as well as the fedayeen, if it does not order the Army to respond.” Paragraph 7: “We hope Israeli leadership will think long and hard before undertaking action which could threaten the integrity of the present Lebanese regime and would almost certainly force the Lebanese Army (against its will but of necessity — i.e., to avoid being classed as traitors in Sunni, leftist and fedayeen eyes after ‘having killed brother Arabs’ in Sidon) to respond energetically to any Israeli action.”

This is not a routine diplomatic observation. The Ambassador is stating that the continued integrity of the Lebanese regime depends on Israeli restraint, and he is requesting that Israeli leadership understand this. In the diplomatic logic of the cable, the message will be conveyed to Israeli leadership through U.S. channels: the State Department will forward the cable to Kissinger, who is physically in Jerusalem conducting shuttle diplomacy, and the analysis will enter the active decision-making on the Israeli side.

The cable was forwarded that same day. State Department cable 057899 (signed by Acting Secretary Robert S. Ingersoll, Kissinger being in Israel) transmitted the verbatim text of BEIRUT 3203 to Secretary Kissinger’s traveling party via the U.S. Consulate in Jerusalem. The reference line “TOSEC 523” identifies the cable as number 523 in the series of cables sent to the Secretary during this particular travel. The cable’s distribution was limited to the NSC Executive Secretariat and to the S/S office (the Secretary’s immediate staff).

As an administrative matter, the warning reached Kissinger. As a policy matter, no U.S.-sanctioned Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack materialized in the immediate window. Kissinger’s shuttle collapsed on March 22 over Israeli refusal to accept partial withdrawal from the Mitla and Giddi passes, and the Secretary returned to Washington empty-handed. The eventual Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack — Operation Marvad, the Mount Moussa raid — came nine months later, on December 2, 1975. By that point, the Lebanese civil war had been under way for eight months; the Lebanese regime Godley had tried to protect had effectively ceased to function.

The compromise solution

Cable 1975BEIRUT03256, filed 48 hours after 03203, records the political outcome of the March 12 Council of Ministers meeting. The compromise solution foreseen in the first cable had been achieved. The Sunni-leftist demand for a Command Council reorganizing the Army’s command had been referred to a special cabinet committee for “study” — the traditional Lebanese parliamentary device for deferring a demand indefinitely while allowing tempers to cool. The Economy Minister had been charged with satisfying the fishermen. Frangieh and Solh had agreed to transfer the Army’s Southern District commander and his intelligence officer to “other posts” — a lateral reassignment that conceded the appearance of the Sunni-leftist demand without actually conceding its substance.

The Solh cabinet survived. Confessional tensions were expected to subside. The Sidon general strike would soon be called off. In the short term, the political logic of the Lebanese system had once again produced a compromise that held the cabinet together.

But Godley’s comment paragraph records the residual fact: the fedayeen were showing “some reluctance to allow that city to revert to full GOL control.” The compromise had resolved the cabinet crisis; it had not dislodged the fedayeen from Sidon. Sidon’s dual-sovereignty condition — formally under Lebanese state authority, operationally under fedayeen control — would become the new normal and would persist through the civil war. The Lebanese Army would not return to the city in force until after the Syrian intervention of 1976.

The double game

The most politically consequential allegation in cable 03256 is Kanaan’s charge that the PLO leadership had been playing a “double game.” Arafat and the Fatah central command had publicly deployed Abu Za’im (Atallah Atallah, a senior Fatah intelligence officer who had been arrested by the Lebanese Army on March 2 and subsequently released) and Abu Hassan (Ali Hassan Salameh, Arafat’s senior intelligence operative) as mediators to negotiate with Abu Saleh. The Army had intercepted Fatah radio communications urging Abu Saleh and his associates to cooperate with the GOL. On the face of the evidence, Fatah was cooperating.

But Kanaan’s judgment was that the radio communications were themselves part of the performance, that the PLO leadership was pretending to cooperate with the GOL while privately doing nothing to reduce the fedayeen occupation of Sidon. The strategic logic, Kanaan told the Embassy, was that Arafat — having been “filled full of scare stories re an impending Lebanese Army attempt to ‘liquidate’ the fedayeen presence by Soviet officials — including Amb Soldatov — in recent weeks” — was “anxious to see the Army’s ability to confront the fedayeen further circumscribed” by the Sidon clashes and the confessional turmoil that followed.

The named identification of Aleksandr A. Soldatov, the Soviet Ambassador to Lebanon, in a SECRET/LIMDIS/NOFORN U.S. Embassy cable, as an active manipulator of Arafat’s political calculations is a significant diplomatic marker. The charge is routed through Lebanese Army G-2 intelligence; the Embassy does not independently confirm it but passes it forward as the professional judgment of the Lebanese Army’s operations chief. The cable was forwarded the same day to Secretary Kissinger’s Jerusalem shuttle. Whatever it would do with the allegation, the U.S. policy-making apparatus had now registered Soviet political-intelligence activity as a specific factor in the chain of events that produced the Sidon crisis.

The murky Syrian role

The final analytical paragraph of cable 03256 engages directly with the question of Syrian intentions. The Embassy identifies contradictory evidence: Syria had counselled Sa’iqa to withdraw from Sidon; but Sa’iqa leaders Zohayr and Majid Muhsen had not obeyed; and Kanaan categorically insists Sa’iqa elements participated in the clashes and in the occupation. The March 7 funeral procession for Saad in Sidon had featured “PLO and Sa’iqa units” in highly visible roles — an operational fact that confirmed Sa’iqa participation regardless of Syrian diplomatic claims.

Kanaan’s hypothesis was that the SARG may have encouraged the Sidon clashes in order to foment wider disturbances in Lebanon. The Syrian strategic calculation would have involved several elements: weakening the Frangieh regime, which was perceived in Damascus as aligned with Saudi Arabia and the West; creating political leverage for eventual Syrian intervention in Lebanon; disciplining the PLO by producing a crisis the PLO could not control; and blocking any Lebanese movement toward a bilateral initiative with Israel. In hindsight, the Syrian intervention in Lebanon would begin in April 1976, under cover of the civil war. The Sidon crisis of March 1975 may be read as an early marker of Syrian willingness to accept Lebanese destabilization as a strategic good.

The close of the record

Cables 03203 and 03256 together close the Sidon crisis diplomatic record. The sequence from 02611 (February 26) through 03256 (March 14) documents the crisis continuously over sixteen days. The final cable’s quiet closing sentence — “the Lebanese Army’s ability to correct this situation has been significantly diminished by the events of the past two weeks” — is the formal record of a strategic fact.

What the Embassy had been watching was the transition of Lebanon from a state that nominally controlled its territory into a state whose control over key urban areas had been transferred to armed groups it could not dislodge. Sidon became the model. Over the next four weeks — from March 14 to April 13, 1975 — the same process would begin to play out in Beirut. The Sidon crisis was not the first warning; it was the last one the Lebanese state received before the Ain al-Rummaneh bus attack began the civil war.

The forwarding of cable BEIRUT 3203 to Kissinger’s shuttle via STATE 057899 establishes the administrative record that the warning reached the highest level of U.S. policy-making at the active decision window. The forwarding did not produce an intervention. Neither the United States nor Israel did anything in the March window that would have changed the trajectory the cables had described. Four weeks after Godley filed the warning, the civil war began.

Archival Note — Merged Entry

This is a merged archival entry combining the two companion SECRET/LIMDIS strategic-assessment cables that close the Sidon crisis diplomatic record, plus the State Department cable that forwarded the first of them to Secretary Kissinger during his Jerusalem shuttle. Cable 1975BEIRUT03203 (121845Z Mar 75) is Ambassador Godley’s retrospective assessment filed on the eve of the March 12 cabinet meeting, documenting for the first time Brigadier Kanaan’s intelligence briefing on the scale of the fedayeen deployment in Sidon (over 1,000 fighters versus 100–150 Army troops) and the identity of its commander (Abu Saleh, recently returned from East Berlin exile). Cable 1975BEIRUT03256 (140715Z Mar 75) is Godley’s follow-up two days later: the compromise has been achieved, Solh’s cabinet survives, and Kanaan has provided further revelations about the PLO’s “double game,” Soviet Ambassador Soldatov’s alleged role in feeding Arafat “scare stories,” and Sa’iqa’s participation despite Syrian denials. Cable 1975STATE057899 (14 Mar 75 2047Z, signed INGERSOLL) is the Department’s forwarding cable transmitting the full text of BEIRUT 3203 to Secretary Kissinger’s traveling party in Jerusalem — establishing as an administrative matter that Godley’s warning about Israeli retaliation was placed in front of the Secretary at the decision point regarding the Savoy Hotel reprisal.

Cable 1 of 3 — 1975BEIRUT03203 — 121845Z Mar 75

Subject: Recent Clashes in Sidon and Their Political Repercussions · SECRET / LIMDIS / NOFORN · Dept pass Secretary’s Party

Summary.
Political repercussions resulting from recent clashes between the Lebanese Army and “armed elements” (mostly fedayeen) in Sidon have dangerously aroused confessional feelings in Lebanon. While wiser heads among the country’s traditional political leaders are working hard to compose feelings among extremist elements in Moslem and Christian communities, the GOL has become further discredited in the eyes of a substantial portion of the Moslem population,[1] and the Lebanese Army’s ability to control the fedayeen — much less to act with the confidence and approval of most Lebanese — has been reduced significantly. In our view, any Israeli retaliation in the near future for the Mar 5 terrorist raid on Tel Aviv in light of the recent Army/civilian clashes would force the Lebanese Army to respond or lose all credibility with the Lebanese public.[2] End summary.

1.
The security situation in Beirut continues to improve in most visible respects since last weekend. Demonstrations and other street disturbances have largely ceased and the city has more or less resumed normal activity. In Sidon, however, the general strike enforced by leftist and fedayeen elements continues to prevail, and the town — while outwardly quiet — remains entirely under control of “armed elements” (mostly fedayeen) who seized key positions in and around Sidon Feb 26.[3]

2.
The local political scene however remains tense and unsettled, as Lebanon’s Sunni Moslem community, including virtually all its political leaders, continues to agitate strongly against the GOL and the Lebanese Army over what Sunnis insist was the latter’s unwarranted intervention in the troubles in Sidon. (This anti-GOL agitation has lately been joined by Shi’ite Imam Musa Sadr, who had remained curiously silent throughout most of last week’s troubles.)[4] Sunnis are also holding the GOL responsible for the death of Maarouf Saad (whom the Army believes was NOT repeat NOT killed by Army bullets)[5] and are decrying what they regard as generally anti-Moslem attitudes displayed at the highest levels of the Frangie regime. Sunni and leftist leaders have joined in demanding inter alia (A) that Army leadership be controlled by a “Command Council” that would ostensibly give non-Christians greater voice in determining military policies, (B) that the concession granted by the GOL to Chamoun’s “Protein” fishing company be revoked,[6] and (C) that the Army commander in Sidon (who is a Moslem) be replaced. Primin Solh has lost heavily in the crisis and would probably be forced to resign were it not that most responsible political leaders do not want a cabinet crisis under present conditions. Primin Solh, in response to above pressures, has evidently decided to adopt many Sunni-leftist demands as his own and to press for their fulfillment as the price of his continuance in office.[7]

3.
With the Council of Ministers meeting scheduled for this evening (Mar 12) and confessional feeling still running high, there has been considerable speculation that a cabinet split (along Christian-Moslem lines) over the above demands would cause the Solh govt to fall, leading to prolonged aggravation of the crisis and a Moslem “boycott” (i.e., by passive and perhaps not-so-passive resistance) of the Frangie regime altogether. Fortunately, however, moderate leaders on both sides have remained in close touch with one another throughout the crisis, attempting to resolve matters by traditional Lebanese means of compromise and conciliation and to restrain their more extreme associates and followers from exacerbating tensions further. Over the past 24 hours, these efforts seem to have borne fruit. The prospect at this writing is that Christian cabinet ministers and Pres Frangie will agree to accept Sunni-leftist demands for “study” by a special ministerial commission[8] and that Primin Solh, together with his Jumblattist and Sunni colleagues in the cabinet, will hold on to their portfolios at least for the time being.

4. Comment.
The confused and emotion-ridden situation described above represents probably one of the more serious surges of confessional feeling in recent years.[9] A contributing factor, of course, can be found in widespread uncertainty and uneasiness experienced by Lebanese and Palestinians alike over the future course of the ME settlement effort and what it spells for them. Another source of current instability, however, doubtless stems from growing Moslem disgruntlement over the Frangie regime’s increasing insensitivity to Moslem interests and aspirations in the conduct of governmental affairs. Events of the past two weeks in Sidon and Beirut have given leftist (especially communist and Baathist) elements an unusually golden opportunity to play on latent Sunni feelings in this regard, supplementing their ongoing efforts to derive advantage from general popular discontent stemming from the GOL’s abysmal neglect of pressing economic and social problems.[10] Given the present highly-charged state of Sunni feelings and the ugly Christian backlash which Sunni agitation has evoked, they appear to have made the most of this opportunity.

5.
Even more mischievous role has been played by the fedayeen. Fedayeen participation in the Sidon clashes and in the widespread anti-GOL demonstrations that followed certainly equalled, and probably surpassed, the leftist role in polarizing confessional feeling over the past week. A reliable Lebanese Army source (Deputy COS for Operations Brig Kanaan)[11] confirms our initial suspicion (Beirut 2611) that “leftist” fedayeen elements (not only from “rejectionist” groups but also elements from Sa’iqa and Fatah as well) at least took advantage of the Sidon affair from the very start, although he claims that other fedayeen units — and not just “militia” from nearby camps — quickly joined in, with overall command being assumed by Fatah’s “leftist” military leader Abu Saleh (recently returned from exile in East Berlin).[12] The result was that the Lebanese Army, when it attempted with only 100-150 troops to force open the main coastal road at Sidon on the evening of Mar 1 (Beirut 2837), found itself opposed by over 1,000 fedayeen firing machine-guns, RPG rockets and recoilless rifles from rooftops and other fortified positions in and around Sidon’s Old City.[13] (Kanaan says that the Army killed some 25-30 fedayeen during this fighting and that the Army’s own losses would have been heavier had its troops not been using armored personnel carriers).

6.
Given deep concern among responsible members of the Moslem and Christian communities that the crisis might degenerate into serious confessional strife, the chances are that the government will contain the crisis and the Solh cabinet will remain in office for the time being. The Army, however, has unfortunately become the subject of confessional controversy, has been hurt by the crisis, and its ability (already limited) to control the fedayeen sorely weakened.[14] Many Lebanese of both faiths, probably correctly, consider the crisis might have been avoided but for the entry of the Army into Sidon. Some, unfairly, blame the Army for action ordered by the civilian GOL leadership. Given these special circumstances, Israeli retaliation against Lebanon in the near future for the Mar 5 terrorist raid on Tel Aviv would place the GOL in an impossible position vis-à-vis many of its own citizens, as well as the fedayeen, if it does not order the Army to respond.

7.
Although we can well understand and sympathize with the depth of Israeli feeling against the fedayeen which has resulted from the Mar 5 raid and may be prompting the GOI to reply in kind, we hope Israeli leadership will think long and hard before undertaking action which could threaten the integrity of the present Lebanese regime and would almost certainly force the Lebanese Army (against its will but of necessity — i.e., to avoid being classed as traitors in Sunni, leftist and fedayeen eyes after “having killed brother Arabs” in Sidon) to respond energetically to any Israeli action.[15]

GODLEY

Cable 2 of 3 — 1975BEIRUT03256 — 140715Z Mar 75

Subject: Security Situation in Lebanon · SECRET / LIMDIS / NOFORN · Ref: BEIRUT 3203

Summary.
The compromise solution foreseen reftel between the GOL and Sunni-leftist leaders who have been agitating fiercely over recent events in Sidon was agreed upon in the Council of Ministers meeting Mar 12. We expect this will help confessional animosities to subside and enable the Solh cabinet to continue in office, at least for the time being. End summary.

1.
As foreseen reftel, the Lebanese Council of Ministers Mar 12 agreed to refer the Sunni-leftist demands for reorganization of the Army command to a special cabinet committee for study.[16] At the same time, the cabinet charged Economy Min Abbas Khalaf with satisfying the demands of Sidon’s fishermen, and Pres Frangie and Primin Solh reportedly have decided to transfer the commander of the Army’s Southern District and his intelligence officer to “other posts”.[17] As a result of these steps, the Solh cabinet will continue in office, confessional tensions will probably be eased somewhat, and there is speculation that the general strike still in progress in Sidon will soon be called off by the “armed elements” who control that city.

2.
Re the situation in Sidon, further details provided by the Lebanese Army (Brig Kanaan) on the role played by the fedayeen during the past two weeks are particularly revealing. Kanaan states that although PLO’s Abu Za’im and Abu Hassan — acting on orders from ’Arafat and Fatah’s Abu Jihad — attempted to assist GOL officials in “negotiating” with Abu Saleh and other “armed elements” in Sidon, their efforts have not been particularly successful.[18] (Kanaan tends to believe, despite the fact that the Army picked up Fatah radio communications urging Abu Saleh and his associates to cooperate with GOL, that PLO leadership has been playing a double game — i.e., pretending to cooperate with GOL while privately doing little or nothing to reduce fedayeen occupation of Sidon.[19] He thinks that ’Arafat, having been filled full of scare stories re an impending Lebanese Army attempt to “liquidate” the fedayeen presence by Soviet officials — including Amb Soldatov — in recent weeks, was anxious to see the Army’s ability to confront the fedayeen further circumscribed by the Sidon clashes and the confessional/political turmoil that he expected would ensue from them.)[20]

3.
According to Kanaan, the GOL and Army leadership never entertained any intention of “smashing” the fedayeen in Sidon, since they knew full well that such a move would provoke an all-out confrontation with the fedayeen and their Lebanese sympathizers throughout the country. Nevertheless, the latter were quick to portray the Army’s Mar 1 action as an initial GOL step to provoke just such a confrontation, thereby successfully polarizing and inflaming Sunni-Christian passions further — so successfully, in fact, that many Sunnis in Sidon have been openly referring to the fedayeen as “our Army”, while Christian groups have been demonstrating in support of “our (Lebanese) Army”.[21]

4.
One murky aspect in all the foregoing is the role played by Syria.[22] We note reports in other channels that SARG had counselled Sa’iqa forces in Lebanon to withdraw from the Sidon area and avoid trouble with the Lebanese Army. We note other reports in the same channels, however, that Sa’iqa leaders Zohayr and Majid Muhsen were not overly careful to comply with these instructions,[23] and Kanaan categorically insists that Sa’iqa elements took part in the clashes in and occupation of Sidon. (Both PLO and Sa’iqa units played a highly visible role in the Mar 7 funeral procession for Maarouf Saad in Sidon.) Kanaan suspects, but cannot prove or figure out precisely why, the SARG may also have encouraged the Sidon clashes in order to foment wider disturbances in Lebanon.[24]

5. Comment.
Although some degree of normal life may return to Sidon as Lebanese confessional and political tensions subside, the fedayeen from reports appear to be showing some reluctance to allow that city to revert to full GOL control.[25] As noted reftel, the Lebanese Army’s ability to correct this situation has been significantly diminished by the events of the past two weeks.

Editorial note — forwarding cable

Cable STATE 057899, signed by Acting Secretary Robert S. Ingersoll, is an administrative repeat: it transmits the full verbatim text of BEIRUT 3203 — Godley’s strategic assessment with the warning about Israeli retaliation — to Secretary Kissinger’s traveling party via the U.S. Consulate in Jerusalem. The reference line “TOSEC 523” is the administrative indicator that the cable is being delivered to the Secretary’s party (sequence number 523 in the series of cables sent to the Secretary during his travels). Kissinger was in Israel March 12–14 conducting shuttle diplomacy on what would become the collapsed second Sinai interim agreement; his shuttle collapsed on March 22 over Israeli refusal to accept partial withdrawal from the Mitla and Giddi passes. The forwarding establishes as an administrative matter that Godley’s Sidon assessment — including its operative conclusion that an Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack would collapse the Lebanese regime — was placed before the Secretary at the active decision point regarding the Savoy Hotel reprisal. No Israeli reprisal materialized in the March window; the eventual Israeli response (Operation Marvad / Mount Moussa raid) came on December 2, 1975, by which point the Lebanese civil war had been under way for eight months and the concerns Godley had raised had largely been overtaken by events.[26]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The Embassy’s judgment of “further discredited”: By March 12, Ambassador Godley had concluded that the Sidon crisis had produced a measurable decline in the Frangieh regime’s political standing within the Sunni community. The word “further” is diagnostic: the regime had not started from a strong baseline. The Higher Islamic Council’s March 6 demand for the cabinet’s resignation (cable 1975BEIRUT03064) had formalized the Sunni religious establishment’s withdrawal of confidence from Prime Minister Solh. Six days later, Godley is stating the political fact: the Sunni community’s political alienation from the Frangieh government had become structural.
[2]The core warning of cable 03203: The operational finding of the cable is that any Israeli retaliation against Lebanon in the near future would force the Lebanese Army to respond. Godley’s logic: if the Army did not respond to an Israeli reprisal, it would be branded as traitors in Sunni, leftist, and fedayeen eyes after “having killed brother Arabs” in Sidon. The Army, already confessionally polarized by Sidon, could not afford further politically-fatal inaction. An Israeli reprisal would therefore drag Lebanon into direct conflict with Israel at a moment when the state lacked the political consensus to sustain such a conflict. The cable was forwarded to Kissinger’s shuttle in Jerusalem the same day by State cable 057899, establishing that the warning reached the Secretary at the active decision window on Israeli reprisal for the Savoy Hotel attack.
[3]Sidon remained under fedayeen control March 12: Two weeks after February 26, Sidon was still occupied by the armed elements that had taken position on February 26. The Lebanese Army had tried to re-enter on March 1, had been ambushed (cable 1975BEIRUT02837), and had not returned. The March 2 truce had given the armed groups effective possession of the city. The general strike continued. The town was quiet only in the sense that no fighting was in progress; it was not under state authority. This is the starting fact of the cable: a Lebanese provincial capital had been outside state control for two weeks, and the state had not yet found a way to reassert itself.
[4]Musa Sadr joins the agitation: Imam Musa al-Sadr — founder of the Movement of the Deprived (1974), Iranian-born Shia cleric, and the principal political organizer of Lebanon’s Shia community — had been conspicuously silent during the first two weeks of the Sidon crisis. The Embassy registers his silence in the parenthetical “curiously silent”. His public alignment with the Sunni-leftist agitation around March 10–11 marked the entry of the organized Shia community into the anti-Frangieh opposition. The political significance is considerable: the Shia had been broadly absent from the Sunni-Palestinian alignment until Sadr moved them. The Amal Movement would be publicly named four months later, in July 1975. In the chronology of Lebanese Shia political mobilization, Sadr’s March 1975 entry into the Sidon agitation is a step along the path from the Movement of the Deprived to Amal to the armed Shia political power of the later civil war.
[5]The Army’s denial that it killed Saad: The parenthetical “whom the Army believes was NOT repeat NOT killed by Army bullets” is the Lebanese Army’s formal counter-narrative to what had by March 12 become a national Sunni political charge. The Army’s position was that Saad was shot by a fedayeen bullet, possibly deliberately, in order to produce exactly the political escalation that had followed. The position had political utility but had not persuaded Sunni opinion; the Sunni community continued to treat Saad’s death as an Army killing. The “repeat NOT” emphasis in the cable is diplomatic craft: repeating “NOT” in all-caps is how cables signaled that the reader should not misread a negative under the visual confusion of all-caps transmission.
[6]The Protein Company SAL concession: The commercial enterprise headed by Camille Chamoun — former President, patriarch of the National Liberal Party, pillar of the Maronite right — that had received an exclusive Mediterranean fishing concession. The concession threatened the livelihoods of approximately 1,000 small-boat fishermen at Sidon and Tripoli. The fishermen’s February 26 demonstration at which Saad was shot was a protest against this concession. For the revocation of the Protein concession to be listed as a Sunni-leftist demand at the Council of Ministers on March 12 was the closing of a circle: the economic grievance that had sparked the crisis was now a formal political demand.
[7]Solh adopts the Sunni-leftist demands: Prime Minister Rashid Solh’s political calculation was that his political survival now depended on adopting the Sunni-leftist agenda as his own. His Sunni base had been mobilized against him by the Higher Islamic Council; his only path to political viability ran through becoming the internal GOL champion of the demands that had been formulated against his government. Solh survived in office for another ten weeks on this basis, but the adoption deepened the cabinet fracture rather than closing it. His May 15 resignation came when the strategy ran out of political road.
[8]Refer to “study” by special ministerial commission: The Lebanese parliamentary tradition of deferring a demand by referring it to a study commission was a well-established technique. The practical effect was to remove the demand from the immediate political agenda, allow tempers to cool, and — if possible — let the demand die in committee. The Embassy understood exactly what was happening and said so in the March 6 cable: “a typically Lebanese solution in which no problems were really solved but were merely to be brushed under the rug.” The Command Council demand — if pursued — would have required constitutional amendment and a reopening of the National Pact; referring it to study was the political equivalent of killing it without saying so.
[9]The Embassy’s comparative judgment: “Probably one of the more serious surges of confessional feeling in recent years” is an explicit comparative statement. Godley is comparing Sidon-March-1975 to the previous benchmarks of confessional crisis in Lebanon — the 1958 civil war, the May 1973 Army-PLO clashes, and the confessional tensions of 1969 that produced the Cairo Agreement. To say that Sidon had produced confessional feelings comparable to these earlier crises was to say that a threshold had been crossed. The benchmark for civil war was closer than the Ambassador was prepared to state outright in a cable that would be distributed across the Washington bureaucracy.
[10]The economic-and-social-problems paragraph: Godley’s Paragraph 4 comment on the “abysmal neglect of pressing economic and social problems” is the Embassy’s acknowledgment that the confessional crisis was not purely a product of fedayeen opportunism or communist agitation but had a real structural base. Lebanese economic stagnation, the government’s neglect of South Lebanon, the concentration of wealth in the Maronite commercial elite, the absence of rural development investment — these were the conditions that allowed the leftist parties to mobilize Sunni popular feeling. The cable is unusual in State Department reporting from this period for the candor of its judgment that the GOL’s structural neglect of social and economic problems was a direct contributor to the confessional polarization.
[11]Brigadier Kanaan as source: Deputy Chief of Staff for Operations of the Lebanese Armed Forces, Brigadier Kanaan is the Lebanese Army’s operational intelligence chief. His briefing to the U.S. Defense Attaché’s Office (DAO) is the single most important documentary source in the Sidon diplomatic record. He is the source for: (a) the figure of over 1,000 fedayeen in Sidon, (b) the identification of Abu Saleh as the commander, (c) the Army’s estimate of 25–30 fedayeen killed, (d) the allegation that Arafat was fed ‘scare stories’ by Soviet Ambassador Soldatov, and (e) the insistence that Sa’iqa participated despite Syrian denials. Kanaan’s briefings would continue through the civil war; his professional judgments appear repeatedly in Embassy cables as “reliable Lebanese Army source”.
[12]Abu Saleh (Nimr Saleh): A senior Fatah military commander of the leftist/Fatah-Uprising tendency within the PLO. Founding Fatah member. The identification of Abu Saleh as the commander of the Sidon fedayeen operation is the Lebanese Army’s professional judgment that Sidon was not a spontaneous camp-militia response to the fishermen’s demonstration but a professionally-commanded Fatah leftist operation, executed under the direction of a senior commander. His East Berlin exile (late 1974 / early 1975) is itself diagnostic: East Germany in the 1970s hosted the Fatah left, provided security-service training to Palestinian commanders, and maintained direct political relations with the faction. Abu Saleh would later break publicly with Arafat and lead Fatah al-Intifada against him in 1983; his leftist posture in Sidon in March 1975 was an early marker of that eventual rupture. He died in 2013.
[13]100-150 Army vs over 1,000 fedayeen: Kanaan’s numerical disclosure is the most important operational fact in the cable. The Lebanese Army had attempted, on the evening of March 1, to reopen the main coastal road at Sidon with 100 to 150 troops. They met over 1,000 fedayeen firing machine guns, RPGs, and recoilless rifles from fortified rooftop positions in and around the Old City. This is an 8:1 numerical disadvantage, with the defender holding the terrain and the firepower. The Army’s decision to commit only 100–150 troops suggests it had not accurately assessed the opposing strength before the engagement; the defeat of the engagement produced exactly the political-military embarrassment the cable is now describing. The use of APCs was decisive: without armored protection, Army losses “would have been heavier”, in Kanaan’s phrasing.
[14]The Army sorely weakened: The Embassy’s judgment that the Army’s ability to control the fedayeen — already limited — had been sorely weakened by the Sidon crisis is the operative conclusion about the state’s capacity to enforce sovereignty within its borders. From this point onward, the Lebanese Army faced a deteriorating political condition: every confessional-political crisis would make action against armed groups harder, because Army action would immediately reopen the confessional wound of Sidon. The Ain al-Rummaneh bus attack on April 13, five weeks later, would find the Army in precisely this paralysis: incapable of imposing state authority on either the Phalange militia or the PLO, because moving against either would be framed as taking the other’s side in the confessional war.
[15]The closing appeal to Israeli leadership: Paragraph 7 of the cable is an explicit appeal to Israeli decision-makers to refrain from near-term retaliation. Godley states directly that he understands and sympathizes with Israeli feeling after the Savoy attack and acknowledges that Israel “may be prompted to reply in kind”. He then requests that Israeli leadership “think long and hard” before acting. In Embassy Beirut’s voice, this is an implicit request that the State Department transmit this concern to Israeli channels — which State did, same day, via cable 057899 to Kissinger in Jerusalem. The eventual Israeli reprisal (Operation Marvad on December 2, 1975) came nine months later, after the civil war had already destroyed the political structure Godley was trying to protect.
[16]Referral of Command Council to study committee: The March 12 Council of Ministers decision was the specific political compromise that allowed the Solh cabinet to survive. The Sunni-leftist demand for a Command Council reorganizing the Army’s Maronite-monopolized command structure was referred to a special cabinet study committee. In Lebanese parliamentary practice, this was a deferral rather than a concession; but it was a deferral that did not require the Christian cabinet ministers to publicly oppose the demand, and it did not require Frangieh to oppose it either. The compromise held the cabinet together. It did not close the issue; the Command Council question would return in 1976.
[17]Transfer of Southern District commander and intelligence officer: The specific operational concession. The Army commander of the Southern District — who had ordered the March 1 intervention at Sidon, and who was himself a Muslim — was to be transferred to “other posts”, along with his intelligence officer. This was the tactical concession to the Sunni-leftist demand for his replacement. In Lebanese political practice, such a transfer was not a demotion but a lateral reassignment that allowed both sides to claim outcomes: the Sunni-leftist coalition could claim that they had secured the commander’s removal, while the Army command could claim that the officer had merely been rotated in the normal course of assignments.
[18]Abu Za’im and Abu Hassan as Arafat’s emissaries: The intra-PLO politics of the negotiation are revealing. Abu Za’im (Atallah Atallah, a senior Fatah intelligence officer) had been arrested by the Lebanese Army on March 2 (cable 1975BEIRUT02894) during the Sidon clashes; his subsequent deployment by Arafat as negotiator-to-Abu-Saleh three days later is one of the sharper illustrations of the PLO-state relationship in the period. Abu Hassan is Ali Hassan Salameh, Arafat’s senior intelligence operative and Western contact man, who would be assassinated by Mossad in Beirut in January 1979. Their operation was to be conducted under the direction of ‘Arafat and Abu Jihad (Khalil al-Wazir); the combined authority is the Fatah political-military command in Beirut. That the negotiations “had not been particularly successful” is the Embassy’s polite formula for the assessment that the Fatah leadership had either lacked the ability to control Abu Saleh’s leftist faction or had not really tried.
[19]Kanaan’s “double game” charge: The most politically consequential allegation in the cable. Kanaan’s judgment is that the PLO leadership — Arafat and the Fatah central command — was publicly pretending to cooperate with the GOL while privately doing nothing to reduce the fedayeen occupation of Sidon. The Army had intercepted Fatah radio communications urging cooperation; the radio communications themselves were part of the double game, giving Arafat a paper trail of apparent cooperation while the underlying operational posture remained unchanged. The allegation is serious because it fingers the political strategy of the PLO leadership: to use the Sidon crisis as a lever to weaken the Lebanese state’s capacity to confront the fedayeen, while maintaining a surface posture of cooperation that protected the PLO from direct GOL retaliation.
[20]Soviet Ambassador Soldatov’s role in Arafat’s calculations: Kanaan’s charge — that Soldatov and other Soviet officials had been feeding Arafat scare stories about an impending Lebanese Army liquidation operation — is the Lebanese Army’s reading of the political mechanism by which Arafat had been induced into his pre-Sidon posture. The charge is routed through Lebanese Army G-2 intelligence; the Embassy does not independently confirm it but passes it forward as the professional judgment of the Army’s operations chief. Soldatov’s name appearing by name, signed by a U.S. Ambassador, in a SECRET/LIMDIS/NOFORN cable forwarded to Kissinger’s shuttle, is a significant diplomatic marker: the U.S. is now registering Soviet political-intelligence activity as a specific factor in the chain of events that produced the Sidon crisis.
[21]“Our Army” — fedayeen or Lebanese: The confessional fracture made literal. The cable records that by mid-March 1975 Sunni residents of Sidon were openly referring to the fedayeen as “our army”, while Christian groups in Beirut and the mountain were demonstrating in support of “our (Lebanese) army”. The phrase “our army” was now being claimed by two different populations to mean two different armed formations. The state monopoly on armed force, already fictive, had been verbally surrendered in Sidon. This is the phrase the Embassy chooses to preserve in the cable because it captures the diagnostic condition of the pre-civil-war political order: two armies, each publicly claimed by a different confessional community, operating in the same country.
[22]The murky Syrian role: Cable 03256 is unusual in Embassy reporting of this period for its direct engagement with the question of Syrian intentions. The Embassy identifies contradictory evidence: Syria had counselled Sa’iqa to withdraw from Sidon; but Sa’iqa leaders Zohayr and Majid Muhsen had not obeyed; and Kanaan categorically insists Sa’iqa elements participated. The Embassy’s comment that Kanaan “suspects, but cannot prove or figure out precisely why, the SARG may also have encouraged the Sidon clashes in order to foment wider disturbances in Lebanon” is the diplomatic register of the Lebanese Army’s suspicion that Syria was playing a double game of its own — publicly restraining Sa’iqa while privately encouraging the operation as a way to destabilize a Lebanese government whose Maronite Christian orientation ran counter to Syrian strategic interests.
[23]Zuhayr Muhsen: Leader of the Sa’iqa Palestinian faction, directly controlled by the Syrian Ba’th Party. Muhsen was famously quoted in 1977 (to the Dutch newspaper Trouw) stating that there was no Palestinian people, only Jordanians and Syrians — a statement reflecting his pan-Arabist and Syrian-nationalist political position. He was assassinated in Cannes in 1979. His brother Majid Muhsen held the second-rank command in Sa’iqa. Their non-compliance with Syrian government counsel to withdraw from Sidon suggests either that they were operating with a different set of orders from Syrian intelligence, or that Sa’iqa’s operational discipline in this period was looser than the SARG publicly claimed.
[24]Syrian strategic calculation: The Embassy’s hypothesis, via Kanaan, is that the SARG may have had an interest in fomenting wider disturbances in Lebanon. The Syrian strategic reasons would have been several: to weaken the Frangieh regime, which was perceived as aligned with Saudi Arabia and the West; to create political leverage for eventual Syrian intervention in Lebanon; to discipline the PLO by producing a crisis the PLO could not control; and, more diffusely, to produce a Lebanese instability that would block Lebanese movement toward any bilateral peace initiative with Israel. In hindsight, the Syrian intervention in Lebanon would begin in April 1976 under cover of the civil war; the Sidon crisis of March 1975 may be read as an early marker of Syrian willingness to accept Lebanese destabilization as a strategic good.
[25]Fedayeen reluctance to cede Sidon back to the state: The closing comment of cable 03256 is Godley’s judgment that the fedayeen were showing “some reluctance to allow that city to revert to full GOL control”. What the Embassy was observing was the beginning of the dual-sovereignty condition that would characterize Sidon for the rest of the pre-war period and through much of the civil war. The Lebanese state was formally sovereign; the fedayeen held operational control of key urban positions. The fishermen’s grievance had been resolved and the general strike would be called off, but the fedayeen occupation of the Old City had simply become the new normal. The Lebanese Army would not return to the city in force until after the Syrian intervention of 1976.
[26]Cable STATE 057899 — the forwarding to Kissinger’s shuttle: The State Department cable of March 14, 1975 transmitted the verbatim text of BEIRUT 3203 to Secretary Kissinger’s traveling party, routed through the U.S. Consulate in Jerusalem. The reference “TOSEC 523” identifies the cable as number 523 in the series sent to the Secretary during this particular travel. The cable was drafted by NEA/ARN’s P.K. Bullen and approved by Bullen himself; distribution was limited to NSC/Executive Secretariat and S/S (the Secretary’s immediate staff). The cable was signed by Robert S. Ingersoll, Acting Secretary during Kissinger’s absence. The administrative significance: Godley’s warning that Israeli retaliation for the Savoy attack would collapse the Lebanese regime was placed before the Secretary at the active decision point for U.S. policy on Israeli reprisal. Kissinger’s shuttle negotiations collapsed on March 22, 1975; no U.S.-sanctioned Israeli reprisal for the Savoy attack occurred in the immediate window. The eventual Israeli response (Operation Marvad / Mount Moussa raid, December 2, 1975) came nine months later, by which point the Lebanese civil war had been under way for eight months.
