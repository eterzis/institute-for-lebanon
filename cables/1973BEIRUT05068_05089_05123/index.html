Beirut, May 3 &ndash; May 4, 1973

The Two-Day War: Hour by Hour, May 3&ndash;4, 1973

Three consecutive Situation Reports in a 17-hour window document Lebanon&rsquo;s first major Army&ndash;fedayeen confrontation &mdash; the dress rehearsal for 1975. Syria denies its own cross-border intervention, then quietly undoes it. Sadat phones Frangieh. Mahmoud Riad flies in. A ceasefire holds. And the U.S. Embassy writes, that night, that even if Arafat signs a new agreement, his ability to sustain it is open to doubt.

Based on cable 1973BEIRUT05068_05089_05123 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

From 10:20 PM on May 3 to approximately 5:55 PM on May 4, 1973, the U.S. Embassy filed three Situation Reports (SITREP 6, 8, and 9) documenting the hour-by-hour arc of what became known as the Two-Day War &mdash; Lebanon&rsquo;s first large-scale Army&ndash;fedayeen engagement since the 1969 Cairo Agreement. Approximately 1,000 fedayeen had crossed into Lebanon from Syria the previous afternoon; the Syrian government denied any crossing had occurred; 24 hours later the same Syrian government quietly promised to withdraw its Yarmouk Brigade under cover of darkness. Prime Minister Amin al-Hafez submitted and then withheld his resignation in a maneuver the Embassy called "a bit of Lebanese flim-flam." President Sadat phoned Frangieh directly and promised to pressure Assad. Arab League Secretary-General Mahmoud Riad arrived to mediate. A ceasefire held. But the Embassy&rsquo;s judgment that night &mdash; that even a new Arafat agreement would be unsustainable &mdash; was the structural diagnosis that would be vindicated by the Melkart Protocol&rsquo;s failure, the 1975 Sidon Crisis, and ultimately the civil war itself.

What this document reveals
  • Syria deployed a named military formation across the Lebanese border and then flatly denied it. The Yarmouk Brigade — a Syrian-controlled Palestine Liberation Army unit — crossed into Lebanon on May 3. On May 3 Syria denied any crossing. On May 4 the Syrian Prime Minister reportedly promised his Lebanese counterpart to withdraw the unit under cover of darkness. The two positions are documented 14 hours apart.
  • Sadat phoned Frangieh and promised to pressure Assad. Egypt’s president intervened directly on Lebanon’s behalf against Syria — five months before the October War, four years before Sadat’s Jerusalem trip, and in an Egypt–Syria relationship that would rupture over the subsequent four years. The Embassy documented the call the night it happened.
  • The Embassy characterized the Prime Minister’s resignation maneuver, on the record, as "a bit of Lebanese flim-flam." Amin al-Hafez announced he had submitted his resignation but couldn’t deliver it because the President was asleep. Within hours, after Sadat’s phone call, the resignation was "temporarily withdrawn." The diplomat’s editorial comment is preserved verbatim.
  • Lebanese civilians were killed by indiscriminate fedayeen fire on May 3 — a distinction the cable draws explicitly from combat casualties. The Embassy notes that Frangieh and the Army are "angered." This is the exact mechanism by which PLO armed presence alienated successive Lebanese administrations over the pre-war decade.
  • The Embassy’s structural diagnosis, at 1:15 AM on May 4, was that Arafat couldn’t sustain a ceasefire even if he signed one. That diagnosis — written in the exhaustion of a night of fighting — was vindicated by the Melkart Protocol’s failure, the 1975 Sidon Crisis, and the civil war. The judgment is on the record as a contemporary reading, not a retrospective one.
  • Mixed Army–fedayeen patrols were already circulating by 1400Z on May 4 — anticipating the "mixed commission" architectural device of the Melkart Protocol two weeks later. The ground-level de-escalation mechanism preceded the formal settlement that tried to codify it.
  • Sidon appears in this cable series, already in 1973, as a politically mobilizable zone in support of the fedayeen. A "half-hearted street rally" was attempted there on May 4. Sidon would become the epicenter of the February–March 1975 crisis that immediately preceded the civil war. The 1973 rally is the same political ecosystem at an earlier stage.
Chronology

What happened, in sequence

~1630Z, May 3
Yarmouk Brigade crosses
Approximately 1,000 fedayeen of the Syrian-controlled Yarmouk Brigade cross from Syria into the Lower Bekaa&ndash;Arkub zone of Lebanon. Lebanese press reports the figure at 4,000.
May 3 evening
Sadat phones Frangieh
Egyptian President Sadat telephones Lebanese President Frangieh directly, promising to weigh in with Hafez al-Assad of Syria.
~2130Z, May 3
Army briefs Embassy
Lebanese Army (Kanaan) informs the Embassy that fighting in the Lower Bekaa&ndash;Arkub zone has diminished considerably. No further crossings reported.
Post-midnight May 4
PM Hafez&rsquo;s contingent resignation
Prime Minister Amin al-Hafez announces he has submitted his resignation but has not been able to deliver it to President Frangieh, who was already asleep. The Embassy terms this "a bit of Lebanese flim-flam."
~0120Z, May 4
SITREP 6 filed
Embassy files SITREP 6 (cable 05068) summarizing the evening: fighting diminished, Sadat intervention, PM&rsquo;s contingent resignation, Embassy judgment that Arafat cannot sustain any ceasefire he signs. Signed HOUGHTON.
~1000Z, May 4
Ceasefire holding
Calm in Beirut area. No incidents. Observers "impressed and surprised by the effectiveness of the ceasefire." Army morale reported very high. BBC reports of heavy Arkub fighting judged "without foundation."
May 4 morning
SARG promises Yarmouk withdrawal
Syrian Prime Minister Ayyubi reportedly commits to Lebanese PM Hafez to withdraw remaining Yarmouk Brigade personnel under cover of darkness &mdash; a change from Syria&rsquo;s previous position denying any crossing occurred.
~1114Z, May 4
SITREP 8 filed
SITREP 8 (cable 05089) filed under NIACT IMMEDIATE. Documents Syria&rsquo;s shift from denial to tacit admission, Mahmoud Riad en route, King Hassan emissary arriving, PM Hafez&rsquo;s resignation temporarily withdrawn.
Afternoon May 4
Mahmoud Riad arrives
Arab League Secretary-General Mahmoud Riad arrives in Beirut to mediate. Special emissaries from Morocco and Iraq also arrive.
Afternoon May 4
Sidon rally attempt
A "half-hearted attempt to organize a street rally in Sidon" is reported &mdash; early evidence of Sidon as a politically mobilizable zone in support of the fedayeen.
~1400Z, May 4
Mixed patrols circulating
Mixed Army&ndash;fedayeen patrols circulating. Fedayeen leaders asking people to return arms to a central location. The Presidency reports most of the Syrian-crossed fedayeen have withdrawn with their heavy weapons.
~1455Z, May 4
SITREP 9 filed
SITREP 9 (cable 05123) confirms stabilization. Archival copy corrupts beyond the political-situation passage; remainder of the cable is not recoverable from the WikiLeaks archive.

The Record

What these three cables document, hour by hour

On the night of May 2–3, 1973, a ceasefire stemming from an earlier agreement broke down. On the afternoon of May 3, approximately one thousand fedayeen of the Syrian-controlled Yarmouk Brigade crossed the border from Syria into the Lower Bekaa–Arkub zone of Lebanon. Press reporting placed the number at four thousand. Fighting continued into the evening between Lebanese Army units and the fedayeen. On May 3 the Syrian government denied that any fedayeen had crossed the border at all.

Fourteen hours later, on the morning of May 4, the Syrian Prime Minister reportedly communicated to his Lebanese counterpart that the remaining Yarmouk Brigade personnel would be withdrawn that night under cover of darkness. The cables document this shift — from categorical denial to tacit admission — in the natural language of Embassy sources reporting as the crisis unfolded.

The diplomatic machinery that produced the ceasefire

Between the two Syrian positions, at approximately 1:15 AM Beirut time on May 4, the Embassy filed SITREP 6. The cable documents that Egyptian President Sadat had telephoned Lebanese President Frangieh directly and promised to pressure Assad. The Embassy records this as the “major favorable development” that had improved the Lebanese political situation that evening. Arab League Secretary-General Mahmoud Riad was en route. Moroccan and Iraqi emissaries were also inbound.

At home, Lebanese Prime Minister Amin al-Hafez had announced that he had submitted his resignation — but had not delivered it, because President Frangieh was asleep. The Embassy’s bracketed comment, preserved in the cable: “a bit of Lebanese flim-flam.” By morning, after the Sadat call had taken effect, the resignation was “temporarily withdrawn.” The contingent resignation — maintained as leverage — had served its political purpose.

The embassy’s structural diagnosis

SITREP 6 closes with what may be the most consequential sentence in the three-cable series: “Even should Arafat reach a new agreement with the GOL, his ability to sustain it is open to doubt.”

This is not a retrospective assessment. It is a contemporary judgment, filed in the exhaustion of the night, by diplomats who had spent the day in briefings with the Army, the Presidency, and the Foreign Ministry. Within two weeks, Arafat would reach exactly such an agreement — the Melkart Protocol of May 17, 1973, documented in cables 1973BEIRUT05755 and 05776. Within months, cross-border raids would resume. Within two years, the Sidon Crisis of February–March 1975 (cables 02611_02682 through 03203_03256) would document the same pattern. Within another few weeks, the April 1975 events in Ain el-Rummaneh would trigger the civil war.

The Embassy’s diagnosis of May 3–4, 1973 was vindicated four times over before the decade was out.

The half-hearted rally in Sidon

Among the smaller observations in SITREP 8, one stands out in historical hindsight: a “half-hearted attempt to organize a street rally in Sidon” in support of the fedayeen. Sidon — the southern coastal city whose local politics favored the Palestinian armed presence — is already visible in this 1973 cable as a politically mobilizable zone. Sidon would become, twenty-two months later, the epicenter of the February 26 – March 14, 1975 crisis that immediately preceded the civil war. The 1973 rally was the same political ecosystem at an earlier stage.

What SITREP 9 does — and doesn’t — record

SITREP 9, filed at 2:55 PM GMT on May 4, confirms that mixed Army–fedayeen patrols are circulating. It notes that fedayeen leaders have asked people to return arms to a central location. It documents that the Presidency sees no indication Prime Minister Hafez intends to submit his resignation. Beyond this point, the WikiLeaks archival copy of the cable is corrupted — text from an unrelated cable on a World Health Organization housing-allowance matter in Geneva appears interposed. That portion has been excluded from the reproduction above; the SITREP’s recoverable content ends with the political stabilization passage.

The dress rehearsal

These three cables document what historians now call the Two-Day War — a name the cables themselves never use. Read in sequence, they record the first full-scale collision between the Lebanese state and the Palestinian armed presence on Lebanese territory since the 1969 Cairo Agreement. The Embassy reported it as a crisis that stabilized within a day. It also reported, in the same breath, that the stabilization would not hold. Two weeks later, the Melkart Protocol would formalize a settlement. Two years later, Sidon. Four years later, the camps’ war. Fifteen years later, the Taif Accord.

All of it starts, in the documentary record, at 10:20 PM on May 3, 1973.

Archival Note — Merged Entry This entry presents three consecutive Situation Reports on the May 1973 crisis — known later as the Two-Day War between the Lebanese Army and the fedayeen — filed in the 17-hour window from 10:20 PM Beirut time on May 3, 1973 to approximately 5:55 PM Beirut time on May 4, 1973. SITREP 6 (05068), SITREP 8 (05089), and SITREP 9 (05123) have been merged because they are a contiguous narrative sequence from a single correspondent (Houghton, under the NIACT IMMEDIATE protocol for SITREP 8) documenting the transition from active combat to ceasefire stabilization. SITREP 7 is not in the WikiLeaks archive as of this writing. Each cable below is reproduced verbatim with its own banner. The final paragraph of SITREP 9 in the WikiLeaks archive is corrupted by intrusion of an unrelated cable; this has been marked as an archival transmission error and the extraneous text excluded. See footnote [21].

SITREP 6 — Cable 1973BEIRUT05068 — 10:20 PM Beirut, May 3

Originally classified CONFIDENTIAL · Filed under Night Action Immediate protocol · Now UNCLASSIFIED

1.
The Lebanese Army contact Kanaan[1] informed the Embassy at 2130Z that fighting in the Lower Bekaa–Arkub region between the Army and fedayeen has diminished considerably. No additional crossings from Syria are reported, although fedayeen who crossed earlier (about 1630Z) are still in place and believed to number perhaps up to 1,000.[2] (Note: press reporting places the figure at 4,000.) The threat of heavy fighting remains, especially since the Lebanese Army does not have much strength in that area. The GOL (Dib[3] of the Presidency) is less optimistic than before. A further danger is that, should Syria and the fedayeen leadership adopt a clearly hostile attitude, trouble could break out in the South and North, which have thus far remained calm. There are no indications this will occur, but at this time no one can predict. The situation in the Beirut camps is reported calm, although there has been some crowd movement reported in Moslem areas (with no violence).

2.
Both the Foreign Minister (Abou Hamad[4]) and Tony Frangieh[5] have been actively working on the Syrian government, but both report results discouraging. The Syrians even deny that any fedayeen have crossed into Lebanon.[6] Abou Hamad feels the Egyptian attitude, on the other hand, is still favorable. Sadat has spoken by phone to Frangieh and promised to weigh in with Hafez al-Assad of Syria.[7] Abou Hamad fears Syrian influence on Arafat’s position regarding any agreement with the GOL.

3.
The Prime Minister announced after midnight local time on May 4 that he had submitted his resignation but had not been able to submit it to Frangieh, who was already asleep (Comment: a bit of Lebanese flim-flam[8]). At the same time, there have been some encouraging developments, and if they continue it is possible he will reconsider his position later on May 4. We are told that the major favorable development was Sadat’s conversation with Frangieh. However, Tony Frangieh and FonMin Abou Hamad say that the odds favor Hafez’s resignation, with a military government being designated by Frangieh. Efforts are still alive at reaching a new agreement with the fedayeen, and should this take place Hafez could reconsider. Considerable political maneuvering is obviously going on and the situation is fuzzy. However, even should Arafat reach a new agreement with the GOL, his ability to sustain it is open to doubt.[9] He was unable to sustain the ceasefire stemming from the May 2 agreement, and casualties on May 3 were heavy, including Lebanese civilians killed by indiscriminate fedayeen shooting and rocket-firing which has angered Frangieh and the Army.[10]

4.
It is now 1:15 AM. We are retiring to take a fresh reading in the morning.

HOUGHTON[11]

SITREP 8 — Cable 1973BEIRUT05089 — 11:14 AM GMT, May 4

Originally classified CONFIDENTIAL · Filed under NIACT IMMEDIATE · Now UNCLASSIFIED

1.
Reports from the Foreign Office (Haddad[12]), the Army (Kanaan and G-2), and the Presidency (Dib) continue to be encouraging as of 1000Z May 4. Calm remains in the Beirut area with no reported incidents. However, there are reports of occasional fedayeen checkpoints near the refugee camps. We are assured that reports (by BBC among others) of heavy fighting in Arkub are without foundation, although there was a brief exchange of mortar fire at one spot early in the day. No contact thus far May 4 with the heavily armed elements who came from Syria (and may have returned there.)[13] The situation in South Lebanon is reported quiet except for a half-hearted attempt to organize a street rally in Sidon.[14] North Lebanon is also quiet. Observers are impressed and surprised by the effectiveness of the ceasefire to this hour. Morale of the Army is also reported very high.

2.
FonMin has informed us through Haddad that the SARG (reportedly PM Ayyubi[15] to PM Hafez) has promised to withdraw the remaining personnel of the Yarmouk Brigade[16] under cover of darkness May 4. It is hard to say what value to place on the promise, but it is a change from the May 3 position of denying any elements crossed the border. The attitude of the Egyptian is still considered positive. Mahmoud Riad arrives afternoon May 4.[17] A special emissary from King Hassan[18] is also arriving, along with an Iraqi envoy — the former considered to be helpful, the latter less so. In any event, the GOL (Dib, Tony Frangieh, and Abou Hamad) is encouraged by the present state of play with other Arab governments.

3.
Concerning the internal political situation, FonMin (via Haddad) and Dib say that the attitude of Prime Minister Hafez is much more positive than the evening of May 3, and that his resignation threat has been at least temporarily withdrawn. Very active political bargaining is expected for the rest of the day and probably for several days ahead. President Frangieh reportedly remains quite firm in his insistence that any new agreement reached with the fedayeen not accord them the sort of freedom they had under the old Cairo Accords.[19]

4.
UNRWA[20] officials report they have had no access to any refugee camps here since fighting began May 2, and that access is still restricted both by the Army and fedayeen. The agency has no precise estimate of the casualty rate among residents of the camps around Beirut, but considers it to be high. Camps elsewhere in Lebanon are described as quiet.

HOUGHTON

SITREP 9 — Cable 1973BEIRUT05123 — 2:55 PM GMT, May 4

Originally classified CONFIDENTIAL · Filed IMMEDIATE precedence · Now UNCLASSIFIED · Archival copy partially corrupted — see [21]

1.
Situation remains calm in Beirut and elsewhere in country at 1400Z. No incidents reported in Beirut or elsewhere since the last SITREP. The Presidency (Dib) tells us that most fedayeen who came in from Syria the night of May 3 have withdrawn together with their heavy armament, and the small number who remain will follow. The Army (G-2) tells us all is calm, nothing has budged throughout May 3. Mixed Army–fedayeen patrols are circulating[22] and fedayeen leaders have asked people to return arms to a central location. The Army is being very careful of possible recurrence of isolated incidents which could spread.

2.
Dib says that the political situation is also stabilizing, with no indication that Prime Minister Hafez intends to submit his resignation.[21]

Editorial note: the WikiLeaks archival copy of BEIRUT 05123 becomes corrupted at this point, with text from an unrelated cable on a World Health Organization housing-allowance matter interposed. That extraneous content has been excluded above. The recoverable portion of SITREP 9 ends with the political stabilization passage. See footnote [21].

Editorial Footnotes
[1]Kanaan: the Lebanese Army contact who appears repeatedly across the Embassy's 1973 and 1975 reporting as the Embassy's most consistent military interlocutor. He reappears in cables 1973BEIRUT05155 and 1973BEIRUT05159 during the May 7–8 outbreak, and again in 1975BEIRUT03203 during the Sidon Crisis, where he gives the Embassy the specific 8:1 numerical disclosure (100–150 Army versus 1,000+ fedayeen in southern Lebanon). His institutional position and Arabic rendering of his name are not fully documented in declassified sources; he is identified in the cables simply as "Army (Kanaan)."
[2]Lower Bekaa–Arkub region: the eastern and southeastern border strip of Lebanon adjoining Syria and Israel. The Arkub — known in Western press as "Fatahland" — was the primary fedayeen operational zone from which raids into northern Israel were launched. The fedayeen crossing from Syria into this zone on May 3 constitutes a Syrian-enabled reinforcement of Palestinian armed capacity inside Lebanon at the moment the Lebanese Army was attempting to restore sovereignty. The 1,000–4,000 figure range between Embassy and press reporting illustrates the fog of the moment.
[3]Dib: the Presidency contact at Baabda Palace cited across multiple Embassy cables — 1973BEIRUT05155, 1973BEIRUT05159, and the Melkart Protocol series (1973BEIRUT05755/05776). His name appears as a routine channel for Presidency views. The cable record identifies him only as "(Dib of Presidency)"; his full name and title are not established in declassified sources.
[4]Foreign Minister Abou Hamad (أبو حمدAbu Hamad): Lebanese Foreign Minister in 1973 under President Frangieh. He is the direct conduit to Washington on negotiations with both the Syrian government and the PLO in early May 1973. His assessment that "Syrian influence on Arafat" is the controlling variable — rather than Arafat’s own agency — is a recurrent Embassy finding across the 1973 crisis.
[5]Tony Frangieh: son of President Suleiman Frangieh, serving as an informal Presidential channel to the Embassy. The same Tony Frangieh appears in 1973BEIRUT05159 relaying the Army’s ceasefire to the Embassy on the morning of May 8. He was assassinated on June 13, 1978, by Lebanese Forces militiamen under Bashir Gemayel’s command — the Ehden massacre. His appearance here, five years earlier, is among the earliest documented instances of his role as a Presidency-Embassy intermediary.
[6]Syrian denial that any fedayeen crossed into Lebanon: the May 3 Syrian position is flatly contradicted by events on the ground and by the subsequent (May 4) Syrian admission that the Yarmouk Brigade would be withdrawn under cover of darkness. The evolution from categorical denial to tacit admission within 24 hours is documented in this cable series and constitutes direct evidence of Syrian disinformation at the moment of the crisis.
[7]Sadat’s phone call to Frangieh: Egyptian President Anwar Sadat telephoned the Lebanese President directly on May 3, promising to "weigh in with Hafez al-Assad" — i.e., to pressure Damascus. This is one of the earliest documented instances of Egypt intervening against Syria on Lebanon’s behalf, and it reflects the tension in the Sadat–Assad relationship that would widen later in 1973 (around the October War) and definitively break in 1977 (Sadat’s Jerusalem trip). The Embassy’s frontline documentation predates this rupture.
[8]"A bit of Lebanese flim-flam": the Embassy’s verbatim editorial assessment of Prime Minister Amin al-Hafez’s declaration that he had submitted his resignation but not yet delivered it because President Frangieh was already asleep. The phrase captures a diplomat’s practical judgment — Hafez was reserving the option to withdraw the resignation if circumstances improved, which indeed occurred within hours after the Sadat call. The device of a contingent, unreceived resignation is not unique to Lebanese politics, but the Embassy’s willingness to characterize it explicitly is an instance of unusually candid on-record reporting.
[9]Arafat’s inability to sustain ceasefires: the Embassy’s judgment — at 1:15 AM Beirut time on May 4, 1973 — that "even should Arafat reach a new agreement with the GOL, his ability to sustain it is open to doubt" proves prescient. The Melkart Protocol of May 17, 1973 (documented in cables 1973BEIRUT05755/05776) would indeed prove unsustainable; cross-border raids would resume within months; and the 1975 Sidon Crisis would document the same pattern of agreements made and broken. This paragraph is therefore a contemporary record of the structural problem that would drive the civil war.
[10]Lebanese civilians killed by fedayeen shooting and rocket-firing: the cable documents, in unequivocal terms, fedayeen casualty-causing actions against Lebanese civilians on May 3, 1973 — distinct from combat casualties in engagement with the Army. The record of fedayeen fire harming Lebanese civilians in Lebanese territory, with the Embassy noting Frangieh and the Army are "angered," documents the mechanism by which the PLO’s armed presence alienated successive Lebanese constituencies and administrations across the pre-war decade.
[11]HOUGHTON: Dwight J. Houghton, senior Embassy officer (Deputy Chief of Mission / Chargé d’Affaires) filing the SITREP series under the Night Action Immediate protocol. The same signatory appears on 1973BEIRUT04974 (April 30, 1973), the SITREPs of May 3–4 reproduced here, 1973BEIRUT05155 (May 7), 1973BEIRUT05159 (May 8), and the Melkart Protocol cables 1973BEIRUT05755 and 05776 (May 18). His consistent presence across the May 1973 crisis makes him the Embassy’s principal reporter on the period.
[12]Haddad: a Lebanese Foreign Office official serving as the FonMin’s channel to the Embassy during the crisis. The identification is by surname only; not conclusively tied to any specific individual in declassified sources.
[13]"No contact thus far May 4 with the heavily armed elements who came from Syria (and may have returned there)": this carefully hedged formulation documents the Embassy’s tentative acceptance of the Syrian government’s partial admission. Within 14 hours of the previous SITREP, where Syria flatly denied any cross-border infiltration, the Embassy is now tentatively documenting that the infiltrated forces may have withdrawn.
[14]"Half-hearted attempt to organize a street rally in Sidon": noteworthy in historical context — Sidon appears here, in May 1973, as already a politically-mobilizable zone in support of the fedayeen. The same Sidon would become the epicenter of the February–March 1975 fedayeen-instigated crisis (cables 1975BEIRUT02611_02682 through 1975BEIRUT03203_03256) that immediately preceded the civil war. The 1973 rally is the same political ecosystem in early form.
[15]Mahmoud al-Ayyubi: Syrian Prime Minister under Hafez al-Assad (1972–1976). The cable relays that he reportedly committed to Lebanese PM Amin al-Hafez — presumably by telephone or via Syrian diplomatic channels — to withdraw the Yarmouk Brigade personnel under cover of darkness on May 4. The undertaking at PM-to-PM level indicates the gravity of the crisis and the scale of the cross-border intervention being quietly undone.
[16]Yarmouk Brigade (Arabic: لواء اليرموكLiwa’ al-Yarmouk): a Syrian-controlled Palestinian military formation within the Palestine Liberation Army (PLA), named after the 636 CE Battle of Yarmouk. Its identification here as the Syrian-deployed cross-border unit provides direct attribution: the "heavily armed elements" who crossed from Syria were not fedayeen in general but a specific Syrian Army-controlled Palestinian formation acting as a Syrian proxy inside Lebanese territory.
[17]Mahmoud Riad: Arab League Secretary-General (1972–1979), previously Egyptian Foreign Minister. His arrival on May 4 to mediate the Lebanese crisis is an early Arab League intervention in what would become a pattern over the next decade — Arab League mediation missions to Lebanon during recurring Lebanese-Palestinian confrontations. The mediation arc that began in May 1973 with Riad’s visit arguably continued through the Taif Accord of 1989.
[18]King Hassan II of Morocco (1929–1999): the Moroccan monarch whose special emissary is noted as arriving alongside the Iraqi envoy. Morocco’s helpful posture is in the Embassy’s judgment consistent with its broader alignment with the moderate Arab consensus (Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Egypt) against the radical Arab states (Iraq, Libya, South Yemen) in this period.
[19]Frangieh’s insistence that any new agreement not accord fedayeen "the sort of freedom they had under the old Cairo Accords" is the direct antecedent of the Melkart Protocol language two weeks later (cables 1973BEIRUT05755/05776, May 18), which similarly sought to avoid ratifying the 1969 Cairo Agreement framework. The consistency of Frangieh’s position from the night of the crisis through the formal settlement documents the Presidency’s strategic continuity.
[20]UNRWA (United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East): established 1949. The Embassy records UNRWA’s inability to access any Beirut-area refugee camp since May 2 — access being restricted "both by the Army and fedayeen." The dual restriction is documented plainly and is one of the earliest cable references to the access problem that would recur over subsequent decades.
[21]The WikiLeaks archival copy of BEIRUT 05123 is corrupted from approximately this point onward. Text from an unrelated cable — concerning a World Health Organization Director-General housing-allowance matter in Geneva — appears interposed. The extraneous content has been excluded from the reproduction above. The recoverable content of SITREP 9 ends with the political stabilization paragraph. This is a well-known type of archival transmission error in the declassified cable corpus, caused by concatenation of adjacent microfilm frames during digitization.
[22]Mixed Army–fedayeen patrols: the emergence of joint patrols by May 4, 1400Z, reflects the de-escalation mechanism stipulated under the May 2 agreement — a framework that would be formalized two weeks later in the Melkart Protocol. The practical mechanism of a "mixed commission" to administer implementation, documented from the ground here in the SITREP, anticipated the architectural device of the Melkart Protocol (cables 1973BEIRUT05755/05776).
