Beirut, May 8, 1973

Before the Civil War: The Lebanese Army Opened Fire on Palestinian Camps

This cable, filed at 8:08 AM on May 8, 1973 — more than two years before the civil war — documents what the standard account of Lebanon's pre-war period does not: the Lebanese Army deploying aircraft and tanks against Palestinian refugee camps in Beirut, a Prime Minister resigning in mid-crisis, and U.S. Embassy documentation of Syrian manipulation of Palestinian factions to prevent a negotiated settlement.

Based on cable 1973BEIRUT05159 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

At 8:08 AM on May 8, 1973, the U.S. Embassy filed this Night Action Immediate cable — the highest available urgency level. By that point, the Lebanese Army had already deployed aircraft and tanks against Palestinian refugee camps in the southeastern suburbs of Beirut: Sabra/Chatila, Bir Hassan, and Borj el Brajne. The Prime Minister had resigned at 5:00 AM. The fighting that produced this had started the previous evening — a deliberate attempt, in the Embassy's assessment, by Palestinian factions including the PDFLP, PFLP/GC, and possibly Syria's client faction Sa'iqa, to torpedo negotiations that were moving toward an accommodation. Yasser Arafat had been in those negotiations when the fighting started and was, according to U.S. sources, trying to calm his forces by morning. A ceasefire held. The Prime Minister withdrew his resignation. An agreement was reached. But the pattern documented here — armed Palestinian factions sabotaging diplomacy, Syrian manipulation of events, Lebanese state institutions stretched to breaking — repeated itself in April 1975. That time, there was no ceasefire that held.

What this document reveals
  • The Lebanese Army used aircraft and tank fire against Palestinian refugee camps in Beirut in May 1973 — more than two years before the civil war. This event is largely absent from standard accounts of pre-war Lebanon.
  • The U.S. Embassy assessed the fighting as a deliberate attempt by specific Palestinian factions to torpedo ongoing negotiations — not a spontaneous escalation or a response to Lebanese Army provocation.
  • U.S. Embassy sources documented Syrian manipulation of Palestinian factions: Syria's foreign minister publicly announced more favorable terms in Damascus than had actually been agreed, while Syrian-aligned factions (PFLP/GC, Sa'iqa) led the military attack.
  • Arafat was in negotiations with Lebanese politicians when his own movement's hardliners opened fire — the same dynamic documented in the April 30, 1973 cable, where BSO activists acted against Arafat's wishes.
  • The GOL's tough public stance and refusal to accept Syrian-dictated terms is documented here in real time. The crisis ended in an agreement — the Melkart Protocol — that resolved nothing structurally and repeated the Cairo Agreement's pattern of paper compromises.

The Record

May 1973: Before the War

The Lebanese Civil War began on April 13, 1975. But the conditions that produced it were visible — and documented — years earlier. This cable, filed at 8:08 in the morning on May 8, 1973, is a window into one of those earlier moments: a night when Palestinian factions opened fire on Lebanese Army positions, the army responded with aircraft and tanks, a prime minister resigned before dawn, and the U.S. Embassy sent a Night Action cable to Washington.

The fighting of May 7, 1973

The fighting began around 7:00 PM on May 7. This cable describes it as a "deliberate attempt" by specific Palestinian factions to torpedo ongoing negotiations between the PLO and the Lebanese government — not a spontaneous escalation, not a response to Lebanese Army provocation. The factions the Embassy's Army sources identified as responsible were the PDFLP and the PFLP/GC, with possible PFLP and Sa'iqa involvement. All were on the harder-line end of the Palestinian spectrum. Two of them — the PFLP/GC and Sa'iqa — operated with close ties to Syrian intelligence.

The method was systematic: rockets and mortars at military installations and the airport, followed by armed vehicles driving through the city firing at police. The stated design, in the cable's words, was "to create panic and touch off a major army-fedayeen confrontation."

The Lebanese Army's response

It succeeded in touching off a confrontation, though perhaps not the one intended. The Lebanese Army responded with aircraft and tanks. By 7:00 AM on May 8, when the Embassy filed this cable, the army had already deployed this firepower against targets in and around three Palestinian refugee camps in Beirut's southeastern suburbs: Sabra/Chatila, Bir Hassan, and Borj el Brajne.

This is a fact that does not appear in the standard account of pre-war Lebanon. The Lebanese Army used aviation and armor against Palestinian camp areas in Beirut in May 1973. The conflict was contained. A ceasefire was arranged. An agreement — the Melkart Protocol — was reached nine days later. And the episode was largely absorbed into Lebanon's managed history of crises that somehow didn't become wars.

Two years later, they did.

Arafat in the negotiations

The cable's paragraph 4 contains a detail that mirrors the pattern documented in the April 30, 1973 cable from this archive: Yasser Arafat was in diplomatic meetings — with Lebanese politicians including Saeb Salam and Raymond Edde, and with Egyptian and Syrian representatives — when the fighting started. As the U.S. Embassy had assessed nine days earlier, hardliners within or adjacent to the Palestinian movement were acting in ways Arafat had not authorized and may have been unable to prevent.

By 8:00 AM on May 8, the Embassy's contact at the Lebanese Army confirmed: Arafat was trying to calm his forces.

The Syrian dimension

The cable documents something that rarely appears in standard historical accounts of May 1973: explicit U.S. Embassy awareness of Syrian manipulation. Paragraph 4 notes that Lebanon's government suspected a "Syrian hand working behind the scene" — and gives specific reasons. The Syrian Foreign Minister, Abdul Halim Khaddam, had publicly announced terms in Damascus far more favorable to the Palestinians than what had actually been agreed in Beirut negotiations. Meanwhile, the factions the Embassy identified as having initiated the fighting — PFLP/GC and Sa'iqa — were precisely those with the closest ties to Damascus.

Syria's interest in preventing a stable Lebanese-Palestinian accommodation was clear: such an accommodation would reduce Syrian leverage over both the PLO and Lebanon. The May 1973 crisis, in this cable's documentation, was not simply a Palestinian-Lebanese confrontation. It was a confrontation in which Syrian-aligned factions were working to prevent the outcome that Arafat and the Lebanese government were moving toward.

The pattern

The crisis ended the way Lebanese crises ended in those years: a ceasefire, a political adjustment, a formal agreement. Prime Minister Hafez withdrew his resignation. The Melkart Protocol of May 17, 1973 was signed — another paper framework governing Palestinian armed activity on Lebanese soil, like the Cairo Agreement before it.

But this cable documents the pattern that the Melkart Protocol did not resolve: Palestinian hardliners willing to use military force to prevent political accommodation; Syrian manipulation of events through proxy factions; a Lebanese state deploying its army against camps and then negotiating as though the army had not just used aircraft and tanks in a residential area of its own capital; a Lebanese political class managing the crisis rather than resolving its causes.

The pattern held for two more years. On April 13, 1975, it stopped holding.

Originally classified CONFIDENTIAL · Declassified and released by U.S. Department of State under EO Systematic Review · Now UNCLASSIFIED

1.
As of 0700Z [7:00 AM Greenwich Mean Time, May 8], the Beirut area is calm after the Lebanese Army had expended considerable firepower — including aircraft and tank fire[1] — against targets in and around refugee camps in the general southeastern quadrant of Beirut: specifically Sabra/Chatila, Bir Hassan, and Borj el Brajne camps.[2] The rest of the country has thus far remained calm despite the fighting in Beirut, although army units in the South Bekaa are fully alert to possible infiltration from Syria.[3]

2.
At 0500Z [5:00 AM GMT], Prime Minister Hafez resigned,[4] and the cabinet went into session to examine next steps — most important of which could be the designation of a military man as interim Prime Minister.

3.
The fighting which broke out at about 1900Z [7:00 PM GMT] on May 7 appears, in the light of day, to have been a deliberate attempt by some fedayeen elements to torpedo any possibility of stabilizing fedayeen–GOL relations on a new basis.[5] Army G-2 believes that the PDFLP and PFLP/GC[6] were involved, with probably some elements of PFLP and possibly Sa’iqa[7] as well. There are also reports of involvement by armed elements from the LCP.[8]

Between 1900Z and 1930Z, there was widespread firing of rockets and mortars, directed at two or three military installations and the airport but also at random throughout the city. At the same time, several cars full of armed men drove through parts of the city firing at police and other targets. The obvious design was to create panic and touch off a major army–fedayeen confrontation.

4.
At the time the fighting began,[9] Arafat[10] was meeting with several Lebanese political figures — including Saeb Salam[11] and Raymond Edde[12] — to discuss the progress of talks with the GOL on May 7. In these talks — in which Egyptian and Syrian representatives were present — Prime Minister Hafez had reached “some sort of vague first-step agreement” on the route to obtaining a new understanding. According to army participants in the talks, this agreement was nowhere near as detailed or firm — or as pro-fedayeen — as announced by the Syrian Foreign Minister[13] upon his return to Damascus. This is merely one of the reasons for GOL suspicion that a Syrian hand was working behind the scene.[14]

5.
Developments in the immediate future will depend on such factors as: whether Arafat will try and be able to calm the bulk of Fatah fedayeen, keeping as many as possible out of the fighting; whether leftist Lebanese elements (LCP, Ba’ath, PPS, etc.) and Sunni Muslims generally join the fedayeen in continuing the fight with the GOL; and whether the GOL decides actually to move into the camps — which it has not yet done.

Morale of the army continues to be good, with no sign of Muslim/Christian dissension.[15] Reinforcements have been brought into the Beirut area from the south. Army controls around the camps and checkpoints elsewhere are much tighter than before. The atmosphere in Beirut is very tense, with a curfew reimposed and the army — in accordance with the state of emergency — in charge of all security. Emergency law is being used to impose strict censorship on the press and also authorizes the army to arrest virtually anyone suspected of creating trouble. No American casualties reported and no Americans known to be in danger. Embassy well protected. Americans, especially tourists, are quite nervous. We are advising everyone to stay put. Airport closed.

6.
At 0800Z, an EMBOFF talked to the army (Kanaan[16]), who informed us that Arafat is — RPT — is trying to calm his troops. Immediately thereafter, Tony Frangie[17] told us that a new ceasefire came into effect as of 0800Z, and that it is quite possible that the Prime Minister will once again withdraw his resignation.

Tony confirmed that the GOL intends to take a tough line with the fedayeen in any talks and will not give in to demands, reach any sort of formal agreement, or accept the sort of nonsense that Khaddam[18] announced in Damascus has been agreed upon on May 8.[19]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The cable reads "TAK FIRE" — an apparent teletype transmission error or abbreviation for "TANK FIRE." The corrected reading is supported by context: the cable describes the Lebanese Army employing aviation and armor in combined-arms operations against urban refugee camp targets. Such errors were common in teletype cable transmission of the era and are preserved verbatim in the raw text.
[2]The Lebanese Army's use of aircraft and tank fire against Sabra/Chatila, Bir Hassan, and Borj el Brajne — three Palestinian refugee camps located in the heavily populated southeastern suburbs of Beirut — is one of the most significant facts in this cable. By the morning of May 8, 1973, the Lebanese state had committed its air force and armor against civilian-adjacent camps on Lebanese sovereign territory. This level of military force against Palestinian positions in Beirut had not previously occurred since the Lebanese Army was effectively prohibited from full enforcement by the 1969 Cairo Agreement. The cable reports this as already completed as of 0700Z — the fighting, and the army's response to it, had occurred overnight.
[3]"Army units in South Bekaa fully alert to possible infiltration from Syria": the cable explicitly flags Syria as a potential external source of infiltration in the event of escalation. This concern is elaborated in paragraph 4, where the Embassy records GOL suspicion of a "Syrian hand working behind the scene." The South Bekaa — the Bekaa Valley's southern portion — shares a long border with Syria and was a known route for Palestinian and Syrian-backed armed movements into Lebanon.
[4]Amin al-Hafez (1911–1989): Lebanese Sunni Muslim politician, Prime Minister of Lebanon from April 1973. His cabinet had been attempting to negotiate a revised agreement governing Palestinian armed activity in Lebanon when the May 7 fighting erupted. His resignation at 0500Z on May 8 — in the middle of an active military crisis — represented the collapse of civilian political authority at the moment it was most needed. As paragraph 6 records, he later withdrew the resignation. Al-Hafez should not be confused with Hafez al-Assad, the Syrian President at the time.
[5]The Embassy's assessment that the May 7 fighting was a "deliberate attempt by some fedayeen elements to torpedo any possibility of stabilizing fedayeen-GOL relations" is a significant analytical judgment. It indicates that the Embassy did not regard the outbreak of fighting as accidental, reactive, or attributable to Lebanese Army provocation — but as a purposeful act by specific Palestinian factions intent on preventing a negotiated settlement. This assessment is filed at 0808Z on May 8, within hours of the events described.
[6]PFLP/GC: Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine — General Command. A breakaway faction from the PFLP founded in 1968 by Ahmad Jibril, a former Syrian Army officer. The PFLP/GC split from the PFLP over the latter's ideological emphasis, preferring purely military operations. Operating with close ties to Syrian intelligence and the Syrian military, the PFLP/GC was one of the Palestinian factions most resistant to accommodation with the Lebanese state and most susceptible to direction from Damascus. Its named involvement in the May 7 fighting — alongside the PDFLP and possibly Sa'iqa — is consistent with the cable's concurrent suspicion of Syrian manipulation of events.
[7]Sa'iqa (formally "Vanguards of the Popular Liberation War" — Arabic: طلائع حرب التحرير الشعبية): Syrian Ba'ath Party-sponsored Palestinian armed faction operating in Lebanon. Unlike Fatah, which sought autonomy from Arab state influence, Sa'iqa was effectively an instrument of Syrian policy — its leadership appointed by Damascus and its operations coordinated with Syrian intelligence. Its possible involvement in the May 7 fighting — alongside the PFLP/GC, another Syrian-aligned faction — is one of the facts that generated GOL suspicion of a "Syrian hand working behind the scene," as the cable notes in paragraph 4.
[8]LCP: Lebanese Communist Party (الحزب الشيوعي اللبناني). Founded in 1924, the LCP was a multi-sectarian Marxist party that aligned with the PLO and Palestinian resistance during the 1970s. The cable reports "armed elements" from the LCP as involved in the May 7 fighting — meaning that the confrontation was not exclusively Palestinian, but included Lebanese leftist factions. This is consistent with the pre-civil war pattern in which Palestinian armed organizations and Lebanese leftist parties increasingly coordinated against the Lebanese Army and the Maronite-dominated state.
[9]The cable reads "AT TIME FIRMING BEGAN" — an apparent teletype transmission error for "AT TIME FIGHTING BEGAN." The corrected reading is unambiguous from context: the preceding paragraph describes the outbreak of fighting at 1900Z on May 7, and the subsequent sentence describes Arafat's activities during that fighting. The original error is preserved verbatim in the raw cable text.
[10]Yasser Arafat (1929–2004): Chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization and leader of Fatah. The cable's description of Arafat meeting with Lebanese political figures — including representatives of both communities — at the precise moment fighting broke out is significant. It places Arafat in a negotiating posture while other Palestinian factions (PDFLP, PFLP/GC, Sa'iqa) initiated military action. Whether Arafat had foreknowledge of the May 7 attacks and was using the negotiations as cover, or was genuinely caught off-guard by factions operating outside his control, is not resolved in this cable — though paragraph 6 reports he was "trying to calm his troops" by 0800Z.
[11]Saeb Salam (1905–2000): Sunni Muslim politician from one of Beirut's most prominent families, a former Prime Minister of Lebanon (1952–1953, 1960–1961, 1970–1973). Salam had recently resigned as Prime Minister in April 1973, just days before the events documented in this cable, specifically over the question of the Lebanese Army's response to Palestinian armed activity — arguing the army was not doing enough. His presence in the negotiations documented in this cable, sitting alongside Arafat when fighting erupted, places a major Lebanese Sunni political figure in direct diplomatic contact with PLO leadership during the crisis.
[12]Raymond Edde (1913–2000): Maronite Christian politician, leader of the National Bloc (Kataeb rival), and a consistently independent voice in Lebanese politics known for his opposition to Syrian influence and his criticism of both PLO armed activity in Lebanon and excessive Maronite intransigence. His presence in the negotiations alongside Arafat and Salam indicates that the talks involved cross-sectarian Lebanese political representation. Edde would later go into voluntary exile in Paris in 1976, citing threats to his life.
[13]The "Syrian Foreign Minister" referred to is Abdul Halim Khaddam (1932–2020), Syrian Ba'ath politician who served as Foreign Minister 1970–1984 before becoming Vice President of Syria. A key operative of the Assad regime's Lebanon policy, Khaddam was closely involved in Syrian manipulation of Lebanese affairs throughout the civil war. The cable's note that Khaddam publicly announced terms in Damascus far more favorable to the fedayeen than what had actually been agreed — and that GOL army participants in the talks confirmed this discrepancy — is a specific documented instance of Syrian disinformation in the context of Lebanese-Palestinian negotiations.
[14]"Syrian hand working behind the scene": the cable records this as GOL's own assessment, not merely Embassy speculation. The simultaneous involvement of PFLP/GC and Sa'iqa — both Syrian-aligned or Syrian-directed factions — in the May 7 fighting, combined with Khaddam's inflation of the agreement terms, constituted a coherent pattern: Syrian interest in preventing a stable Lebanese-Palestinian accommodation that might reduce Syrian leverage over both parties. This cable, filed May 8, 1973, documents U.S. Embassy awareness of Syrian manipulation of Lebanese-Palestinian relations more than two years before the civil war began.
[15]"No sign of Muslim/Christian dissension" within the Lebanese Army: the cable explicitly notes the army's internal cohesion as of May 8, 1973. This cohesion would progressively erode over the following two years as the sectarian polarization of Lebanese society deepened. By early 1976, significant portions of the Lebanese Army — predominantly Muslim officers and enlisted men — mutinied and joined the Palestinian-LNM alliance, fracturing the institution entirely and removing the last restraint on full-scale civil war.
[16]"Kanaan": the Lebanese Army contact referenced here has not been definitively identified. The name may refer to a senior Lebanese Army officer serving in a liaison capacity with the U.S. Embassy. It should not be confused with Ghazi Kanaan (1942–2005), the Syrian intelligence chief who would later dominate Lebanon — though that Syrian officer would not have had this role in 1973.
[17]Tony Frangie (Toni Frangieh, 1942–1978): eldest son of President Suleiman Frangieh, serving at this time as a presidential aide and informal channel between the Lebanese Presidency and the U.S. Embassy. Tony Frangieh was the father of Suleiman Frangieh Jr., the current Lebanese political leader. He was assassinated on June 13, 1978, along with his wife, daughter, and bodyguards, in a pre-dawn raid on his home at Ehden by Bashir Gemayel's Lebanese Forces — one of the most consequential intra-Maronite killings of the civil war, which permanently poisoned relations between the Frangieh and Gemayel clans.
[18]Abdul Halim Khaddam: see footnote 13. The cable's characterization of Khaddam's Damascus announcement as "nonsense" — in the voice of Tony Frangieh relaying the GOL's official position — is a notably blunt diplomatic formulation, preserved verbatim. (The cable reads "NONESENSE" — a teletype spelling error for "NONSENSE," preserved in the raw text.)
[19]The cable's closing note that the GOL "will not give in to demands, reach any sort of formal agreement, or accept" terms as stated by Khaddam reflects a deliberately tough public stance. In fact, the crisis was resolved within days: a ceasefire held, PM Hafez withdrew his resignation, and negotiations produced the Melkart Protocol of May 17, 1973 — an agreement that, like the 1969 Cairo Agreement before it, formally governed Palestinian armed activity on Lebanese soil while falling far short of restoring Lebanese state sovereignty. The pattern documented in this cable — military confrontation followed by negotiated agreement that neither side fully honored — repeated itself until the civil war erupted in April 1975.
[20]The cable is signed HOUGHTON — the same signatory as cable 1973BEIRUT04974 (April 30, 1973). This is likely Roderic L. Houghton, a senior U.S. Foreign Service Officer serving at the Beirut Embassy in 1973. Ambassador William Buffum was the U.S. Ambassador to Lebanon from 1970 to 1974; cables signed by a named official other than the Ambassador typically indicate the Ambassador was absent or that the Deputy Chief of Mission (DCM) or Chargé d'Affaires dispatched the cable. The NIACT IMMEDIATE designation — requiring attention regardless of the hour — confirms the urgency with which the Embassy assessed the situation.
