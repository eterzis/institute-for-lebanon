Beirut, July 9, 1974

More PLO Than the PLO: Kuwait's Draft at the Arab Defense Council

Filed July 9, 1974, SECRET/EXDIS/NOFORN. The Lebanese presidential adviser's readout of the Arab Defense Council meeting in Cairo: Kuwait tried to equate the PLO with the Lebanese state, proposing LL 50 million each in defense funding. The Kuwaiti FM questioned Lebanon's Arab status — and privately admitted his life had been threatened by the PLO. Saudi Arabia kept its defense minister in reserve 'for when things turned bad.' Syria, surprisingly, moderated.

Based on cable 1974BEIRUT07889 SECRET → UNCLASSIFIED

On July 8, 1974, U.S. Ambassador Godley's deputy called on Presidential Adviser Boutros Dib for a readout of the July 3-4 Arab Defense Council meeting in Cairo. Dib gave him one on condition of strictest handling: SECRET/EXDIS/NOFORN, need-to-know basis only. The cable reports what Dib said: Kuwait's Foreign Minister Sabah circulated a draft resolution treating the PLO and Lebanon as equals, with LL 50 million each for defense. The Kuwaiti representative questioned Lebanese 'Arab' status — prompting PM Solh to threaten walking out. In private, the Kuwaiti FM admitted the PLO had threatened his life. The Sa'iqa representative Zuhair Mohsen was 'particularly violent,' and the Lebanese delegation believed he was personally profiting from Kuwaiti money. Syrian FM Khaddam, who had been a provocateur in 1973, played moderator in 1974. Iraq and Libya attacked Syria and Egypt for having accepted disengagement. Saudi Arabia kept its Defense Minister absent from the outset 'to save his punch for later' if the meeting went badly. The Lebanese non-Muslim ministers took pains to defend the Muslim PM from attacks by other Arabs. The final resolutions — the 'Secret Resolutions' — papered over everything.

What this document reveals
  • Kuwait's Foreign Minister circulated a draft resolution at the Arab Defense Council proposing LL 50 million each for the PLO and the Lebanese government, treating an armed non-state actor as diplomatically equal to a sovereign state within Arab League defense architecture.
  • The Kuwaiti FM questioned Lebanon's 'Arab' status in open ADC discussion. Prime Minister Solh threatened to walk out; the Kuwaiti withdrew the paper.
  • In private diplomatic conversation, the Kuwaiti FM admitted his life had been threatened by the PLO — and, the Lebanese assessed, this fear was governing his positions.
  • The Lebanese delegation was convinced that Sa'iqa leader Zuhair Mohsen personally profited immensely from “Kuwaiti generosity” — consistent with the pattern of faction leaders as intermediaries for Gulf money into Lebanese political operations.
  • Syria had reversed its 1973 role: FM Khaddam, who had been a provocateur in 1973, played moderator in 1974 — moderating even between Lebanon and Sa'iqa, a Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction.
  • Iraq and Libya directed their venom not at Lebanon but at Syria and Egypt for having agreed to disengagement — confirming cable 1974BEIRUT05740's May forecast that radical Arab states would work to derail the post-war diplomatic architecture.

The Record

The call on Dib

On the afternoon of July 8, 1974, four days after the Arab Defense Council meeting in Cairo ended, a U.S. Embassy officer called on Presidential Adviser Boutros Dib in Beirut. Dib had accompanied Prime Minister Solh, Foreign Minister Naffah, and Defense Minister Maalouf to the meeting. The Embassy officer complimented Dib on the outcome — press accounts suggested Lebanon had successfully avoided committing to Arab military assistance while maintaining its Arab-world position. Dib confirmed the success and then gave a detailed readout of how it had been achieved.

Ambassador Godley filed the cable the next day under the tightest handling restrictions available: SECRET, EXDIS (Exclusive Distribution), NOFORN (No Foreign nationals), and transmitted in two sections. Dib had requested that the material be used "only on a need-to-know basis." He was being candid about Arab delegations in a way he could only be candid if he was confident the readout would not surface in Arab diplomatic channels.

The Kuwaiti draft

The central incident of the ADC meeting was the Kuwaiti initiative. Before the plenary session on July 3, Kuwaiti Foreign Minister Sheikh Sabah circulated a draft resolution that proposed LL 50 million each for the Government of Lebanon and the PLO, to be distributed on equal terms for defense purposes. To the Lebanese delegation, this was unacceptable in principle: it treated a sovereign state and an armed non-state actor as diplomatically equivalent within an Arab League collective defense framework. The Lebanese rejection argued both Lebanese sovereignty and practical consequences — arming the PLO further would "jeopardize the current fragile peace along the border and result in Israeli reprisals."

The exchange escalated. The Kuwaiti representative questioned Lebanese "Arab" status. Prime Minister Solh — a Sunni Muslim politician from one of Lebanon's most prominent political families — replied that if the assertion were not withdrawn immediately, the Lebanese delegation would not attend the ADC meeting. The Kuwaiti withdrew the paper.

Fear as foreign policy

But the more striking piece of Dib's readout was what he said the Kuwaiti delegation had admitted in private discussion. When Lebanese representatives pressed the Kuwaitis on the discrepancy between Kuwait's maximalist PLO advocacy at the ADC and Kuwait's own domestic handling of its large Palestinian population, the Kuwaiti Foreign Minister stated that his life had been threatened by the PLO — and, in Dib's characterization, "gave every appearance of permitting this threat to govern his actions."

A sovereign Gulf foreign minister, acknowledging in private diplomatic conversation that his public policy was being driven by assassination threats from an armed non-state actor. Preserved in the U.S. diplomatic record. The cable does not embellish or moralize; it records the observation as Dib made it.

The Sa'iqa paymaster picture

The Sa'iqa representative Zuhair Mohsen — the same figure who would become famous three years later for declaring that Palestinian identity was a political construction — led the most aggressive attacks on the Lebanese delegation at the ADC, supporting the Kuwaiti draft. The Lebanese delegation's assessment, transmitted through Dib: Mohsen was personally profiting immensely from "Kuwaiti generosity." The allegation is consistent with cable 1973BEIRUT06768, which had documented Arafat's direct payments from the Fatah treasury to Lebanese religious and political leaders. Across the 1973–1974 cable record, the picture that emerges is of a political economy in which Palestinian faction leaders function as intermediaries for state funding flows — with personal enrichment as a structural feature, not an aberration.

Khaddam, moderator

The 1974 Syrian posture at the ADC was strikingly different from 1973. Foreign Minister Khaddam — who in cable 1973BEIRUT05159 was documented deliberately misrepresenting Lebanese negotiating positions during the May 1973 crisis — here acts as "wise moderator," repeatedly mediating between the Lebanese delegation and the Sa'iqa representative. The irony is specific: Sa'iqa was a Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction. Khaddam was mediating between Lebanon and a group his own government directed. Syrian control of Sa'iqa was functioning as diplomatic leverage — Syria could produce moderation by constraining Sa'iqa, and could produce confrontation by unleashing it. In July 1974, with Israeli-Syrian disengagement fresh, Syria was choosing moderation.

The rejectionists' target

Iraq and Libya, as cable 1974BEIRUT05740 had forecast in May, were working against the post-October 1973 diplomatic architecture. But their venom at the ADC was directed not at Lebanon — which had taken a pro-PLO political position rhetorically while rejecting it materially — but at Syria and Egypt for having accepted disengagement. The Lebanese delegation was "surprised by the degree of their vehemence." The rejectionist Arab states were attacking the moderate Arab states. The diplomatic coalition that had unified during the October war was fracturing along the lines the May cable had predicted.

Sultan in reserve

One detail captures Saudi Arabia's characteristic posture. The Saudi delegation at the outset was headed by the Saudi Chief of Staff and the Saudi Arab League representative — not by Defense Minister Prince Sultan, whose attendance would have been the normal Saudi level. The Lebanese were told that Sultan was probably being held back "to save his punch for some later occasion, should the meeting turn out badly." The Saudi calculation was that the ADC meeting might go wrong from a Saudi perspective, and that intervening at the highest level was an option to be preserved for a later contingency. The graduated, conservative Saudi approach to Arab diplomacy was structural.

Lebanese internal dynamics

The cable's sixth paragraph contains an observation that would be easy to overlook but captures something important about pre-war Lebanese politics. Within the GOL delegation, Christian Foreign Minister Naffah and Christian Defense Minister Maalouf "took pains to protect Muslim Prime Minister Solh from attacks by other Arabs." Sunni Arab attacks on a Sunni Lebanese Prime Minister, deflected by his Christian cabinet colleagues. The confessional system's external stress points ran at right angles to its internal stress points — Lebanese Christians and Muslims, in government service, defended each other against external attacks that used confessional frames against them.

That solidarity would not hold indefinitely. Nine months after this cable was filed, the Lebanese civil war began. But in July 1974, in Cairo, a Lebanese delegation internally unified across confessional lines defeated a Kuwaiti proposal that would have equated the Lebanese state with an armed non-state actor operating on Lebanese territory.

The resolutions that papered over everything

The final "Secret Resolutions" contained three clauses, all acceptable to the GOL: diplomatic pressure would be applied to the "Great Powers" (i.e., the United States) to restrain Israeli action against Lebanon; unspecified aid would be provided to the GOL for its defensive position; and the PLO was re-recognized as the representative of the Palestinian people consistent with the Algiers summit language. There was no specific reference to material or financial assistance to the PLO in Lebanon. The Lebanese had succeeded in obtaining the rhetorical Arab solidarity they needed while avoiding the substantive commitments that would have further eroded their sovereignty.

The success was temporary. Cable 1974BEIRUT11970, filed three months later, would document the Solh government's collapse. The Arab resolutions of July 1974 went into the record. The Lebanese internal disintegration continued.

Summary
A high-ranking GOL source who was present at the Arab Defense Council (ADC) meeting in Cairo has confirmed our impression that the GOL successfully managed to deflect offers of Arab military assistance.[1] He also provided some interesting insights into the positions adopted by the various Arab delegations.

1. The readout meeting
We called yesterday afternoon, July 8, on Presidential Adviser Boutros Dib,[2] who accompanied Prime Minister Takieddine Solh,[3] Foreign Minister Naffah, and Minister of Defense Maalouf to Cairo for the Arab Defense Council meeting July 3–4. We complimented Dib on the meeting’s outcome, since from press accounts it appeared that the Lebanese objective of not accepting all-out Arab military assistance had been achieved while at the same time maintaining Lebanon’s position in the Arab world. Dib said that the Lebanese had succeeded, but that it had involved acrimonious and often extremely tough exchanges with Arab colleagues.

2. The Kuwaiti draft
The principal problem, and a rather unexpected one, was the position of Kuwaiti Foreign Minister Sabah,[4] who, prior to the plenary session July 3, had circulated a draft resolution which treated the PLO and GOL on an equal footing and which provided for 50 million Lebanese pounds to be accorded each to the GOL and the PLO on an equal basis for defense.[5] The Kuwaiti representative appeared to the GOL delegation to be “more PLO than the PLO,” and he led the hard-line Arabs in subsequent discussions. The Kuwaiti position was particularly resented by the Lebanese — for, according to the latter, the Kuwaitis showed total disregard for Lebanese sovereignty and all that the GOL had done for the PLO, as well as the sufferings borne by the Lebanese due to this “hospitality.”

In pre-plenary discussions the Lebanese rejected Kuwait’s position, arguing not only Lebanese sovereignty but also the fact that Kuwaiti generosity to the PLO would jeopardize the current fragile peace along the border and result in Israeli reprisals.[6] The Kuwaiti representative at one point questioned Lebanese “Arab” status, to which Prime Minister Takieddine Solh replied that if this assertion were not withdrawn immediately, the Lebanese would not attend the ADC meeting. As a result, the Kuwaiti representative withdrew his paper.

3. Plenary and closed sessions
The plenary session on the afternoon of July 3 was, according to Dib, relatively anodyne, with each delegation making anticipated public statements. The ensuing closed sessions, at which only delegation chiefs (Foreign Ministers and/or Ministers of Defense) were present, were, however, acrimonious. They coincided with working meetings, either with all delegations represented or in smaller groups.

4. Delegation-by-delegation behavior
Dib described the behavior of the various delegations along the following lines:

Egyptians were gracious hosts, saying relatively little but showing understanding of the Lebanese position.

Syrians were most helpful in supporting the GOL position. Foreign Minister Khaddam[7] acted wisely and, on several occasions, as moderator between the GOL and the Sa’iqa representative Mohsen.[8]

Sa’iqa delegation, led by Muhsen, was particularly violent, supporting the Kuwaitis and frequently attacking the Lebanese. (As noted REFTEL, Muhsen has continued to criticize the GOL since returning from Cairo.) Dib said the GOL delegation was convinced that Muhsen personally profited immensely from “Kuwaiti generosity”.[9]

Kuwaiti delegation, in addition to what is indicated above, appeared, according to Dib, terrified of the PLO. At an initial private discussion, the Kuwaiti representative, when challenged by the Lebanese regarding the relative status of Palestinians in Lebanon versus Kuwait, stated that his life had been threatened by the PLO and gave every appearance of permitting this threat to govern his actions.[10]

Algerian delegation under Bouteflika’s leadership[11] showed great statesmanship as a moderating element. On one occasion, however, Bouteflika advanced a lengthy legal argument regarding PLO rights having been accepted at the UN, but he formally withdrew his comments when attacked by Naffah, the GOL Foreign Minister.

Iraqi and Libyan delegations caused no great problems for the GOL in that most of their venom appeared directed against the Syrians and Egyptians for having agreed to disengagement.[12] Dib said the GOL delegation was surprised by the degree of their vehemence, which the Syrians and Egyptians handled in the most dignified manner.

5. The Saudi delegation
The Saudi Arabian delegation was headed by the Saudi Chief of Staff and Representative to the Arab League. The absence of Saudi Minister of Defense Sultan at the outset caused the GOL delegation some concern, but their concern was somewhat mollified when informed that Sultan was probably absent to save his punch for some later occasion, should the meeting turn out badly.[13]

6. GOL delegation internals
Dib said that the GOL delegation was in total harmony, and that the non-Muslims — e.g., Foreign Minister Naffah and Minister of Defense Maalouf — took pains to protect Muslim Prime Minister Solh from attacks by other Arabs.[14] Solh behaved most effectively, particularly as he was smarting from early Kuwaiti attacks that the GOL was not behaving as an Arab state.

7. The “Secret Resolutions”
The final “Secret Resolutions,” according to Dib, contained three clauses which were totally acceptable to the GOL:

(A) Diplomatic pressure would be brought on the “Great Powers” — i.e., the U.S. — to persuade Israel not to continue action against Lebanon. (In this connection Dib pointed out that fedayeen activity from Lebanon should cease.)

(B) Unspecified aid would be accorded the GOL in its efforts to improve its defensive position. (Dib commented that Minister of Defense Maalouf pointed out that Lebanon’s LL 2.2 billion defense program over the next five years was a big burden on the GOL but it would be met. If other states desired to contribute to this program, assistance would of course be most welcome provided no strings attached.)[15]

(C) The Council agreed that the PLO represented the Palestinian people and that it would be supported in its struggle to regain its homeland. Dib emphasized that this had already been agreed to at Algiers[16] and that there was no specific reference to material or financial assistance to the PLO in Lebanon.

8. Caveat
We recognize the foregoing is obviously one-sided. We believe, however, that the atmospherics set forth above might be in interest, and it is a relatively accurate description of the Lebanese delegation’s views and impressions of the meeting. Repeatedly Dib emphasized the sensitivity of the foregoing and requested that it not only be strictly NOFORN but used only on a need-to-know basis.[17]

GODLEY

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The Arab Defense Council (ADC) meeting of July 3-4, 1974 in Cairo was convened in the aftermath of the May 31 Israeli-Syrian disengagement agreement and the intensified Israeli attacks on southern Lebanon documented in cable 1974BEIRUT05740. The Arab League framework provided for collective defense arrangements; Lebanon's strategic objective at the meeting was to obtain diplomatic support against Israeli retaliation without accepting Arab military assistance that would have further compromised its sovereignty and triggered more Israeli action. The phrase "deflect offers of Arab military assistance" captures the Lebanese state's core dilemma in this period: accepting Arab military aid would have required hosting additional foreign armed elements on Lebanese soil and would have provoked Israeli escalation; refusing it required walking a diplomatic tightrope of appearing loyal to Arab solidarity while protecting Lebanese sovereignty.
[2]Boutros Dib: Presidential Adviser to President Suleiman Frangieh, one of the Embassy's most senior Lebanese sources. Dib appears in the 1973 cable series (1973BEIRUT05437) as a Presidency source and here in 1974 as a member of the GOL delegation to the ADC. His role as the Embassy's channel for confidential readouts of sensitive Lebanese diplomatic engagements is a structural feature of U.S.-Lebanese communication in the pre-war period.
[3]Takieddine Solh (1908–1988): Sunni Muslim Prime Minister of Lebanon, June 1973 – October 1974. His government — assessed in cable 1974BEIRUT11970 as characterized by "do-nothingness" — would collapse in the fall of 1974. Solh's presence at the ADC meeting, and his direct confrontation with the Kuwaiti representative who questioned Lebanese "Arab" status, documents a rare moment of Lebanese firmness from a government whose general characteristic was passivity. A Sunni PM defending Lebanese sovereignty against a hostile Kuwaiti (Sunni) delegation illustrates the complexity of intra-Sunni Arab politics that the sectarian frame obscures.
[4]Sheikh Sabah al-Ahmad al-Jaber al-Sabah: Kuwaiti Foreign Minister (1963–2003), later Emir of Kuwait (2006–2020). His role at the July 1974 ADC meeting — circulating a draft resolution treating Lebanon and the PLO as equal claimants on Arab defense funding, and openly questioning Lebanon's Arab status — is documented here. The Kuwaiti position is analytically significant: Kuwait was not a radical Arab state but rather a pro-Western Gulf monarchy whose Palestinian population was large and politically influential. The cable's suggestion that Sabah's actions were partly driven by personal threats from the PLO — "his life had been threatened by the PLO" — is a striking allegation preserved in the diplomatic record.
[5]LL 50 million for each of the GOL and PLO, on equal footing, for defense: this is the specific proposal the Lebanese delegation rejected. Equating a sovereign state with an armed non-state actor — and funding them equally for defense activities on Lebanese territory — would have formally elevated the PLO to parity with the Lebanese state in the Arab League framework, with structural implications for Lebanese sovereignty far beyond the monetary value of the proposal. This is the concrete diplomatic form of the "state within a state" dynamic that successive cables in this archive document. LL 50 million in 1974 was approximately USD 15-20 million at contemporary exchange rates.
[6]The Lebanese argument — that Arab military aid to the PLO in Lebanon would jeopardize the "fragile peace along the border and result in Israeli reprisals" — accepted the implicit logic of the feedback loop documented in cable 1974BEIRUT05740: arming the fedayeen would produce Israeli retaliation would produce Lebanese instability. The GOL's diplomatic strategy was to prevent the first link in this chain by rejecting Arab support that would arm the fedayeen further. This was the policy choice for which Solh's government was simultaneously most successful diplomatically and most criticized politically.
[7]Abdul Halim Khaddam (1932–2020): Syrian Foreign Minister (1970–1984), later Vice President. Khaddam's appearance in this cable as a "wise moderator" stands in sharp contrast to his role in the 1973 cable series, where cable 1973BEIRUT05159 (May 8, 1973) reported Khaddam deliberately misrepresenting negotiating terms during the May 1973 crisis. The inversion is notable: in 1973, Khaddam was the Syrian provocateur; in 1974, following Syrian-Israeli disengagement, Khaddam played moderator. Syrian policy toward Lebanon was shifting. The cable's observation that Khaddam "moderated between the GOL and Sa'iqa" is particularly revealing, because Sa'iqa was a Syrian-controlled Palestinian faction — meaning Khaddam was moderating between Lebanon and a PLO faction Syria itself directed. Syria was using its control of Sa'iqa as leverage in Arab diplomacy.
[8]Zuhair Mohsen (also spelled Muhsen; 1936–1979): leader of Sa'iqa, the Syrian-backed Palestinian faction within the PLO. Mohsen was one of the most militant and outspoken Palestinian leaders, famous for his 1977 statement that "there is no difference between Jordanians, Palestinians, Syrians, and Lebanese... Only for political reasons do we carefully underline our Palestinian identity." His position at the ADC supporting the Kuwaiti draft resolution — attacking the Lebanese delegation from within the Arab framework — and his continued criticism of the GOL after returning to Beirut (noted as having been documented in REFTEL) illustrate the tactical alignment of Syrian-backed Palestinian factions with Kuwaiti policy despite the two being from very different points on the Arab political spectrum. Mohsen was assassinated in Cannes in 1979.
[9]"Muhsen personally profited immensely from Kuwaiti generosity": this is a specific allegation — that the Sa'iqa leader was receiving direct Kuwaiti payments — preserved in the U.S. diplomatic record on the basis of a statement by the Lebanese presidential adviser. The allegation is consistent with the broader pattern documented in cable 1973BEIRUT06768 (June 8, 1973), which recorded Arafat's direct payments to Lebanese political figures from the Fatah treasury. The emerging picture, across these cables, is of a political economy in which Palestinian faction leaders were intermediaries for Gulf Arab state funding to be directed into Lebanese political operations — with personal enrichment for the intermediaries as a structural feature of the system.
[10]"His life had been threatened by the PLO": this is the most striking single sentence in the cable. A Kuwaiti Foreign Minister — sovereign of a major Arab state — admitting in private diplomatic discussion that his public policy positions were being driven by PLO death threats against him personally. The Lebanese delegation's reading — that this fear "governed his actions" — is recorded as the GOL assessment of the Kuwaiti position. This observation, preserved in the U.S. diplomatic record, is a factual datum about the operational pressure under which Gulf Arab states were making Palestinian-related policy in 1974.
[11]Abdelaziz Bouteflika (1937–2021): Algerian Foreign Minister (1963–1979), later President of Algeria (1999–2019). Bouteflika was the "moderating element" at the ADC meeting — consistent with Algeria's position as a radical-but-responsible Arab actor in this period. His formal withdrawal of a legal argument on PLO UN rights after being attacked by Lebanese Foreign Minister Naffah illustrates the specific diplomatic deference Bouteflika was willing to extend to Lebanese sensitivities, even at the cost of PLO positional advocacy.
[12]Iraq and Libya directed their criticism at Syria and Egypt (for accepting disengagement) rather than at Lebanon — exactly the pattern cable 1974BEIRUT05740 predicted: radical Arab states working to derail the post-war diplomatic architecture. The Embassy had forecast this dynamic on May 22; the ADC meeting of July 3-4 confirmed it. The Lebanese delegation's "surprise at the degree of their vehemence" documents that even experienced Lebanese diplomats underestimated the intensity of intra-Arab disagreement about post-October 1973 Middle East diplomacy.
[13]"To save his punch for some later occasion, should the meeting turn out badly": Prince Sultan bin Abdulaziz al-Saud, Saudi Defense Minister (1963–2011), was absent from the outset of the ADC meeting. The Saudi delegation explanation — that Sultan was being held in reserve for a later contingency — suggests the Saudi assessment that the ADC meeting might "turn out badly" (from a Saudi perspective), requiring higher-level Saudi intervention if outcomes proved adverse. Saudi Arabia's approach to Arab collective defense diplomacy, as reflected here, was characterized by graduated escalation reserved for contingencies — a pattern consistent with Saudi diplomatic style across the period.
[14]"Non-Muslims took pains to protect Muslim Prime Minister Solh from attacks by other Arabs": the inversion captured here is diplomatically striking. In the Lebanese confessional system, the Prime Minister is constitutionally Sunni Muslim. At this meeting, the Christian Foreign Minister (Naffah) and Christian Defense Minister (Maalouf) were publicly defending their Sunni Prime Minister against attacks from Sunni Arab states (Kuwait in particular). The internal Lebanese delegation dynamic — Christians defending Muslim leadership against Muslim external attacks — illustrates that Lebanese sovereignty concerns, at least at the governmental level, had transcended confessional alignment in ways that Lebanon's critics in the Arab world did not expect.
[15]LL 2.2 billion (approximately USD 800 million to USD 1 billion at 1974 exchange rates) five-year defense program: this is a specific commitment by the Lebanese Ministry of Defense documented here. The caveat — "no strings attached" — signals the Lebanese insistence that any Arab financial assistance not carry political conditions that would compromise Lebanese sovereignty or force Lebanese state engagement against the fedayeen, or for them. Cable 1974BEIRUT11970 (October 2, 1974) notes that parliamentary inertia had prevented significant progress in strengthening the Lebanese Army over the next three months, despite the announced defense program — documenting the gap between GOL declared commitments and GOL legislative capacity.
[16]The Algiers Arab League Summit of November 1973 — the first Arab League summit after the October Yom Kippur War — formally recognized the PLO as "the sole legitimate representative of the Palestinian people," a formulation that would be enshrined in subsequent Arab collective positions and ultimately endorsed by the UN General Assembly in Resolution 3236 of November 1974. Dib's emphasis that the ADC meeting merely reiterated what had been agreed at Algiers — without specific reference to PLO operations in Lebanon — documents the GOL's careful calibration: supporting the PLO's political role in principle while resisting its operational role on Lebanese territory.
[17]NOFORN and "need-to-know basis": the tightest handling restriction combination. NOFORN prohibits sharing with foreign governments, which given the cable's contents (detailed readouts of other Arab delegations' private positions, including specific allegations about individual foreign leaders' corruption, personal fears, and motivations) was essential. The classification — SECRET/EXDIS/NOFORN — is among the most restrictive in the State Department system. The cable was written on the understanding that it would not circulate beyond a small U.S. readership. That it now appears in the public record is a function of Freedom of Information Act declassification processes half a century after the events.
