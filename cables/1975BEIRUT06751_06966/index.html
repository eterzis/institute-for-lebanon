Beirut · Damascus · Washington, May 27 – June 3, 1975

The Karame Reconciliation: Syrian Mediation, the Jordan-1970 Warning, and the Bardaqan Killing

Fourteen cables across four posts (Beirut, Damascus, State) document the week after the Rifai military government’s collapse: Rashid Karame’s designation as Prime Minister, the anonymous “U.S. Official” Reuters comment that alarmed Lebanese leaders by comparing the crisis to Jordan 1970, the two Embassy Damascus cables recording Syria’s explicit non-intervention commitment, the June 1 assassination of Phalange-NLP liaison Naim Bardaqan, the Phalange-NLP general strike that followed, and the establishment of joint police–PLO patrols that defined Beirut’s first buffer zones. Godley’s June 3 retrospective judges Syrian mediation “generally constructive” and notes that Damascus is no longer giving the PLO a blank check.

Fourteen U.S. diplomatic cables across four posts document the week between the Rifai military government’s collapse and the establishment of Karame’s civilian cabinet. Rashid Karame, rejected by Frangieh on May 23 for refusing to pre-commit to using the Army, was designated Prime Minister on May 27 on effectively his own terms. The week of cabinet formation was interrupted by armed leftist rocket and mortar attacks on Christian Ashrafiyeh (May 29, 6 dead); an anonymous “U.S. official” Reuters comment comparing the crisis to Jordan 1970 that alarmed Lebanese elites (May 29); approximately 38 kidnappings (May 31); the assassination of Phalange-NLP liaison Naim Bardaqan (June 1) and the consequent Phalange-NLP general strike; the establishment of joint police-PLO patrols in contested Beirut neighborhoods (June 1–3). Two Embassy Damascus cables establish that Syrian presidential advisor Daoudi explicitly denied to the U.S. Embassy that Syria would intervene militarily. Godley’s June 3 retrospective finds that Syria is no longer giving the fedayeen a “blank check” and that Frangieh’s reversal of the military government was driven by domestic opposition rather than by Syrian pressure. Final judgment: Khaddam’s behavior has been “generally constructive” but “final judgments on SARG’s role in Lebanon’s agony may therefore be premature.”

What this document reveals
  • The anonymous ‘U.S. Official’ comment to Reuters on May 29, 1975 compared Lebanon’s crisis to Jordan 1970 and warned that “a single brigade” from Syria could trigger regional conflict. The State Department filed its own cable (STATE125125) the same day documenting the diplomatic backlash. Acting Foreign Minister Dahdah and Saeb Salam contacted the U.S. Embassy seeking clarification.
  • Embassy Damascus cable DAMASC01993 (May 29) records that Syrian presidential advisor Daoudi explicitly told the U.S. Embassy that Syria would NOT intervene militarily “due to Israeli counter-action risk.” At the moment Lebanese elites were reading American signals as threatening intervention if Syria moved militarily, the U.S. and Syrian positions on non-intervention were aligned.
  • Armed leftist groups launched heavy rocket and mortar attacks on the Christian Ashrafiyeh quarter overnight May 28–29. Six killed, fifteen wounded. This was indirect fire on a civilian area, not militarized combat at a flashpoint.
  • Approximately 38 kidnappings documented in Beirut by May 31. The confessional kidnapping pattern that would define the war’s middle years was already emerging in the first weeks.
  • The June 1 assassination of Naim Bardaqan — military aide to former President Chamoun and, per the Embassy, the operational coordinator of ammunition supplies between the Kataeb (Phalange) and the NLP (Chamounist) militias. The Embassy assessed it as premeditated given his specific role. The NLP would now be drawn further into the fighting.
  • Joint police-PLO patrols announced by Vice Premier Musa Kanaan on June 3 establishing buffer zones in contested Beirut neighborhoods. A mechanism that partitioned Lebanese sovereignty with a non-state armed actor in the capital.
  • Syrian Foreign Minister Khaddam’s harsh line with Arafat: “berating him for not cooperating more effectively with GOL and ridiculing him for not being able to restrain all his followers.” Khaddam was equally harsh with local Sunni leaders, accusing them of having “milked Palestinian cause for benefit of their own selfish interests for years.”
  • Embassy’s finding in cable 06966: Frangieh’s reversal of the military government was driven “not so much by SARG as by widespread domestic political opposition and adverse public reaction — both of which Frangieh had grievously misjudged.” Khaddam “merely assisted (albeit considerably) the process whereby this miscalculation was brought home to Frangieh.”
  • Embassy’s final strategic finding: Syria’s worst fear — Israeli military presence on Syria’s western flank — could be realized not through Syrian intervention but through Syrian failure to restrain the PLO in Lebanon. A prediction that would be partially vindicated by Operation Litani (1978) and fully vindicated by the 1982 Israeli invasion.

The Record

The week the state re-formed, and the state fractured

The Rifai military government had collapsed on May 26. Approximately 100 people had died in Beirut in the preceding week. On May 27, President Frangieh designated Rashid Karame — parliament’s original preference, whom Frangieh had rejected four days earlier for refusing to pre-commit to using the Army — as Prime Minister. Fourteen cables across four U.S. diplomatic posts document what happened next.

The Jordan-1970 incident

The most politically consequential development of the week was not, at first glance, a Lebanese event. On May 29, an unnamed “U.S. Official” gave comments to Reuters comparing Lebanon’s crisis to Jordan 1970 — “Black September,” when the Jordanian army expelled armed Palestinian factions from Jordanian territory. The official warned that “a single brigade” from Syria could trigger regional conflict, a formulation Lebanese elites read as implicit American signaling of possible intervention.

Acting Foreign Minister Dahdah and former Prime Minister Saeb Salam contacted the Embassy the same day. That the State Department filed its own cable (STATE125125) documenting the backlash indicates the leak was treated as a diplomatic incident. In the background of these conversations, two Embassy Damascus cables (DAMASC01959 and DAMASC01993) were establishing, for the U.S. government record, that Syrian presidential advisor Daoudi had explicitly told Embassy Damascus Syria would not intervene militarily. The U.S. and Syrian positions on non-intervention were, at this moment, aligned.

Rocket attack on Ashrafiyeh

While the diplomatic geometry of the week played out in telegrams between Beirut, Damascus, and Washington, the ground situation deteriorated. On the night of May 28–29, armed leftist groups launched heavy rocket and mortar attacks on the Christian Ashrafiyeh quarter in East Beirut. The Embassy’s count the next morning: six dead, fifteen wounded. This was not militia combat at a neighborhood flashpoint; this was indirect fire on a defined Christian civilian district in central Beirut. Cabinet formation consultations between Karame, Jumblatt, and the Phalange continued while the rockets were falling.

Kidnappings and the Bardaqan killing

By May 31, the Embassy was reporting approximately 38 kidnappings in Beirut. Confessional hostage-taking, the operational pattern that would define the civil war’s middle years, was already emerging in its first weeks. Then, on June 1, Naim Bardaqan — military aide to former President Camille Chamoun, and, per the Embassy, the operational coordinator of ammunition supplies between the Kataeb and the NLP militias — was assassinated.

The Phalange and the NLP responded by declaring and enforcing a general strike on June 2. Christian-dominated areas of Beirut became, as cable 06917 put it, impassable. The Embassy’s warning in that cable was specific: the assassination could polarize previously neutral political figures and draw the NLP (the “Chamounists”) further into the fighting. The warning proved accurate. The NLP would be a full coalition partner with the Phalange as the war continued.

Joint police-PLO patrols: the mechanism that defined Beirut

Karame’s operational response to the continuing violence was novel. On June 3, Vice Premier Musa Kanaan announced that joint police-PLO patrols had begun establishing buffer zones in contested Beirut neighborhoods, with armed factions withdrawing from both sides. Violence did measurably reduce in the following days. The mechanism was an explicit acknowledgment that the PLO was now a de facto partner in the Lebanese state’s law-and-order apparatus in its own capital. The operational gain was real; the sovereignty cost was larger than the specific arrangement.

Godley’s retrospective

The closing cable of the cluster — 1975BEIRUT06966, CONFIDENTIAL LIMDIS, filed June 3 — is Ambassador Godley’s retrospective on the week. It contains four findings that revise the standard narrative of Syrian mediation in May 1975.

First, Frangieh’s May 26 reversal of the military-government decision was driven “not so much by SARG as by widespread domestic political opposition and adverse public reaction.” Khaddam assisted that process “considerably” but did not drive it. Frangieh had “grievously misjudged” his own country’s political tolerance for military rule.

Second, Khaddam was not, in Damascus’s own telling, softly delivering Syrian support to any Lebanese faction. He was “particularly harsh” with Arafat — “berating him for not cooperating more effectively with GOL and ridiculing him for not being able to restrain all his followers.” He was “scathing” with local Sunni Moslem leaders, accusing them of having “milked Palestinian cause for benefit of their own selfish interests for years.” He warned Jumblatt and the leftists that Syria would not take sides in their vendetta against the Phalange.

Third, Syria was — in the Embassy’s reading of the week’s traffic — no longer giving the PLO a “blank check.” The fedayeen’s increasingly unbridled behavior in Lebanon was, Damascus had concluded, endangering Syria’s interest in maintaining a stable neighbor on its right flank against Israel.

Fourth, the Embassy’s final strategic finding: if the fedayeen succeeded in rendering the Lebanese state non-sovereign, the Israelis might intervene. Syria’s most feared outcome — Israeli military presence on Syria’s western flank — could be realized not through Syrian intervention in Lebanon, but through Syrian failure to restrain the PLO. That strategic finding, filed on June 3, 1975, would be partially vindicated by Operation Litani (1978) and fully vindicated by the June 1982 Israeli invasion.

What the week leaves on the record

By the evening of June 3, Lebanon had a designated Prime Minister (Karame), a set of joint police-PLO patrols, a fragile cabinet formation process, and a marginally improved security situation. The May round was behind it; the summer of 1975 lay ahead. These fourteen cables are the diplomatic record of the week in which a state attempted to re-form itself — partitioning sovereignty with a non-state armed actor to do so — while an anonymous U.S. comment to Reuters briefly threatened to ignite the crisis into a regional one, and while a targeted assassination drew the principal Christian militia further in.

The Week After
Eight days of fighting had just killed ~100 people. The military cabinet Frangieh tried to substitute for parliamentary choice had collapsed in three days. On May 27, Frangieh designated Rashid Karame — parliament’s original preference — as Prime Minister. The arc documented in these fourteen cables covers the week that followed: Karame’s attempts at cabinet formation, an unattributed “U.S. Official” comment to Reuters that threatened to ignite Lebanese fears of foreign intervention, the Syrian-Lebanese-U.S. triangular diplomacy playing out in real time between Beirut and Damascus, the June 1 assassination of a Phalange-NLP liaison that briefly reopened the fighting, and the gradual establishment of joint police–PLO patrols that defined Beirut’s first “green line” buffer zones.[1]

1. Karame Designated (May 27–28): Cables 06698, 06751
With the Rifai military government gone and fighting ebbing from the May 20–27 peak, Frangieh on May 27 designated Rashid Karame as Prime Minister to form a “national reconciliation” cabinet. Karame had been parliament’s clear preference a week earlier; Frangieh’s refusal to appoint him then had required a military cabinet and its three-day collapse. Now, with no commitment from Karame on pre-deploying the Army, Frangieh accepted what he had rejected a week before. Heavy fighting briefly resumed May 27 overnight but became sporadic by morning.[2]

2. Ashrafiyeh Rocket Attacks (May 29): Cable 06779
Most of Beirut was relatively calm. But armed leftist groups launched heavy rocket and mortar attacks on the Christian Ashrafiyeh quarter overnight. The Embassy’s count: six dead, fifteen wounded. This was not skirmishing at a neighborhood flashpoint; this was indirect fire on a defined Christian civilian area in central Beirut. Cabinet formation consultations continued between Karame and the various factions.[3]

3. The “U.S. Official” Reuters Comment (May 29): Cables 06825, STATE125125
In the middle of the political reconstruction, the diplomacy was interrupted. An unnamed “U.S. official” speaking to Reuters compared Lebanon’s crisis to Jordan 1970 — referring to the Jordanian army’s expulsion of armed Palestinian factions in “Black September.” The official warned that “a single brigade” from Syria could trigger regional conflict. Lebanese officials reading the Reuters wire interpreted this as American signaling of potential intervention if Syria moved militarily.

Acting Foreign Minister Dahdah and former Prime Minister Saeb Salam contacted the Embassy seeking clarification. The State Department’s own cable (STATE125125, May 29) confirmed that the unattributed remarks had sparked concern. The Embassy cable (06825) placed the comment in context: Lebanese elites were reading it both as a warning to Syria and as a signal of possible U.S. military posture.[4]

4. Syrian Mediation as Seen From Damascus (May 27, 29): Cables DAMASC01959, DAMASC01993
Two cables from Embassy Damascus, filed May 27 and May 29, are essential counterpoints to the Beirut reporting. Cable DAMASC01959 analyzes Syria’s motivations: Damascus sought to prevent a situation requiring direct Syrian military intervention while maintaining Palestinian operational freedom in south Lebanon. Cable DAMASC01993 is more specific — Syrian presidential advisor Daoudi explicitly told the Damascus Embassy that Syria would not directly intervene militarily due to Israeli counter-action risk, and that Damascus was balancing its Palestinian and Lebanese relations through consultation with various Lebanese factions.[5]

5. Kidnappings and Escalation (May 30–31): Cables 06840, 06873
The Karame cabinet negotiations bogged down on the composition question: leftists resisted Phalange inclusion; Phalange demanded it. During those days a new pattern emerged: approximately 38 kidnappings documented in Beirut. “Major gun-battle between Phalangist forces and Moslem-leftist factions” spread from the capital to coastal areas. The kidnapping pattern — hostage-taking across confessional lines — would be a defining feature of the civil war’s middle years.[6]

6. Joint Police-PLO Patrols (June 1–3): Cables 06878, 06965
Karame’s response to the continuing violence was operationally novel: joint police-PLO patrols. Vice Premier Musa Kanaan announced on June 3 that joint operations had begun establishing buffer zones in contested areas, with armed factions withdrawing from both sides. The mechanism was an acknowledgment that the PLO was now a de facto partner in Beirut law-and-order policy. The implications for Lebanese sovereignty were significant; the operational value was real, and violence did measurably reduce.[7]

7. The Bardaqan Assassination (June 1–2): Cable 06917
On June 1, Naim Bardaqan was assassinated. Bardaqan was a military aide to former President Camille Chamoun — and, per the Embassy, the operational figure coordinating ammunition supplies between the Kataeb (Phalange) and the NLP (Chamounist) militias. His killing was, the cable assesses, almost certainly targeted and premeditated given his specific role. The Phalange and NLP responded by declaring and enforcing a general strike on June 2, with Christian-dominated areas of Beirut becoming, as the cable put it, impassable. The Embassy’s warning: this could polarize previously neutral political figures and draw the Chamounists further into escalating sectarian violence.[8]

8. The Embassy’s Retrospective on Syria (June 3): Cable 06966 — Anchor
The closing cable of the cluster — 1975BEIRUT06966, CONFIDENTIAL LIMDIS, signed by Godley — synthesizes the Embassy’s view of the preceding week’s Syrian role. Four findings:

First, that Syria’s role “has naturally been self-interested” but has also been “generally constructive in helping GOL gradually to restore some semblance of public order.”

Second, that the reversal of Frangieh’s military-government decision was driven not primarily by Khaddam but by “widespread domestic political opposition and adverse public reaction.” Khaddam “merely assisted (albeit considerably) the process whereby this miscalculation was brought home to Frangieh.”

Third, that Khaddam had been harsh with Arafat — “berating him for not cooperating more effectively with GOL and ridiculing him for not being able to restrain all his followers” — and equally harsh with local Sunni Moslem leaders, accusing them of having “milked Palestinian cause for benefit of their own selfish interests for years.”[9]

Fourth, the most consequential finding: that Syria was no longer giving the fedayeen a “blank check.” Damascus had concluded that increasingly unbridled fedayeen behavior in Lebanon was endangering Syria’s interest in maintaining a stable neighbor on its right flank against Israel. The Embassy’s strategic read: if the fedayeen pushed the GOL to the point where “even pretense of its sovereignty and control over Lebanon is no longer credible,” the Israelis might intervene — and Syria’s fear of Israeli military presence on its western flank could be realized not through Syrian intervention, but through Syrian failure to constrain the PLO.[10]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]This cluster represents a full week of multi-post diplomatic traffic (Beirut, Damascus, State Department) covering Karame’s cabinet formation, the “U.S. Official” Reuters incident, and the establishment of joint police-PLO patrols. It is the highest-density diplomatic period of the civil war’s first two months.
[2]Frangieh’s May 27 designation of Karame — whom he had rejected on May 23 for refusing to pre-commit to using the Army — indicates that the military-cabinet path had been exhausted as a political alternative. Karame now took the premiership on effectively his own terms.
[3]The rocket and mortar attack on Ashrafiyeh on the night of May 28–29 is notable as an instance of indirect fire on a defined Christian civilian quarter, not a militarized flashpoint. The six fatalities occurred among residents rather than combatants. This pattern — indirect fire on civilian areas across confessional lines — would become a feature of the war.
[4]The "Jordan 1970" reference by the anonymous U.S. official alludes to "Black September" — the Jordanian army’s 1970 expulsion of armed Palestinian factions from Jordan. The reference carried specific meaning: if Lebanon became a "second Jordan," then either the Lebanese Army would move against the PLO (as the Jordanian army had) or outside forces would. The comment was sufficient to cause both Salam and Dahdah to contact the Embassy. That State Department filed its own cable (STATE125125) the same day documenting the backlash indicates the leak was treated as a diplomatic incident.
[5]The two Damascus cables (DAMASC01959 and DAMASC01993) together establish something important: that Syrian presidential advisor Daoudi explicitly denied to the U.S. Embassy in Damascus that Syria would intervene militarily. This is on the record in the diplomatic traffic at the moment when Lebanese elites were reading American signals as threatening just such an intervention. The U.S.-Syrian positions on non-intervention were, at this moment, aligned.
[6]The May 31 cable (06873) documents ~38 kidnappings in Beirut — a significant early datapoint. Confessional kidnapping would become one of the defining operational patterns of the civil war. Its emergence in the immediate aftermath of the May round, and in the middle of Karame’s formation effort, indicates the political process had not produced operational restraint.
[7]Joint police-PLO patrols in Beirut were a novel operational mechanism. The Lebanese state effectively deputized the PLO as a partner in law enforcement within its own capital. Vice Premier Musa Kanaan’s June 3 announcement makes the mechanism explicit. The short-term effect was real: violence did reduce. The long-term implication — that Lebanese sovereignty had been formally partitioned with a non-state armed actor — was larger than the specific operational arrangement.
[8]The Bardaqan assassination on June 1, 1975 is significant specifically because Bardaqan's operational role was coordinating ammunition supplies between the Phalange and the NLP Tigers. Taking him out disrupted that coordination and, per the Embassy, threatened to draw former President Chamoun’s NLP further into the fighting. The Phalange-NLP joint strike on June 2 is the visible consequence. The Embassy’s warning — that this could polarize previously neutral figures — was accurate: the NLP would become a full coalition partner with the Phalange as the war continued.
[9]Khaddam’s May 25 meeting with local Sunni Muslim leaders, as reported by the Embassy on June 3, is a remarkable diplomatic datapoint. Accusing Lebanese Sunni political leaders of having "milked Palestinian cause for benefit of their own selfish interests for years" is not the language of a state providing political cover. It is the language of a state signaling that a client relationship has ended. Lebanon’s Sunni leaders, the cable implies, could no longer count on reflexive Syrian support in their disputes with the Christian establishment.
[10]The Embassy’s final strategic finding in cable 06966 — that Syrian failure to constrain the PLO could produce exactly the Israeli military presence on Syria’s western flank that Syria most feared — is a remarkable piece of strategic analysis. It frames the 1975 Lebanese crisis as a test of Syrian statecraft, not merely a Lebanese internal dispute. The prediction would be partially vindicated by the 1978 Operation Litani and fully vindicated by the 1982 Israeli invasion.
