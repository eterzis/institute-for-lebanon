Beirut, April 30 – May 16, 1975

The Interregnum: Sadr’s Parallel Government

Four cables spanning April 30 – May 16, 1975 document the seventeen days between the April 16 ceasefire and the second round of Beirut fighting. Two parallel structures operate simultaneously: the Solh government, which the Embassy judges produces nothing; and the Shiite-led “National Committee for Public Pacification” convened by Imam Musa Sadr, which the Embassy regards as the “only real efforts thus far which show promise.” By May 16 the Solh cabinet has collapsed and Sadr’s initiative has stalled.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT05611_05753_05913_06284 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

Four U.S. Embassy cables document the parallel processes by which Lebanon attempted, unsuccessfully, to stabilize itself in the seventeen days between the April 16 ceasefire and the outbreak of the second round of fighting on May 20. The Solh government’s official consultations produced, in the Embassy’s assessment, nothing. A joint GOL–PLO committee announced “practical steps” without specifics. Meanwhile Imam Musa Sadr — acting independently of the state — convened a 77-person “Conference of National Reconciliation” on April 19 and established a 14-member “National Committee for Public Pacification” charged with drafting a new National Charter. President Frangieh blessed the initiative; Arafat endorsed it before leaving for Damascus and Moscow; even Pierre Gemayel reacted favorably. The Embassy’s judgment: “timely, constructive and potentially significant” — but likely to end as “pious expression of well-meaning but vague and unrealizable ‘principles.’” On May 8 six Phalangist and allied ministers resigned in a coordinated action the Embassy reads as orchestrated in advance by Frangieh and Gemayel. On May 16 Solh formally resigned, with the Embassy sharply critical of his delay.

The Record

Two parallel structures, one collapse

Between the April 16 ceasefire and the outbreak of Beirut’s second round on May 20, seventeen days passed. During those days, two parallel processes ran in Lebanon, each claiming to address the same crisis, and each documented in the Embassy’s cables.

The first process was official. Prime Minister Rashid Solh and President Suleiman Frangieh conducted rounds of consultation with fedayeen leaders, Phalangist leaders, and representatives of other Lebanese political groupings. On May 3, a joint GOL–PLO committee met with military and PLO representatives and announced “practical steps,” unspecified. In his public statements, Solh characterized the state of Lebanese–fedayeen relations as “excellent.”

The Embassy’s judgment of this official process, in Ambassador Godley’s own phrase, was that it had produced “no agreement on concrete or practical measures.”

The second process was private. On April 19, Shiite Imam Musa Sadr — acting independently of the state — convened a “Conference of National Reconciliation” attended by 77 notables from across Lebanon’s confessional lines. From the conference emerged a 14-member “National Committee for Public Pacification” under Sadr’s leadership. Its charge: draft a proposed new “National Charter” covering three distinct areas — public order, a new accord governing Lebanese–Palestinian relations, and socio-economic reform for Lebanon’s underprivileged populations.

Frangieh gave the initiative his blessing. Arafat, before departing for Damascus and Moscow, assured Sadr of PLO support. Phalange leader Pierre Gemayel reacted favorably. The Embassy’s April 30 assessment: “the only real efforts thus far which show promise of injecting new elements of much-needed stability into Lebanese–Palestinian relations.”

But the Embassy also predicted the initiative’s fate. The proposed Charter, Godley wrote, was “not likely to replace” the 1969 Cairo Agreement but “may take form of protocol or some other ‘add-on’ to it.” And: “there is every possibility that Imam Sadr’s ‘Charter’ — like the Cairo Agreement — may never progress beyond stage of pious expression of well-meaning but vague and unrealizable ‘principles.’” The Embassy further worried that by lumping socio-economic reform into the Charter, Sadr might be “biting off more than he, GOL and Lebanese body politic can chew.”

May 8: the cabinet breaks

The Solh cabinet convened for the first time since the April 17 ceasefire on May 8. Before the meeting ended, six Phalangist and allied ministers had submitted resignations to President Frangieh. A seventh, from the Armenian Tashnag party, signaled oral resignation intent.

The Embassy’s reading of this event, documented in cable 1975BEIRUT05913, was not that the ministers had independently reached the same conclusion. It was that Frangieh and Pierre Gemayel had orchestrated the walkout in advance. The six ministers walked out simultaneously; the political geometry was too clean to be accidental.

Solh, who had publicly assured the country as recently as May 5 that no confrontation was occurring, was contradicted within hours of the cabinet’s opening.

May 16: Solh resigns

Eight days later only four of twenty ministers remained. Solh delivered a long parliamentary statement — blaming the Phalange, endorsing leftist-Palestinian demands for systemic reform, defending his tenure — and then, formally, resigned. The Embassy’s cable 1975BEIRUT06284 is sharply critical. Solh’s “refusal to play by the rules” of Lebanese political practice — the expected norm of resigning promptly once a cabinet has functionally collapsed — had, in the Embassy’s judgment, actively worsened confessional tensions at a critical moment. Frangieh accepted the resignation with criticism. He directed Solh to continue as caretaker.

Four days later the second round of Beirut fighting would begin.

What the interregnum documents

These four cables, read together, document something the standard narrative of the Lebanese Civil War’s opening does not give much attention to: that for the five weeks after April 13, the Lebanese state was not the only structure attempting to handle the Palestinian question. A parallel, unofficial, Shiite-led body had the explicit blessing of the President, the PLO Chairman, and the Phalange leader — and was, in the U.S. Embassy’s written assessment, the only effort that showed promise.

That structure went nowhere. The cabinet collapsed under it on May 8. The prime minister fell from under it on May 16. Four days after that, the second round of Beirut fighting would make any reconciliation charter moot. Three years later the cleric who had convened the process would disappear in Libya.

The record of what was tried, and by whom, is in these four cables.

The Interregnum
Between the April 16 ceasefire and the second round of Beirut fighting that would begin on May 20, seventeen days passed. During those days, two parallel processes ran in contradiction. In one, the Government of Lebanon’s “consultation” led by Prime Minister Rashid Solh and President Suleiman Frangieh produced, in the Embassy’s assessment, nothing. In the other, a private initiative led by the Shiite cleric Imam Musa Sadr — a “Conference of National Reconciliation” of 77 notables convened on April 19, followed by a 14-member “National Committee for Public Pacification” charged with drafting a new “National Charter” — offered what Ambassador Godley called the “only real efforts thus far which show promise.” The parallel structure would collapse alongside the Solh cabinet by May 16, when the Prime Minister was forced from office.[1]

1. Sadr’s National Charter (Cable 05611, Apr 30, 1975)
Imam Musa Sadr’s “Conference of National Reconciliation” on April 19, 1975 convened 77 notables — in the Embassy’s phrase, “from virtually all segments of Lebanese political, economic, religious and professional life” — to address the post-ceasefire crisis. From this conference a 14-member “National Committee for Public Pacification” was formed under Sadr’s leadership. Its task: draft a new National Charter addressing (a) public order, (b) a new Lebanese–Palestinian accord, and (c) socio-economic reform.[2]

President Frangieh gave the initiative his blessing. Yasser Arafat, before departing for Damascus and Moscow, assured Sadr of his support. Phalange leader Pierre Gemayel reacted favorably. The Embassy’s judgment: “timely, constructive and potentially significant,” but likely to end as a “pious expression of well-meaning but vague and unrealizable ‘principles’” — like the 1969 Cairo Agreement itself.[3]

2. The GOL–PLO Joint Committee (Cable 05753, May 5, 1975)
Running in parallel to Sadr’s private initiative, the government’s official response took the form of a joint Lebanese–Palestinian committee that met on May 3 with military and PLO representatives. Prime Minister Solh described relations as “excellent” — a characterization the Embassy received with open skepticism. The committee announced “practical steps” to prevent renewed clashes, without specifics. The Embassy’s judgment: underlying grievances would persist; recent Phalangist–fedayeen fighting could resurface “in more acute form despite temporary diplomatic gestures.”[4]

3. The Cabinet Breaks (Cable 05913, May 8, 1975)
Three days after the joint committee’s meeting, the Solh cabinet convened for its first full meeting since the April 17 ceasefire. Six Phalangist and allied ministers submitted resignations to President Frangieh. A seventh, from the Armenian Tashnag party, signaled oral resignation intent. The Embassy’s reading: Frangieh and Pierre Gemayel had coordinated the move in advance — a pre-orchestrated collapse of Solh’s government. Solh’s public assurances that no confrontation had occurred were contradicted within hours of their being given.[5]

4. Solh’s Resignation (Cable 06284, May 16, 1975)
By mid-May only four ministers remained. Solh, whom the Embassy’s April 26 strategic assessment (cable 1975BEIRUT05387) had already judged “discredited beyond repair,” delivered a lengthy parliamentary statement justifying his tenure, blaming the Phalangist Party, and endorsing leftist–Palestinian demands for systemic reform. He then submitted his resignation to Frangieh. The Embassy’s judgment: Solh’s “refusal to play by the rules” — in particular his delay in resigning after the cabinet’s functional collapse — had actively worsened confessional tensions.[6] Frangieh accepted with criticism and directed Solh to continue as caretaker.

What the Interregnum Reveals
These four cables together document something the standard civil-war narrative does not fully address: that in the five weeks after the April 13 bus attack, two parallel structures attempted to handle the Lebanese–Palestinian crisis. The official state process — cabinet consultations, presidential meetings, a joint GOL-PLO committee — produced, in the Embassy’s own characterization, nothing concrete. The unofficial Shiite-led process — a 77-person reconciliation conference, a 14-member committee drafting a new National Charter — operated with the blessing of the President, the PLO Chairman, and the Phalange leader, and was the only effort the Embassy regarded as “potentially significant.”

Three years later Musa Sadr would disappear in Libya. The structure he tried to build in April and May 1975 would be one of the last serious attempts by a non-governmental Lebanese figure to mediate the Palestinian question before the war consumed everything. When the state collapsed on May 20, the National Committee for Public Pacification went with it.

GODLEY

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The four cables in this merged cluster span the 17-day interregnum between the April 16 ceasefire and the outbreak of Beirut’s second round of fighting on May 20. They are presented as a single narrative because the Sadr initiative and the Solh cabinet’s collapse are not separable events — they are the two arcs of the same 17-day interregnum.
[2]The “Conference of National Reconciliation” convened by Sadr on April 19, 1975, has received relatively little attention in Anglophone scholarship on the civil war’s opening. Its 77 participants cut across Lebanon’s confessional lines; its 14-member executive drafted a proposed “National Charter” that would have amended or added to the 1969 Cairo Agreement framework. The Embassy’s description of Sadr’s initiative as “the only real efforts thus far which show promise” is a notable contemporaneous judgment.
[3]The parallel with the 1969 Cairo Agreement is Ambassador Godley’s explicit framing: the Embassy predicts that Sadr’s Charter, if completed and endorsed, would be as hollow in implementation as Cairo had been. The 1969 Cairo Agreement had given the PLO the right to maintain armed forces in Lebanon; by 1975 the Embassy regarded its constraints as “largely irrelevant to current state of GOL-fedayeen relations.” A new agreement, the Embassy implies, would share that fate.
[4]Prime Minister Solh’s characterization of Lebanese–fedayeen relations as “excellent” on May 5, 1975 — seventeen days after three dozen people had been killed in three days of urban combat — is documented in cable 1975BEIRUT05753. The Embassy’s skepticism was precise: it did not believe the crisis had been resolved. Fifteen days after this cable, the second round of fighting would kill more than a hundred.
[5]The Embassy’s identification of Frangieh–Gemayel pre-coordination of the May 8 ministerial resignations is significant. The resignations ended the public pretense that the Solh government was functioning. Six Phalangist and allied ministers walking out simultaneously was not a spontaneous political event; it was, per the Embassy, a coordinated act.
[6]The Embassy’s closing judgment on Solh is sharp: “his refusal to play by the rules” of Lebanese political practice — meaning the expected norm of resigning promptly once a cabinet has functionally collapsed — “exacerbated sectarian tensions at a critical moment.” Four days after Solh’s May 16 resignation, the second round of fighting would begin.
