Beirut, May 20 – 27, 1975

Beirut’s Second Battle: The May Round and Rifai’s 72-Hour Military Government

Eight cables spanning May 22–27, 1975 document the eight-day second round of Phalangist–fedayeen fighting that killed approximately 100 people and wounded 300. President Frangieh rejected parliament’s choice of Rashid Karame as Prime Minister because Karame would not pre-commit to using the Army, and instead appointed a military government under General Nureddin Rifai. The Phalange stopped fighting the moment the military cabinet was announced. Under combined Syrian pressure and Muslim, leftist, and — crucially — Christian opposition, the Rifai government resigned after three days. Karame was designated Prime Minister by May 27.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT06591_06698 SECRET → UNCLASSIFIED

The second round of Phalangist–fedayeen fighting erupted on May 20, 1975, four days after Rashid Solh’s resignation. Over eight days the fighting killed ~100 people and wounded 300, saw five ceasefires negotiated and broken, triggered a constitutional crisis in which President Frangieh appointed Lebanon’s first military government in decades under General Nureddin Rifai, and watched that military government collapse in three days under combined Syrian and domestic opposition. The Embassy’s SECRET cable 1975BEIRUT06670 documents what actually happened: parliamentary deputies favored Rashid Karame as Prime Minister (21 explicit, 9 with alternative, 40 deferring to the President); Frangieh rejected Karame because Karame would not pre-commit to using the Army; Frangieh then — per the Embassy’s informant — appointed the military cabinet without broader consultation. The Phalange stopped fighting the moment the military government was announced; main Christian quarters fired shots in the air in approval. Muslim, leftist, and many Christian opponents of the military government forced Frangieh to reverse. Karame was designated Prime Minister by May 27. Among snipers arrested at the end of the round: members of the Communist Party and the Syrian Popular Party (PPS). The round proved April 13 had not been an isolated event.

What this document reveals
  • Approximately 100 dead and 300 wounded in Beirut between May 20 and May 27, 1975. The Embassy’s contemporaneous count from cable 06698.
  • Lebanon’s first military government in decades, formed May 23 by President Frangieh under General Nureddin Rifai, resigned after three days. Per cable 06670, Frangieh bypassed parliament’s clear preference for Karame (21 explicit + 9 conditional + 40 deferrals) because Karame would not pre-commit to using the Army. No Sunni leader would have made that commitment.
  • The Phalange stopped fighting the moment the military government was announced. Main Christian quarters in Beirut fired shots in the air in approval. This sequence — hostilities continuing until the military cabinet was formed, then ceasing — convinced Lebanon’s Muslim political class that Frangieh and Pierre Gemayel had coordinated the outcome.
  • Arafat convened fedayeen leaders on May 21 and proposed Fatah command over all Palestinian armed groups in Beirut. Other factions initially resisted. The PLO was not a unitary actor in May 1975; Arafat had to negotiate command, not exercise it.
  • Five ceasefires were negotiated and broken in six days. The May 26 SITREP described overnight firing as the “heaviest and most widespread” of the crisis.
  • Libyan and Iraqi external actors identified as attempting to escalate tensions on May 22. Consistent with the April 26 strategic assessment (cable 1975BEIRUT05387) that documented the Libyan Embassy’s $6M/month payroll to Lebanese dissidents.
  • Among snipers arrested May 27: members of the Communist Party and the Syrian Popular Party (PPS). PPS operational involvement had been documented in the April round as well.
  • Syrian Foreign Minister Khaddam’s mediation was the visible factor that produced Rifai’s resignation, but cable 1975BEIRUT06966 (June 3) judges the reversal was “dictated not so much by SARG as by widespread domestic political opposition and adverse public reaction.”

The Record

Eight days that ended with a military government

On the morning of May 20, 1975 — four days after Prime Minister Rashid Solh’s resignation — fighting erupted around the Tel Zaatar refugee camp and the adjacent Beirut neighborhood of Dekwaneh. Over the next eight days, the Embassy would file eight cables documenting what it called the “heaviest and most widespread firing of the current crisis.” Approximately 100 people would die. Some 300 would be wounded. Five successive ceasefires would be negotiated and broken. And for 72 hours in the middle of the eight-day arc, Lebanon would be governed by its first military cabinet in decades — a cabinet the Phalange immediately welcomed, the Muslim political class rejected as unconstitutional, and domestic opposition (combined with Syrian mediation) forced to resign on the third day of its existence.

This cluster of eight cables is the Embassy’s real-time record of the round that proved April 13 was not an isolated incident.

The parliamentary preference that Frangieh refused

The single most analytically important cable in the cluster is 1975BEIRUT06670, filed May 25, classified SECRET, sent IMMEDIATE. A source the Embassy describes as a “close confidant” of President Frangieh — one who nonetheless opposed the military-government decision — explained to the Deputy Chief of Mission exactly what had happened inside the Baabda Palace over the preceding weekend.

Speaker of Parliament Kamal As’ad, believing he had Frangieh’s blessing to canvass deputies for prime-ministerial preferences, polled them. The count: 21 explicitly for Rashid Karame; 9 for Karame with an alternative; 40 deferring to the President’s choice. In ordinary Lebanese political practice, that count would have produced a Karame premiership. Frangieh said as much to a friend of Karame’s.

But Frangieh conditioned his appointment on a commitment Karame would not make: that the new Prime Minister would commit in advance to using the Lebanese Army to quell the fighting. Karame, in the cable’s phrasing, “quite understandably” responded that “no head of government could give such a commitment because the use of the Army would obviously depend on the circumstances.” No Sunni leader would have made that commitment; Frangieh, concluding “there was no political alternative,” appointed the military cabinet instead — without, the cable notes, “much consultation with others.”

What the Muslim political class believed had happened

Muslim political leaders believed Frangieh had been waiting for this opportunity since Pierre Gemayel began his public campaign against the fedayeen. The Embassy’s informant disputed that reading; the Embassy itself acknowledged the reading was hard to refute given the circumstantial evidence. Two facts in particular:

First, the Phalange stopped fighting the moment the military cabinet was announced. If the Phalange had been pursuing some objective in the fighting that ended upon announcement of the military cabinet, then by inference the military cabinet was that objective — or at least a sufficient condition for cessation.

Second, main Christian quarters of Beirut fired shots in the air in approval when the military cabinet was announced. The symbolism was unmistakable.

The Embassy’s own retrospective judgment, in cable 1975BEIRUT06966 filed June 3, was that Frangieh had “grievously misjudged” the domestic reaction. Syrian Foreign Minister Khaddam’s intervention — sometimes credited with forcing the reversal — had “merely assisted (albeit considerably) the process whereby this miscalculation was brought home to Frangieh.” The military government, the Embassy wrote, could not have significantly altered the balance of force between the GOL and the fedayeen; the notion that it might have imposed greater restrictions on the fedayeen struck the Embassy, in retrospect, as “highly academic.”

The PLO was not a unitary actor

Cable 06611, filed May 23, records a fact that sits uneasily with most narratives of the PLO in Lebanon. Under pressure from Egyptian President Sadat and other Arab leaders, Yasser Arafat on May 21 convened the Palestinian faction leaders and proposed that Fatah assume military command of all fedayeen groups in Beirut. Other Palestinian factions — the PFLP, Sa’iqa, the Arab Liberation Front, and others — initially resisted.

Arafat had to negotiate command. He did not exercise it. That the Chairman of the PLO had to attempt, mid-crisis, to consolidate operational control of Palestinian armed groups in Lebanon’s capital — and that his attempt met resistance — is a critical piece of context for understanding the fighting.

Foreign actors, again

The May 22 cable (06591) identifies Libyan and Iraqi actors as attempting to escalate tensions. The identification is consistent with cable 1975BEIRUT05387 (April 26), which had documented the Libyan Embassy’s ~$6 million-per-month payroll to dissident Lebanese factions. When snipers were arrested in Beirut at the end of the round, the Embassy noted (cable 06698) that they included members of the Communist Party and the Syrian Popular Party (PPS). The PPS had been documented as participants in the April fighting. Its continued operational presence in May is on the record in these cables.

The count

By noon May 27, the Embassy’s eight-day accounting stood at approximately 100 dead and 300 wounded. The Rifai government had fallen. Parliament’s original choice — Rashid Karame — was designated Prime Minister the following day. The Lebanese state had survived an eight-day armed political crisis at a cost that would have been unimaginable two months earlier.

That this round had been followed the same week by a military government, and the week after by its collapse, and the week after that by a new civilian cabinet, suggested to the Embassy that Lebanon’s political class retained a capacity to muddle through. The fighting that would resume in the summer and autumn would test that capacity to its breaking point.

The record of eight days in May is in these cables.

The Second Battle
On the morning of May 20, 1975 — four days after Prime Minister Rashid Solh’s resignation — the second round of Phalangist–fedayeen fighting erupted around Beirut’s Dekwaneh neighborhood and the Tel Zaatar refugee camp. Over the next eight days Beirut would see what the Embassy called the “heaviest and most widespread firing of the current crisis.” Approximately 100 people would die and 300 be wounded. Lebanon’s parliament, unable to name a new civilian prime minister, would watch President Frangieh appoint a military government under General Nureddin Rifai on May 23. That military government would resign after three days under combined Syrian and domestic pressure — the shortest-lived cabinet in modern Lebanese history — and Rashid Karame would be designated Prime Minister by May 27. April 13 had not been an isolated incident. May 20 proved it.[1]

1. Opening Round (May 20–22): Cable 06591
The fighting began around Dekwaneh and the Tel Zaatar refugee camp, south of Beirut’s city center. In the 5-hour window 0100–0600 local on May 22 alone, the Embassy recorded 10 killed and 52 wounded in the vicinity of Tel Zaatar. Palestinian forces deployed mortars and Soviet-manufactured rockets — heavier weaponry than had been used in April. Saeb Salam and Imam Musa Sadr attempted local ceasefires that did not hold. The Embassy’s contemporaneous observation: the major PLO factions had not yet fully engaged, but Iraqi and Libyan external actors were attempting to escalate tensions.[2]

2. Fatah Takes Command (May 23): Cable 06611
By the fourth day the fighting had spread across Beirut neighborhoods. Lebanese security forces attempting to mediate drew fire from both sides; four gendarmes were wounded. On May 21, under pressure from Arab leaders including Egyptian President Sadat, Arafat convened the fedayeen leaders and proposed that Fatah assume military command of all Palestinian armed groups in Beirut — a consolidation that other Palestinian factions initially resisted. The Embassy classified the situation as “confused and extremely unpredictable.”[3]

3. The Military Government (May 23–24): Cable 06652
On May 23 — seven days after Solh’s resignation, with fighting at its peak — President Frangieh abandoned the civilian track and appointed a military government under General Nureddin Rifai. Hostilities in Beirut significantly decreased within hours of the announcement. Main Christian quarters fired shots in the air in approval. The Phalange stopped fighting. Sunni political leaders, leftists, and Palestinians condemned the move as undemocratic. A nationwide strike was called for May 24 — complete shutdowns in Tripoli and Sidon; partial effectiveness in Beirut’s Muslim quarters.[4]

4. Why Frangieh Bypassed Karame (May 25): Cable 06670 — SECRET
The analytically critical cable in this cluster was classified SECRET and sent IMMEDIATE. A confidant of President Frangieh — the Embassy describes him as a “close confidant” who nonetheless opposed the military government — explained to the Deputy Chief of Mission on May 25 what had really happened.

Parliamentary Speaker Kamal As’ad had polled deputies: 21 preferred Rashid Karame as Prime Minister, 9 preferred Karame with an alternative, and 40 deferred to the President. Frangieh told a friend of Karame’s that under normal circumstances he would appoint Karame on those numbers. But Karame — like any Sunni leader — could not commit to using the Army to quell the fighting, and Frangieh would not appoint any Prime Minister unwilling to make that commitment.[5]

Karame’s own response, as recorded in the cable, was that “no head of government could give such a commitment because the use of the Army would obviously depend on the circumstances.” Frangieh, concluding “there was no political alternative,” appointed the military cabinet instead. The Muslim political class, according to the informant, believed Frangieh had been waiting for an opportunity to impose military rule since Pierre Gemayel “began his campaign against the fedayeen.” The Embassy’s informant disputed that reading; the cable notes the difficulty of refuting it given the circumstantial evidence — particularly that the Phalange stopped fighting the moment the military cabinet was announced.

5. Fighting Intensifies (May 25–26): Cables 06668 & 06678
Despite the military government, fighting intensified rather than diminished. The May 25 SITREP: 50 dead since Tuesday. The May 26 SITREP: 80 dead; overnight firing described as the “heaviest and most widespread” of the crisis; a fifth ceasefire agreement was negotiated but failed to take hold. Residents could not move freely through their own city.[6]

6. The Military Government Falls (May 26): Cable 06684
Three days after its formation, under combined Syrian pressure (transmitted through Foreign Minister Khaddam) and domestic opposition, Rifai’s military cabinet resigned. Frangieh directed them to stay as caretakers. The Embassy’s read: appointment of Rashid Karame — or another civilian Sunni candidate — was now imminent. The constitutional experiment was over. Lebanon had spent approximately 72 hours with a military government; the system had rejected it.[7]

7. The Accounting (May 27): Cable 06698
By noon May 27 violence had reduced. The Embassy’s body count over the eight-day round: approximately 100 dead, 300 wounded. The nationwide general strike was suspended. Barricades came down. Security forces arrested multiple snipers, including Communist and PPS (Syrian Popular Party) members.[8] Airport traffic remained open throughout the crisis. Frangieh was expected to announce Karame’s appointment shortly.

Four days later, the Bardaqan assassination would reopen the Phalange-NLP-Palestinian front. The May round was over; the war was not.

GODLEY

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The May 20–27, 1975 round is often compressed in Anglophone narratives into a one-line mention that fighting "resumed" after April 13. These eight cables document the round with specificity: duration (eight days), casualties (~100 dead, 300 wounded), constitutional consequence (first military government in decades, then its collapse in three days), and the regional-mediator geometry (Sadat, Khaddam). This cluster is the Embassy’s real-time record of the fighting that proved April 13 was not a discrete event.
[2]The Embassy’s May 22 identification of Libyan and Iraqi external actors as attempting to escalate is consistent with cable 1975BEIRUT05387 (April 26), which documented the Libyan Embassy’s ~$6M/month payroll to dissident Lebanese factions. The pattern repeats in May.
[3]Arafat’s May 21 convocation of the fedayeen leaders, under explicit Arab-state pressure, and his attempt to impose Fatah command over all Palestinian armed groups in Beirut is a critical datapoint. It contradicts the narrative in which the PLO functioned as a disciplined unitary actor. Other Palestinian factions — PFLP, Sa’iqa, ALF, and others — were sufficiently independent that Arafat had to negotiate command rather than exercise it.
[4]The May 23 Rifai military-government appointment was the first time since before the 1958 crisis that Lebanon had a non-civilian cabinet. That the Phalange immediately ceased fighting once it was announced was read by Lebanon’s Muslim political class (per cable 06670) as evidence that the military-government move was what the Phalange had been seeking since the April 13 clashes began.
[5]Frangieh’s refusal to appoint Karame because Karame would not pre-commit to using the Army is the heart of the constitutional failure. Deputy counts clearly favored Karame. The traditional Lebanese political norm would have been to appoint the candidate with parliamentary support. Frangieh’s substitution of a military cabinet for the parliamentary choice — without broader consultation — was, per cable 06670’s informant, what “infuriated the Muslims.” See also cable 1975BEIRUT06966 for the Embassy’s later retrospective that Frangieh had “grievously misjudged” the domestic reaction.
[6]Five ceasefires negotiated and broken in six days is itself a structural datapoint. The Embassy’s characterization that residents “could not move freely throughout Beirut” reflects that the city had become a patchwork of militia-controlled neighborhoods — a configuration that would define Beirut for the next fifteen years.
[7]The Rifai cabinet’s three-day lifespan was one of the shortest in modern Lebanese history. The Embassy’s cable 1975BEIRUT06966 (June 3) attributes the reversal not primarily to Syrian Foreign Minister Khaddam’s mediation but to “widespread domestic political opposition and adverse public reaction—both of which Frangie had grievously misjudged to his own and GOL’s disadvantage in appointing military cabinet in first place.”
[8]Among the snipers arrested in Beirut at the end of the May round were members of the Communist Party and of the Syrian Popular Party (PPS). PPS participation in the April fighting was documented in cable 1975BEIRUT05387; its continued presence in the May round indicates sustained operational involvement by the Syrian-linked party across the opening months of the civil war.
