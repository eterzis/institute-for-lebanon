Beirut, February 26–27, 1975

The Shot That Began the War

Two cables filed across February 26–27, 1975 document the fishermen&rsquo;s protest in Sidon that ended with Maarouf Saad cut down by Lebanese Army fire, the Army withdrawn to the outskirts of a town now held by fedayeen, and the Embassy predicting &mdash; eight days before it happened &mdash; that Saad&rsquo;s death would produce manifestations that &ldquo;could assume serious proportions.&rdquo;

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT02611_02682 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

On the morning of February 26, 1975, Maarouf Saad &mdash; Sunni Nasserist ex-deputy and longstanding defender of Sidon&rsquo;s small-boat fishermen &mdash; led a demonstration against a fishing concession awarded to a Chamoun-backed industrial trawler company. The protest escalated into armed combat between the Lebanese Army and &ldquo;armed elements&rdquo; &mdash; the diplomatic euphemism for fedayeen &mdash; positioned on rooftops in the old quarter of Sidon. Saad was shot by Army fire; he died eight days later. By the morning of February 27 the Army had withdrawn to the outskirts; Sidon was held by heavily armed fedayeen moving about unmolested; Prime Minister Solh was publicly denying fedayeen involvement while privately negotiating with PLO leaders through the night; and Kamal Jumblatt was trapped in a &ldquo;ticklish position&rdquo; he could not long sustain. The Embassy&rsquo;s prediction &mdash; that Saad&rsquo;s death would produce disturbances of serious proportions &mdash; would be validated seven days later and set the chain of events that produced the civil war.

What this document reveals
  • Maarouf Saad was shot by Lebanese Army fire, not by “crossfire” or unknown attackers. The cable is direct: “Maarouf Saad apparently was cut down by Army fire.” This reading was the immediate operating assumption of U.S. Embassy Beirut on February 26, 1975. The later political narrative that blurred responsibility has no support in the contemporary diplomatic record.
  • The “ruse” hypothesis: The Embassy reports an unconfirmed hypothesis that the fishermen’s demonstration was “a ruse mounted by ‘rejectionist’ fedayeen elements” to draw the Lebanese Army into an unwinnable confrontation and embarrass PLO leadership. The corroborating detail — armed elements pre-positioned on rooftops before the demonstration began — is noted directly. If correct, the Sidon uprising was deliberate political engineering.
  • SAM-7s in the Crusader citadel: The Embassy reports that fedayeen munitions in Sidon’s old quarter included Soviet-made shoulder-fired surface-to-air missiles — “enough... to blow it sky high.” The weapons system that had shot down Israeli aircraft in the 1973 war was in the hands of fedayeen in a Lebanese city in February 1975.
  • The Army withdrawal of February 27 conceded, in practice, that the Lebanese state could not re-enter Sidon without PLO consent. Heavily armed fedayeen moved through the town “unmolested.” This was a sovereignty-transfer event — visible on the record the day it happened.
  • Prime Minister Solh publicly denied fedayeen involvement while privately spending the night negotiating with PLO leaders. The Embassy’s cable records this gap directly, flagging the denial as “belie[d]” by the evidence.
  • The Embassy predicted the March 6 cascade on February 27: “If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions.” Saad died on March 6. The manifestations arrived on schedule. The consequences ran through to April 13.

The Record

The fishermen and the concession

The spark of the 1975 Lebanese Civil War has many candidates in the secondary literature. The Ain al-Rummaneh bus attack of April 13. The Kaslik Conference. The 1969 Cairo Agreement. The entire trajectory of confessional politics since 1943. These U.S. diplomatic cables from late February 1975 suggest a more precise answer: it began on the morning of February 26, in the old port city of Sidon, when fishermen surrounded city hall to protest a fishing concession.

The concession had been granted to a company called Protéine, a modern industrial trawler operation. The company was partly owned by Camille Chamoun, former President of Lebanon and a central figure on the Maronite Christian right. Small-boat fishermen in Sidon — men whose grandfathers had fished the same waters — argued, correctly, that industrial trawlers would rapidly deplete the inshore fish stocks that sustained their livelihoods. The economic injury was real. But the concession carried a meaning that went far beyond fish.

Sidon in 1975 was a Sunni Muslim city in a country whose commercial and political power was concentrated in Christian hands. Its economy was marginal; its young men were migrating to the Gulf or to the Palestinian resistance. When the Solh government awarded a fishing concession that would drive the Sidon fishermen out of business, and when the concession went to a company controlled by one of the most visible Maronite political figures of the era, the grievance was not about fish. The grievance was about a system.

On the morning of February 26, 1975, Maarouf Saad — Sunni Nasserist ex-deputy, founder of the Popular Nasserist Organization, longstanding patron of Sidon’s poor — came out at the head of the fishermen to make the grievance visible. By the end of the morning he had been shot by Lebanese Army fire, the old quarter of Sidon was a battlefield, and the Lebanese state had begun the slow collapse that would produce fifteen years of war.

What happened at city hall

Cable 1975BEIRUT02611, filed at 16:49 GMT on February 26, captures the first day in a form so immediate it reads almost like a news wire. At approximately 0930 local time, demonstrators besieged city hall. Shooting erupted. The Lebanese Army units stationed nearby were ordered to intervene. The melee that followed lasted more than an hour — a running firefight between Army troops, demonstrators, and “armed elements” firing from rooftops in Sidon’s medieval quarter.

The phrase “armed elements” is a diplomatic euphemism the Embassy uses throughout this crisis. It means fedayeen — Palestinian fighters — but the Embassy cannot quite say so directly, because the Lebanese government is publicly committed to the position that the 1969 Cairo Agreement regulates the fedayeen presence and therefore that fedayeen do not fire on the Lebanese Army in Lebanese cities. The cable’s Comment paragraph makes clear what “armed elements” actually are: “fedayeen elements with whom the leftists have a close working relationship.”

At some point during that first hour of melee, Maarouf Saad was shot. The cable reports the fact directly: “Maarouf Saad apparently was cut down by Army fire.” No ambiguity, no alternative attribution. The Army shot him. He was in critical condition in hospital by afternoon. A jeepload of Lebanese soldiers was caught in the subsequent crossfire. Three Lebanese personnel died that morning. Twenty to twenty-five civilians were wounded.

By 1600 local, the Army had sealed Sidon. Roadblocks on every artery into town. Telephone lines cut. UN observers forced to detour and reporting armed fedayeen in the streets and continuing gunfire from the town center. An Embassy officer was clearly still trying to piece the story together when the cable went out less than an hour later.

The “ruse” hypothesis

The Comment paragraph of cable 02611 is one of the most consequential passages in the entire Sidon file. It presents two competing explanations for what had happened. The first is that the Army’s intervention against demonstrators “touched sensitive leftist-fedayeen nerves, with the result that matters got quickly out of hand.” The second, which the Embassy acknowledges as “unconfirmed,” is more disturbing:

“We have picked up an unconfirmed report that the fisherman’s demonstration was only a ruse mounted by ‘rejectionist’ fedayeen elements and their Sidonian leftist allies to draw the Lebanese Army into conflict with fedayeen units in Sidon and elsewhere, and thereby embarrass PLO leadership in its relations with the GOL. One factor which tends to support this theory is evidence that ‘armed elements’ on Sidon’s rooftops may have been positioned there prior to this morning’s demonstration.”

If this is correct — and the corroborating detail about pre-positioned gunmen is not trivial — then the Sidon uprising was not a spontaneous miscarriage of police action. It was deliberate political engineering by Palestinian rejectionist factions (probably PFLP, PFLP-GC, and dissident Sa’iqa elements) seeking to force a confrontation between the Lebanese Army and the broader fedayeen movement. Their objective: embarrass Arafat, break the restraint the PLO mainstream had maintained under the Cairo Agreement framework, and produce the kind of generalized crisis that could collapse the existing Lebanese-Palestinian arrangement.

The cable does not endorse this hypothesis. But it does not dismiss it. It records it as a live possibility on day one, before the historical record had been overwritten by the cascade of events that followed.

SAM-7s in the citadel

The same Comment paragraph contains a parenthetical that should not be read past:

“Fedayeen probably have enough munitions — including SAM-7s at last report — stored in the city’s crumbling Crusader citadel to blow it sky high.”

The Strela-2, NATO designation SA-7 Grail, was a Soviet-made shoulder-fired heat-seeking anti-aircraft missile. It had been introduced to Arab armies in the early 1970s and had shot down Israeli and Egyptian aircraft in the October 1973 war. It was, by 1975, a strategic-level weapons system. And the Embassy is reporting that fedayeen in Sidon have some, stored in a medieval fortress at the center of a Lebanese city.

The 1974BEIRUT11970 cable in this archive also documents SAM-7s in fedayeen possession in Lebanon. Taken together, the reporting establishes that by early 1975 the Palestinian armed presence in Lebanon had moved beyond small arms and rocket-propelled grenades to include man-portable anti-aircraft missiles capable of threatening Israeli or Lebanese military aviation. This is not a detail. It is a structural fact about the balance of force, and it constrained every subsequent Lebanese and Israeli decision about how to handle Sidon.

The Army withdraws

Cable 1975BEIRUT02682, filed at 16:22 GMT on February 27, documents the second day. It opens with a sentence that — read in retrospect — is one of the decisive passages of Lebanese political history.

“Sidon remained isolated from the rest of Lebanon Feb 27, as GOL — in return for what we understand (but cannot confirm) were PLO assurances that it would act to restore public order in town and bring under control ‘armed elements’ who were involved in yesterday’s shooting incidents — withdrew Lebanese Army units to town’s outskirts.”

The Lebanese state had, in the space of twenty-four hours, gone from sealing Sidon with roadblocks to ceding control of the town’s interior to the PLO. The condition of the withdrawal was a PLO promise to restore order; the Embassy notes it cannot confirm the promise was even made. The practical result was that heavily armed fedayeen moved through the streets of a Lebanese city “unmolested.” Shops were closed. Burning tires were everywhere. Sporadic gunfire continued.

This was a sovereignty-transfer event. Not in law — the Lebanese state retained formal authority over its territory — but in fact. The Army would not re-enter Sidon without PLO consent. When the Army tried to reopen the coastal road on March 1, the fedayeen ambushed them and forced a second withdrawal (see cable 1975BEIRUT02837). When the settlement was finally negotiated on March 12–14, the Lebanese government’s ability to police South Lebanon had been visibly broken (see cables 1975BEIRUT03203 and 03256).

The decision to withdraw was rational in isolation. The alternative was a direct Army assault on the fedayeen-held old quarter, a fight the Army could only win at the cost of massive casualties and the destruction of large parts of a historic Lebanese city. No Lebanese government could survive ordering that fight. The problem is that this calculus was structural, not situational. It meant that every comparable crisis — and there would be many — would end with the same accommodation. Each accommodation left the armed groups stronger and the state weaker. The cumulative effect, over years, was the construction of parallel security structures inside Lebanon that no one now had the power to dismantle.

Solh’s public denial

Prime Minister Rashid Solh responded to the crisis with a public statement declaring that fedayeen “were not involved in any way” in the February 26 shooting. The Embassy’s comment on this statement is drily contemptuous:

“Report contained para 1 above, combined with reports that Solh spent most of last night reasoning with PLO leaders and various leftist figures, would appear to belie this claim.”

The gap between what the Lebanese government said in public and what its senior figures did in private was not new in 1975. It was a structural feature of Lebanese politics — the mechanism by which confessional compromise was sustained. The Cairo Agreement had to be presented as a voluntary arrangement between friendly parties; the fedayeen had to be presented as guests; the Army had to be presented as an impartial national institution; the cabinet had to be presented as acting in confessional balance. Everyone understood these were fictions. The fictions were load-bearing.

What made the Sidon crisis different was that the fictions could no longer be maintained in parallel. Solh could not at the same time deny fedayeen involvement in public and negotiate with the PLO in private about fedayeen behavior. Once the contradiction became visible, the Sunni political street demanded that the government act on the public statement, while the fedayeen presence in Sidon made that impossible. The Prime Minister was trapped between two incompatible realities. He would try to navigate the trap for another ten weeks before resigning on May 15.

Jumblatt’s impossible position

The cable’s most psychologically precise observation concerns Kamal Jumblatt. Jumblatt was, by February 1975, the paramount Druze chief, the leader of Lebanon’s largest leftist political party, the principal interlocutor between the Lebanese left and the Palestinian armed movement, and a minister in a cabinet presided over by a Maronite president whose approach to fedayeen-Army relations he had spent years opposing. The Embassy diagnoses his position in three words:

“Jumblatt finds himself in somewhat ticklish position, sympathizing as he does with leftist-fedayeen positions but being obliged — out of loyalty to Frangie — to defend Army’s Feb 26 intervention and subsequent actions in Sidon.”

The “ticklish position” was not sustainable. Jumblatt could hold it as long as no confrontation forced him to choose. The Sidon crisis was that confrontation. Within weeks he would be publicly leading the Lebanese National Movement — the leftist-Palestinian alliance — against the Frangieh government. Within two years he would be dead on a mountain road in the Chouf, almost certainly killed by Syrian agents after he broke with Damascus over a Syrian-backed partition plan. The entire arc from his “ticklish position” of February 27 to his assassination in March 1977 took twenty-five months.

The prediction

The single most historically specific sentence in the two cables is tucked into a parenthetical Comment in paragraph 2 of 02682:

“If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions.”

Saad died on March 6, 1975, eight days after this cable was filed. The manifestations the Embassy predicted — nationwide general strike, mosque-centered demonstrations, the paralysis of Beirut — arrived on schedule and are documented in the 1975BEIRUT03028 and 1975BEIRUT03064 cables in this archive. The Embassy’s prediction was validated precisely and catastrophically.

The prediction is in the record in U.S. Embassy cable traffic on February 27, 1975. No U.S. or Lebanese policy decision was taken on the basis of it. No diplomatic initiative was launched, no mediation proposed, no de-escalation offered to the Sunni political community before Saad died. The Embassy saw the crisis coming eight days in advance, filed the analysis, and then watched it unfold.

What the cables do not say

Two of the most important facts about the Sidon crisis do not appear in these first two cables. First: Saad’s shooting was not an accident of a crowd-control operation gone wrong. The Army was ordered to intervene against a demonstration in which an ex-parliamentarian was leading fishermen against a government-granted concession. Whatever the proximate mechanism of the shooting — crossfire, targeted fire, confused close-quarters action — the underlying fact is that the Lebanese state used lethal military force against a political protest, and the protest leader was killed. That this was sustainable within the existing political system, that no senior official resigned over it, that the Prime Minister was still in office ten weeks later, is itself a diagnosis of the system’s capacity.

Second: the crisis was not a Sunni-Maronite crisis in February 1975. It was not yet a confessional war. The protest was about a fishing concession; the violence was about the Army’s response; the political argument that followed was about army reform and the rights of the Sunni commercial class. The confessional framing would emerge over the next two weeks and is documented in the 1975BEIRUT03203 cable’s SECRET/LIMDIS analysis. What these first cables show is a state apparatus discovering, in real time, that it did not command a monopoly of legitimate force within its own borders — and that this was about to become a political fact visible to its entire population.

On February 27, 1975, the Embassy predicted that if Maarouf Saad died the manifestations would assume serious proportions. On March 6 he died. The next cables in this archive document what happened next.

Archival Note — Merged Entry

This is a merged archival entry combining two companion cables filed on consecutive days, February 26 and 27, 1975, documenting the outbreak of the Sidon crisis. Cable 1975BEIRUT02611 (261649Z) is the first-day report: a fishermen’s protest against a Chamoun-backed fishing concession escalates into a gun battle in which Maarouf Saad — the Sidonian ex-deputy leading the demonstration — is shot by the Lebanese Army. Cable 1975BEIRUT02682 (271622Z) is the following day’s report: the Army has pulled back, the PLO is in control of the town, Prime Minister Solh is denying fedayeen involvement in public while negotiating with them in private, and the Embassy is already predicting a much larger crisis if Saad dies. The two cables are explicitly cross-referenced: 02682 lists “REF: BEIRUT 2611.” They are archived together because together they document the first 36 hours of the event that would, within seven weeks, produce the Lebanese Civil War.

Cable 1 of 2 — 1975BEIRUT02611 — 261649Z Feb 75

Subject: Violence Erupts in Sidon · LIMITED OFFICIAL USE

1.
On the morning of February 26, a leftist-organized demonstration by fishermen[1] in the southern coastal town of Sidon[2] erupted into a general shooting-match between the Lebanese Army and police on one hand and “armed elements”[3] on the other. Three Lebanese personnel were reportedly killed and four wounded, plus some 20–25 civilians wounded as well. Information we have been able to develop thus far indicates that the fishermen were mobilized by local leftist groups, particularly followers of Sidonian ex-deputy Maarouf Saad,[4] to protest the GOL decision to award a fishing concession to a company backed by former President Camille Chamoun.[5] When demonstrators besieged Sidon’s city hall at approximately 0930 hours local, shooting reportedly erupted, nearby Lebanese Army units were ordered to intervene, and a general melee developed which lasted for over an hour between the Army, demonstrators, and “armed elements” positioned on rooftops in Sidon’s old quarter.[6] Maarouf Saad apparently was cut down by Army fire — he reportedly is in critical condition in hospital — and a jeepload of Lebanese soldiers was reportedly caught in the subsequent crossfire. As of 1600 hours local, the Army had managed to isolate Sidon by means of roadblocks established on arteries leading into town, and telephone communications with the town had been cut. UNTSO and UNRWA personnel who were forced to detour around Sidon at 1500 hours local report large numbers of armed fedayeen in evidence and continuing exchanges of small arms fire audible in the direction of the town’s center.

2.
The above developments have already begun to have repercussions elsewhere in Lebanon. Internal security authorities have privately informed us that shooting incidents have erupted in both Tripoli and Tyre — the latter being the scene of a recent shooting incident between Sa’iqa fedayeen and followers of Mayor Abdul Rahman Khalil (see FBIS JN261011). In addition, we understand that a massive protest demonstration is being organized in Beirut for the morning of February 27.

3. Comment
We have had occasion to report in the past that Sidon, particularly its medieval quarter, is infested not only by armed Lebanese leftist elements (followers of Maarouf Saad) but also by various fedayeen elements with whom the leftists have a close working relationship. Fedayeen probably have enough munitions — including SAM-7s at last report — stored in the city’s crumbling Crusader citadel to blow it sky high.[7] It would appear that the Army’s intervention against demonstrators this morning touched sensitive leftist-fedayeen nerves, with the result that matters got quickly out of hand. On the other hand, we have picked up an unconfirmed report that the fisherman’s demonstration was only a ruse mounted by “rejectionist” fedayeen elements and their Sidonian leftist allies to draw the Lebanese Army into conflict with fedayeen units in Sidon and elsewhere, and thereby embarrass PLO leadership in its relations with the GOL.[8] One factor which tends to support this theory is evidence that “armed elements” on Sidon’s rooftops may have been positioned there prior to this morning’s demonstration.

4.
Will report additional information as it becomes available.

Cable 2 of 2 — 1975BEIRUT02682 — 271622Z Feb 75

Subject: Security Situation in Lebanon Following Sidon Clashes · CONFIDENTIAL · Ref: BEIRUT 2611

1.
Sidon remained isolated from the rest of Lebanon on February 27, as the GOL — in return for what we understand (but cannot confirm) were PLO assurances that it would act to restore public order in the town and bring under control the “armed elements” who were involved in yesterday’s shooting incidents — withdrew Lebanese Army units to the town’s outskirts.[9] Throughout the day, travellers on the main coastal road were being detoured around Sidon and the center of the town was reportedly thronged by heavily armed men (including many fedayeen) who apparently were moving about unmolested. Sporadic firing (probably in the air) could be heard, burning tires were much in evidence, and shops and businesses were completely closed.

2.
Primin Solh,[10] faced with outraged reaction from leftist groups and media charging the GOL with a “premeditated attempt” to “murder” Maarouf Saad and demanding that the Governor of South Lebanon and the Sidon Army commander both be sacked, has publicly declared that fedayeen were not involved in any way in yesterday’s shooting. (Comment: The report contained in paragraph 1 above, combined with reports that Solh spent most of last night reasoning with PLO leaders and various leftist figures, would appear to belie this claim.)[11] Socialist leader Kamal Jumblatt, who currently is allied to the Solh govt and Pres Frangie, has also been working hard to calm his leftist associates and act as go-between with the fedayeen. (Comment: Jumblatt finds himself in a somewhat ticklish position, sympathizing as he does with leftist-fedayeen positions but being obliged — out of loyalty to Frangie — to defend the Army’s February 26 intervention and subsequent actions in Sidon.)[12] Nevertheless, rumors (apparently false) of Saad’s death in hospital have kept leftist emotions at a high pitch and provoked angry demonstrations by small groups of university students in Beirut today. A country-wide general strike is being called for by leftist groups on February 28, and a mass protest demonstration is being planned in Beirut for the same day. (Comment: If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions.[13] We are taking necessary precautions to counsel prudence and alertness on the part of Embassy personnel.)

3.
In the short term, the withdrawal of Lebanese Army units from Sidon should have a calming effect, assuming that the PLO lives up to its alleged promise to bring “armed elements” under its control. Even if this should happen, however, the GOL may experience difficulty in fully reasserting its authority over Sidon.[14] Whatever happens, the events of February 26–27 have once again highlighted the difficult security problem (primarily, but not exclusively vis-à-vis fedayeen) which exists for the GOL not only in Sidon but also in other parts of Lebanon, where given the limited forces at its disposal the GOL — in order to preserve a minimal degree of peace and quiet — is often obliged to negotiate and even compromise with those elements who disturbed public order in the first place.[15]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]The fishermen’s protest: The immediate economic grievance was the granting of a fishing concession to Protéine, a modern trawler-fishing operation partly owned by former President Camille Chamoun. Small-boat fishermen in Sidon, whose livelihoods depended on traditional inshore fishing, faced ruin if industrial trawlers monopolized the coastal waters. But the concession was not merely an economic injury: it was widely read as a symbol of Maronite Christian commercial domination of Muslim Lebanon, extracted at the direct expense of the Sunni and Shi’ite poor of Sidon. The 1975 Sidon crisis is frequently reduced in secondary sources to “the fishermen’s protest,” as if the underlying issue were fish. The cables make clear that the issue was confessional economic resentment crystallizing around a visible grievance.
[2]Sidon (صيدا, Saida): The third-largest city of Lebanon, principal port of the south, and the largest predominantly Sunni Muslim city outside Beirut and Tripoli. By 1975 it was also the administrative center for the vast Palestinian refugee presence in South Lebanon — Ain al-Hilweh, the largest Palestinian camp in the country, lies on its outskirts. The city’s combination of Sunni political tradition, Palestinian armed presence, economic marginalization relative to Beirut, and proximity to the Israeli border made it a nexus of the pressures that would produce the civil war.
[3]“Armed elements” (العناصر المسلحة): A deliberately ambiguous diplomatic formula used by U.S. Embassy Beirut across these cables. It covered fedayeen organizations (who could not be named openly without creating political difficulties with the PLO leadership), Lebanese leftist militias associated with Maarouf Saad’s Popular Nasserist Organization, and the tactical overlap between the two. Throughout the Sidon crisis the phrase functions as a semantic fig leaf: everyone knew fedayeen were doing the shooting, but the Lebanese government publicly insisted they were not.
[4]Maarouf Saad (معروف سعد, 1910–1975): Sunni Nasserist politician, founder and leader of the Popular Nasserist Organization (التنظيم الشعبي الناصري). Elected to the Lebanese parliament multiple times from the Sidon district. A longstanding opponent of Chamounist politics and an ally of the Palestinian cause. Shot by Lebanese Army fire on February 26, 1975 while leading the fishermen’s demonstration (the Embassy notes he was “cut down by Army fire”). Died of his wounds on March 6, 1975. Historians including Kamal Salibi, Fawwaz Traboulsi, and Walid Khalidi identify Saad’s shooting as the direct precipitating event of the chain of incidents that produced the Lebanese Civil War on April 13, 1975.
[5]Camille Chamoun and the fishing concession: The concession was awarded to a joint-stock company called Protéine, in which Chamoun and a group of Christian Maronite business partners held a controlling interest. The concession granted exclusive rights to industrial trawler fishing in a zone extending from Sidon southward. Small-boat fishermen argued, correctly, that the trawlers would rapidly deplete inshore fish stocks and destroy their ability to make a living. The Solh government’s decision to approve the concession despite this objection — and the specific involvement of Chamoun, a figure deeply associated with Maronite political dominance — transformed an economic dispute into a confessional one.
[6]Sidon’s old quarter (المدينة القديمة): A densely packed medieval warren of stone buildings, narrow alleys, and flat rooftops organized around the Crusader-era Sea Castle and Castle of St. Louis. The geography is a classic irregular-warfare environment: defenders on rooftops have overlapping fields of fire, attackers on the ground have no cover, and the alleys funnel military vehicles into kill zones. Any Army attempt to clear the old quarter would require either very high casualties or the use of heavy weapons that would level centuries-old architecture. In practice neither option was politically available.
[7]SAM-7s in the Crusader citadel: The Strela-2 (NATO designation SA-7 Grail) was a Soviet-made shoulder-fired heat-seeking anti-aircraft missile with an effective range of about 4–5 kilometers. Introduced to Arab armies in the early 1970s and proven in combat during the October 1973 war, its migration into fedayeen hands by 1974–75 represented a qualitative escalation of the fedayeen arsenal. The 1974BEIRUT11970 cable in this archive also documents SAM-7s in fedayeen possession. The presence of these weapons in Sidon’s old city in February 1975 meant that any Israeli or Lebanese attempt to use helicopter gunships against positions there would face a credible threat — a constraint that shaped every subsequent decision about how to handle the crisis.
[8]The “ruse” hypothesis: The Embassy’s third paragraph presents, in considered diplomatic language, the hypothesis that the entire fishermen’s demonstration was a choreographed provocation. On this reading, rejectionist fedayeen factions (PFLP, PFLP-GC, ALF, and dissident Saʿiqa elements) pre-positioned gunmen on the rooftops of Sidon’s old quarter before the demonstration, knowing that the Army would be forced to intervene when protestors besieged city hall. Once the Army moved in, the fedayeen opened fire from elevated positions, provoking the melee. The political objective: embarrass the PLO leadership by demonstrating that the GOL was willing to use lethal force against Palestinians, and push Arafat into open confrontation with the Lebanese state. The cable presents this as an unconfirmed hypothesis, but the corroborating detail — that armed elements “may have been positioned there prior to this morning’s demonstration” — is significant. If correct, the Sidon uprising was not a spontaneous overreaction but a deliberate act of political engineering by a Palestinian faction seeking to bring about exactly the confrontation it produced.
[9]The Army withdrawal: The most consequential decision in these two cables. On February 26 the Army had intervened, fired on protestors, shot Maarouf Saad, and isolated the town with roadblocks. On February 27 the Army withdrew to the outskirts and let the PLO take over urban security. The Government of Lebanon had, in effect, conceded to the PLO that it would not again attempt to enter Sidon without PLO consent. This was not an arrangement that could be undone. The pattern established here — Army in, fedayeen resistance, Army out, PLO in formal control — would define the next two weeks and would directly produce the “army weakness” assessment the Embassy would file on March 12 (see cable 1975BEIRUT03203). It also confirmed, for Christian political figures across the spectrum, that the state could not protect its own sovereignty without confrontation — which provided the rationale the Phalange would invoke seven weeks later, on April 13, when its own militia rather than the Army carried out the reprisal for an attack.
[10]Rashid Solh (رشيد الصلح, 1926–2014): Sunni politician from the prominent Solh family of Beirut, cousin of Takieddin Solh, who had preceded him as Prime Minister. Rashid Solh served as Prime Minister from October 1974 until his resignation on May 15, 1975 — a resignation that came directly out of the Sidon crisis documented in this and subsequent cables. His political dilemma, visible across these cables, was that as a Sunni he could not deliver the Army reform the Sunni street and Jumblatt were demanding (because Frangieh refused it), but as Prime Minister he could not survive politically without some response to the Sidon violence. When he finally resigned in May, he did so on exactly this ground: that the Army command could not continue to be monopolized by Maronite Christian officers.
[11]The Embassy explicitly flags Solh’s public denial as implausible: having reported in paragraph 1 that heavily armed fedayeen were in open control of the town and that Solh had spent the night negotiating with the PLO, the Embassy notes drily that the Prime Minister’s public statement “fedayeen were not involved in any way” is contradicted by the same reporting. This kind of gap between public Lebanese political speech and private diplomatic reality was characteristic of the period. It was not simply that Solh was lying; it was that Lebanese politics required a set of public fictions (the fedayeen are guests here by invitation, the Army and the PLO cooperate in friendship, confessional balance is respected) without which the political system could not function. The cost of those fictions was that the underlying realities compounded in silence.
[12]Jumblatt’s “ticklish position”: This is the Embassy’s diagnosis of the central contradiction in Lebanese politics in February 1975. Kamal Jumblatt was simultaneously the paramount leader of the Lebanese left — which was aligned with the fedayeen — and a minister in a cabinet presided over by Frangieh, which required him to defend Army actions against those same fedayeen and their allies. This contradiction was sustainable only as long as no fedayeen-Army confrontation required him to choose. The Sidon crisis was that confrontation. Within seven weeks Jumblatt would be leading the Lebanese National Movement against the Frangieh government and in alliance with the PLO; within two years he would be dead, assassinated by Syrian agents after breaking with Damascus over a Syrian-backed partition plan.
[13]The Embassy’s prediction about Saad: “If Saad should actually die, these manifestations could assume serious proportions.” Saad died eight days later. The “manifestations” the Embassy predicted arrived on schedule — and are documented in the 1975BEIRUT03028 and 1975BEIRUT03064 cables, where they take the form of a nationwide general strike, mosque-centered demonstrations across every major Sunni town, and the paralysis of Beirut. The Sidon crisis proceeded, in this sense, on a timetable the Embassy had reported in advance. Knowing what was coming did not translate into the ability to prevent it.
[14]“The GOL may experience difficulty in fully reasserting its authority over Sidon”: Diplomatic understatement. The Lebanese state never again exercised full authority over Sidon before the civil war began. After the Army withdrawal of February 27, the city operated under fedayeen and PLO security for weeks. The March 1 attempt to reopen the coastal road (documented in cable 1975BEIRUT02837) was repelled by fedayeen ambush. By the time a negotiated settlement was reached on March 12–14 (documented in cables 1975BEIRUT03203 and 03256), the Army’s capacity to police South Lebanon had been visibly broken. Within a month the entire country would be at war.
[15]The closing observation of the cable: that the Lebanese state is “often obliged to negotiate and even compromise with those elements who disturbed public order in the first place.” This is the structural diagnosis. The GOL lacked the coercive capacity to impose its will on either the fedayeen or the leftist militias aligned with them, so every crisis ended with an accommodation that left the armed groups slightly stronger. Each accommodation was rational in isolation — the alternative was always an escalation the state could not win. But the cumulative effect, over years, was the construction of parallel security structures inside Lebanon which the state had no way to dismantle. The Embassy’s diagnosis in this closing paragraph of February 27, 1975 is the same diagnosis historians now give for why the civil war happened.
[16]Dwight J. Houghton: Deputy Chief of Mission (DCM) at U.S. Embassy Beirut, senior career Arabist, signing as acting head of mission on these cables in February 1975 during a period when Ambassador G. McMurtrie Godley (who had arrived in Beirut in September 1974) was apparently absent from post or not personally supervising the daily reporting. Houghton’s name appears on the Embassy’s reporting across much of the 1973–1975 period — his signature is on the 1973 Melkart Protocol cables and on the first days of the Sidon crisis. Cables filed from March 12 onward in this series (1975BEIRUT03203 and subsequent) bear Godley’s signature.
