The Calm Before: Lebanon's Political House of Cards, Hours Before the Shooting

This cable — a Washington relay of Embassy Beirut's assessment filed at 9:00 AM on May 7, 1973 — captures what Lebanon's political situation looked like in the hours before fighting erupted that evening. The PM had just announced on TV he would not resign. Three Arab mediators were actively negotiating. And reliable sources close to President Frangieh told the Embassy: there will be no new agreements, just tighter control in practice. By midnight, none of it held.

Based on cable 1973STATE086221 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

At 9:00 AM on May 7, 1973, the U.S. Embassy in Beirut filed a situation report that Washington then forwarded to Cairo. The picture it described was one of managed tension: isolated overnight incidents, Arab mediators shuttling between meetings, a Prime Minister who had just declared on television that he would not resign, and a President privately telling close sources there would be no new formal agreements — just practical tighter controls on Palestinian activity. By 10:00 PM that same evening, fighting had broken out across Beirut. By 11:30 PM, the Lebanese government had declared martial law. The calm documented in this cable lasted less than twelve hours.

What this document reveals
  • The calm documented in this cable — across the entire country as of 9:00 AM May 7 — lasted less than twelve hours. Fighting erupted that same evening (cable 1973BEIRUT05155). The Lebanese Army used aircraft and tanks against camps by morning (cable 1973BEIRUT05159).
  • Sa'iqa — a Syrian-backed Palestinian faction — abducted Lebanese customs officials the previous day and was freed by Syrian authorities. The cable records this as an isolated incident. In context, it is an instance of Syrian dual leverage documented on the morning of the same day Syrian-aligned factions launched the May 7 attack.
  • Frangieh's private position, relayed by 'very reliable sources': 'there will be no new accords or pieces of paper' — only a tacit understanding with practical controls. The crisis ended with the Melkart Protocol, another formal agreement, nine days later.
  • The cable's use of quotation marks around 'mediators' throughout signals Embassy skepticism: Syria and Egypt, whose envoys were 'most active,' both controlled Palestinian armed activity within their own borders while pressuring Lebanon to tolerate it on Lebanese soil.
  • Arafat was already, that morning, blaming renewed Bekaa firing on PFLP/GC and PDFLP — the same factions the Army's G-2 would identify twelve hours later as the instigators of the full-scale May 7 attack.

The Record

9:00 AM, May 7, 1973

At nine in the morning, the U.S. Embassy filed a situation report on Lebanon. It recorded calm. The previous day's incidents were isolated. The country was quiet. Arab mediators were in meetings. The Prime Minister had just announced on television that he would not resign. And reliable sources close to the President had told the Embassy something specific about his private position: there would be no new agreements, no documents, merely a practical tighter arrangement.

Twelve hours later, the fighting started.

The calm and its components

The overnight incidents recorded in this cable are individually modest but collectively instructive. Sa'iqa — the Syrian-backed Palestinian faction — abducted a dozen Lebanese customs officials from a northern border post. Syrian authorities freed them. A courthouse in Sidon was attacked. An army patrol exchanged fire with fedayeen near the Israeli border. The Tel Zaatar camp in eastern Beirut briefly fired on army positions.

Each incident was contained. None produced Lebanese casualties. The cable records them as isolated. Viewed in the context of the cables that followed within hours, they are something else: the texture of a situation in which Palestinian factions — including those directly connected to Syrian intelligence — were routinely testing Lebanese state authority, and in which Syrian state actors were simultaneously provoking and resolving incidents to demonstrate leverage.

What Frangieh was saying privately

The cable's fourth paragraph contains a direct report from "very reliable sources close to" President Frangieh. His private position was this: no new accords, no new pieces of paper. A tacit understanding, and tighter practical controls. The Lebanese Army — which Frangieh "relies more and more heavily" upon — was being kept out of the negotiations until the political noise subsided.

It is a strategy of controlled patience. The Army would not enter talks with the fedayeen at this moment of heightened emotion. It would wait. The politicians and special envoys would exhaust themselves. Then the Army would negotiate from a position of restored calm.

By midnight that same day, the Army had declared martial law and was engaged in fighting across the city.

The mediators and their contradictions

The cable names three Arab mediators active in Beirut: Syria's Khaddam, Egypt's al-Kholi, and the Arab League's Mahmoud Riad. It puts "mediators" in quotation marks each time. The Embassy's skepticism is embedded in the punctuation.

Frangieh made the contradiction explicit in private. He told those around him that Lebanon had as much right to enforce its sovereignty over Palestinians as Syria and Egypt did — and that Syria and Egypt, whose envoys were the most active in Beirut pressing Lebanon to accommodate Palestinian armed groups, both exercised strict control over Palestinian military activity within their own borders. The arrangement they were mediating applied to Lebanon what neither was willing to apply to itself.

The document this cable is not

This is a Washington relay cable — a State Department document forwarding Embassy Beirut's morning report to the U.S. Interests Section in Cairo, which lacked direct access to the Beirut cable traffic. The content is Embassy Beirut's (signed HOUGHTON); the outer document is Washington's (signed ROGERS, for Secretary of State William Rogers). It reaches Cairo as the fight is already starting in Beirut.

It was, at the moment of its creation, a record of stability. Within the same calendar day, it became a document of the hours before the Lebanese government declared martial law for the first time in years, and the Lebanese Army began using aircraft and tank fire against Palestinian camps.

Archival note: This is a State Department cable (from Washington) that relays and forwards to Cairo the text of Beirut cable 5152 — a separate cable sent earlier the same morning of May 7, 1973. The quoted content was drafted by Embassy Beirut (signed HOUGHTON); the outer cable was drafted in Washington by M. Sterner of the Near Eastern and South Asian Affairs bureau and approved by the same officer. The content below is that of the quoted Beirut cable.[1]

Summary
The situation in Lebanon is one of continuing overall calm with occasional incidents, as efforts by Lebanese politicians and Arab “mediators” to “resolve the crisis” continue. Prime Minister Hafez[2] has declared he will not resign, and we understand that President Frangieh[3] remains determined that any modus vivendi[4] concluded with the fedayeen[5] will enable the GOL to place tighter controls on their activity.

2.
As of 0900Z [9:00 AM GMT] on May 7, the situation on the ground remains quiet throughout the country. The only incidents reported on May 6 were:

  • Abduction by Sa’iqa elements[6] of around a dozen Lebanese customs officials from their northern border post at Aboudiyyeh. Syrian authorities freed them shortly afterwards.[7]
  • An early morning explosion and machine-gun attack at the Sidon courthouse (light damage).
  • An exchange of fire between an army patrol and fedayeen approximately 7 kilometers north of the Israeli border in the western sector (at least two fedayeen wounded, according to the army).
  • A brief outbreak of firing from Tal Zaatar camp[8] in the eastern suburbs of Beirut, which drew an army riposte.

All incidents appeared isolated, and no Lebanese casualties were reported. Near midnight on May 6, there was renewed firing in the southeastern Bekaa, but this was quickly brought under control, and Arafat was blaming it on the PFLP/GC and/or PDFLP.[9]

3.
On the political front, collective and individual efforts by Lebanese politicians and Arab “mediators” — including Syrian Foreign Minister Khaddam,[10] Egypt’s Hassan Sabri al-Kholi,[11] and Arab League Secretary General Mahmoud Riad[12] — continue, with numerous meetings on May 5–6 and continuing today.

Prime Minister Hafez made a noteworthy statement on television on the evening of May 5, announcing that he would stay on and expressing hope that problems with the fedayeen could be resolved through mutual cooperation and the help of Arab mediators. He stated his task was to reassure “Palestinian brothers” that President Frangieh, the army, and the Lebanese people support their cause, and to convince the Lebanese that the fedayeen have no intention of infringing on Lebanon’s sovereignty. We noted that in the aftermath of Hafez’s statement, both Rashid Karame[13] and Kamal Jumblatt[14] are no longer pressing him to resign.

4.
We have been told by very reliable sources close to President Frangieh that there will be no new accords or pieces of paper, merely some form of tacit understanding, and that the GOL will — RPT will — succeed in imposing tighter controls on the fedayeen. However, there is no way of predicting at present just what these controls will be in terms of either a new “understanding” or in actual practice.[15]

Both Arab “mediators” and Lebanese politicians appear to have a great deal of talking left in them. At the same time, Frangieh is reliably reported to be taking a firm and clear stand on the basic principle of Lebanese sovereignty, telling all concerned that the GOL has as much right to enforce its sovereignty over Palestinians as such other Arab governments as Syria and Egypt (whose envoys are most active).[16]

The Lebanese Army — upon which Frangieh relies more and more heavily and which will have a major voice in any eventual “understanding” — has not yet been engaged in talks with the fedayeen. Frangieh and the army apparently intend to keep their powder dry until passions have further subsided and politicians and special envoys begin to lose their voice.[17]

HOUGHTON [quoted] / ROGERS[18] [outer cable]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]This document (1973STATE086221) is a State Department cable from Washington that quotes, verbatim, Embassy Beirut cable 5152 — a cable filed earlier on May 7, 1973, before the fighting that night. The State cable was routed to the U.S. Interests Section in Cairo, which did not receive the original Beirut distribution. The outer cable was drafted and approved by M. Sterner of the Near Eastern Affairs bureau; the quoted content was authored by the Embassy (signed HOUGHTON). The cable ID 1973STATE086221 represents the Washington relay document; the original source is BEIRUT 5152. Both record the same political assessment: the calm that existed on the morning of May 7, before fighting erupted that evening.
[2]Amin al-Hafez (1911–1989): Lebanese Sunni Muslim politician, Prime Minister of Lebanon from April 1973. His television statement on May 5 — announcing he would not resign and framing his role as bridge between the Lebanese state and the Palestinian cause — represented a carefully balanced political position that attempted to maintain both Lebanese Army support and Arab mediator goodwill. Within hours of this cable's source material being written, the fighting of May 7 would make that balance untenable; Hafez resigned at 5:00 AM on May 8 before withdrawing his resignation later that day.
[3]President Suleiman Frangieh (1910–1992): President of Lebanon 1970–1976, a Maronite from northern Lebanon with close ties to the Assad family in Syria. The cable's description of Frangieh's position — seeking tighter controls on Palestinian activity, not new formal agreements — is presented as coming from "very reliable sources close to" him. His private stance of demanding sovereignty enforcement while allowing Arab mediators to manage the public process is documented here in the morning; the night's fighting would force a different public posture.
[4]Modus vivendi (Latin: "way of living"): a diplomatic term for a practical working arrangement between parties who cannot reach a formal agreement, allowing them to coexist without a definitive resolution of underlying disputes. The cable's use of this term — attributed to Frangieh's private position — is precise: Frangieh sought not a treaty but a lived arrangement in which Lebanon could exercise tighter actual control without formal documentation that might constrain him or be cited against him.
[5]Fedayeen (Arabic: فدائيين, "those who sacrifice themselves"): Palestinian armed fighters. In the Lebanese context, the term refers collectively to armed members of PLO constituent organizations operating from bases in Lebanese refugee camps under the framework of the 1969 Cairo Agreement.
[6]Sa'iqa ("Vanguards of the Popular Liberation War"): Syrian Ba'ath-sponsored Palestinian armed faction. The abduction of Lebanese customs officials by Sa'iqa elements from a northern border post — and their release by Syrian authorities — is a textbook example of Syrian dual leverage: Sa'iqa created the provocation, Syrian authorities resolved it, positioning Damascus as both problem and solution. This pattern is documented in the morning's cable as an "isolated incident"; the subsequent night's fighting involved Sa'iqa among other Syrian-aligned factions.
[7]The detail that "Syrian authorities freed them soon afterwards" is diplomatically significant. It establishes a pattern: a Syrian-aligned Palestinian faction acts against Lebanese state personnel and infrastructure; Syrian state authorities then intervene to remedy the incident. This positions Syria as simultaneously enabling the provocation and performing the resolution — a leverage mechanism that the cable records without explicitly characterizing.
[8]Tel Zaatar: Palestinian refugee camp in the Dekwaneh area of northeast Beirut, situated within predominantly Christian-controlled eastern suburbs — one of the geographically most exposed Palestinian camps in the city. Its appearance in a cable of May 1973 firing on Lebanese Army positions is an early instance of the confrontation dynamic that would intensify. In August 1976, Tel Zaatar was besieged and overrun by Maronite militias; approximately 1,500–2,000 Palestinians were killed. This May 1973 cable predates that event by over three years.
[9]Arafat blaming the renewed Bekaa firing on PFLP/GC and/or PDFLP — on the same night that the next cable (1973BEIRUT05155) records the outbreak of full-scale fighting — is a document of Arafat's political positioning in real time. He was deflecting responsibility to the harder-line factions, which the Army's G-2 would confirm the following morning (cable 1973BEIRUT05159) were indeed the primary instigators of the May 7 fighting.
[10]Abdul Halim Khaddam (1932–2020): Syrian Ba'ath politician, Foreign Minister 1970–1984, later Vice President of Syria. His role as an Arab "mediator" in the Lebanese-Palestinian crisis of May 1973 — a role he performed simultaneously with Syrian-aligned Palestinian factions actively attacking Lebanese positions — is documented across this cable series. The next morning's cable (1973BEIRUT05159) records GOL suspicion that Khaddam had publicly announced terms in Damascus far more favorable to the Palestinians than what had actually been agreed, and names the "Syrian hand working behind the scene" as a key Lebanese concern.
[11]Hassan Sabri al-Kholi: Egyptian diplomat and close aide to President Anwar Sadat, serving as Egypt's special envoy in multiple Arab political crises during the early 1970s. His presence as a mediator in Beirut in May 1973 reflects the broader Arab interest in managing Lebanese-Palestinian tensions at a moment when Egypt was preparing for what would become the October 1973 War. The Egyptian role in the 1969 Cairo Agreement — under which Egypt pressured Lebanon to grant the PLO an armed presence on Lebanese soil — gave Cairo a particular stake in the stability of the arrangement it had brokered.
[12]Mahmoud Riad (1917–1992): Egyptian diplomat, Secretary General of the Arab League 1972–1979. His presence as a mediator represents the Arab League's institutional investment in containing the Lebanese-Palestinian confrontation. Like Khaddam and al-Kholi, Riad operated within a framework in which Arab state interests — and not Lebanese sovereignty — were the primary reference point. The cable's use of quotation marks around "mediators" throughout reflects Embassy skepticism about whether the Arab states' role was genuinely aimed at stabilization.
[13]Rashid Karame (1921–1987): Sunni Muslim politician from Tripoli, a former Prime Minister of Lebanon multiple times. A consistent advocate for the Palestinian cause within the Lebanese political system, Karame's decision to stop pressing Hafez to resign — in the aftermath of the PM's May 5 television statement — indicates a temporary stabilization of the Sunni political bloc around Hafez's position. Karame was assassinated in June 1987 by a bomb placed in his helicopter.
[14]Kamal Jumblatt (1917–1977): Druze feudal chieftain of the Chouf, leader of the Progressive Socialist Party, and head of the Lebanese National Movement — the coalition of Lebanese leftist and pan-Arab parties aligned with the PLO. His suspension of pressure on Hafez mirrors the broader temporary stabilization recorded in this cable. On April 13, 1975 — the opening of the civil war — Jumblatt immediately called for the removal of Maronite ministers from the Lebanese cabinet, converting that day's violence into political leverage. He was assassinated in March 1977, almost certainly by Syrian intelligence.
[15]"There will be no new accords or pieces of paper, merely some form of tacit understanding": Frangieh's private position — as relayed to the Embassy by "very reliable sources" — is a direct repudiation of the formal agreement approach embodied by the 1969 Cairo Agreement. He sought practical enforcement over documentary commitments. The irony documented in this cable series is that the crisis ended not with the tacit understanding Frangieh wanted, but with the Melkart Protocol of May 17, 1973 — another "piece of paper" in the tradition of the Cairo Agreement. The fundamental issue of Palestinian armed activity on Lebanese soil remained unresolved.
[16]Frangieh's invocation of Syrian and Egyptian sovereignty enforcement over Palestinians — telling Arab mediators that Lebanon has "as much right" as Syria and Egypt to control Palestinian armed activity — is a pointed rhetorical move. Both Syria and Egypt exercised strict control over Palestinian armed activity within their own territory, refusing to allow the PLO to operate autonomously across their borders or within their populations. The Arab consensus that permitted Palestinian armed activity in Lebanon was therefore explicitly asymmetric: what Lebanon was expected to tolerate was not tolerated by the states most actively pressuring Lebanon to tolerate it.
[17]"Keep their powder dry until passions have further subsided and politicians and special envoys begin to lose their voice": the Embassy's characterization of Frangieh's and the army's strategy — waiting for the diplomatic process to exhaust itself before entering direct talks — proved both accurate and consequential. Within twelve hours of this cable's source being written, the fighting of May 7 had erupted, the army was engaged, and the political framework had collapsed into a military confrontation. The "dry powder" was used sooner than anticipated.
[18]ROGERS: the signature of the outer State Department cable, referring to William P. Rogers (1913–2001), U.S. Secretary of State from January 1969 to September 1973. Cables dispatched from Washington in the Secretary's name were signed ROGERS regardless of whether Rogers personally drafted or reviewed them. M. Sterner (full name: Michael Sterner), who drafted and approved the cable, was a Near Eastern Affairs desk officer.
