Beirut, April 15, 1975

The Second Day: What Spread Beyond Beirut

This cable, filed April 15, 1975 at noon, documents how the violence of April 13 spread beyond Beirut within 48 hours. Three churches were bombed in Tripoli. Three non-combatant Christian villages near Sidon were rocketed and then entered. A hospital was occupied and used as a firing position. These events appear in this cable. They do not appear in the standard account of April 1975.

Based on cable 1975BEIRUT04829 CONFIDENTIAL → UNCLASSIFIED

Less than 48 hours after April 13, 1975, the U.S. Embassy filed this two-section situation report as of noon on April 15. The casualty count had reached 80 to 90 throughout Lebanon. The cable documents the geographic spread of violence beyond Beirut: three Christian churches bombed in Tripoli, three non-Phalangist Christian villages near Sidon rocketed from refugee camps and then entered by armed forces. It also documents, on the basis of the Embassy’s own assessment, that the casualty balance at that point ran against the Palestinian and Muslim side — not against the Phalange.

What this document reveals
  • By noon on April 15 — less than 48 hours after April 13 — this cable records 80 to 90 dead throughout Lebanon. The Health Ministry was issuing appeals for blood donors and doctors. This figure is documented in this cable. It does not appear in the standard account of these events.
  • This cable documents that three Christian churches were bombed in Tripoli on the evening of April 14, 1975. This event appears in paragraph 10 of this cable. It does not appear in the standard account of the first days of the Lebanese Civil War.
  • Three Christian villages in the Mieh Mieh region near Sidon — explicitly described as not Phalangist — were rocketed from Palestinian camps. When villagers did not return fire, the cable records that armed forces advanced into the villages and shot civilian family members of a Lebanese security official. This event appears in paragraph 11 of this cable. It does not appear in the standard account.
  • The Embassy’s own assessment at noon on April 15: “The head count to date appears to be in favor of the Phalange.” The Embassy presented this as partial corroboration of Fatah’s claim of restraint. This assessment is documented in this cable. It has not been cited in the standard account.
  • Arafat had remained aloof from all consultations by noon on April 15. He was in Damascus. Arab League Secretary-General Riad was meeting with Abu Iyad and Nayif Hawatmeh — but not Arafat. This absence, documented here, is consistent with the internal PLO command dispute described in the subsequent cable (1975BEIRUT04860).

The Record

Forty-eight hours in

By noon on April 15, 1975 — less than 48 hours after April 13 — the U.S. Embassy estimated 80 to 90 people dead throughout Lebanon. The Health Ministry was calling for blood donors and ordering medical staff to report immediately. The major portion of the capital’s population, the cable records, remained hunkered down in their homes.

This cable, filed at the IMMEDIATE level in two sections, is the Embassy’s most detailed situational account of the crisis’s first 48 hours. It documents events in Beirut, in Tripoli, and in villages near Sidon. What happened outside Beirut does not appear in the standard account of these events.

What the cable documents outside Beirut

In Tripoli, Lebanon’s second city, two major demonstrations occurred on April 14. At least two people were killed and seven wounded. Three Christian churches were bombed that evening. The headquarters of the National Liberal Party — Camille Chamoun’s organization — was sacked.

Near Sidon, three villages in the Mieh Mieh region — the cable specifies they were Christian, and explicitly not Phalangist — were rocketed from nearby refugee camps. According to what the cable describes as a usually reliable Lebanese source, when the villagers did not return fire, armed forces advanced into the villages. At one village, the father and brother of a Lebanese security official were seized and shot.

The Embassy presents this as a single-source account and offers no characterization beyond the documentary record. The event appears in paragraph 11 of this cable, filed before 2:00 PM on April 15, 1975, on record at the State Department. It does not appear in the standard account of these days.

The Embassy’s own casualty assessment

Paragraph 9 of this cable contains a specific assessment: Fatah is claiming its forces are showing real restraint in the face of Phalange “provocations.” The Embassy states: “We are unable to confirm this claim, but it is quite possibly true, since the head count to date appears to be in favor of the Phalange.”

This is the U.S. Embassy’s own assessment, on the record, at noon on April 15. The casualty balance, in the Embassy’s judgment, ran against the Palestinian and Muslim side, not against the Kataeb. This appears in this cable. It has not been cited in the standard account of the Lebanese Civil War’s first days.

Arafat’s absence

Paragraph 7 records that Arab League Secretary-General Riad was meeting directly with Abu Iyad (Fatah’s security chief) and Nayif Hawatmeh (PDFLP leader) on April 15. Arafat “was not present” and had “remained aloof from consultations.” Latest reports placed him in Damascus.

Read alongside the subsequent cable (1975BEIRUT04860), which records an unconfirmed report that Arafat had been favoring a ceasefire for 36 hours while Abu Iyad opposed it, these two cables together document that Abu Iyad was the effective PLO decision-maker in Beirut during the critical period of April 14–15 — with Arafat geographically and apparently politically absent from the negotiations.

The Embassy’s forward-looking warning

The cable closes paragraph 9 with a direct assessment of what would happen if the PLO chose to escalate: residents of Beirut and other parts of the country “will probably have little choice but to batten hatches and try to hold on.” The Embassy notes it had been reporting for “several years” that Palestinian armed forces were positioned in the capital for “sustained urban warfare and terrorism.”

This assessment, documented before the civil war had fully begun, is what the Embassy understood the stakes to be on April 15, 1975. What it was warning about would not materialize — that escalation — for another year.

Summary
Conditions on the ground in Beirut remain unsettled with intermittent firing and explosions occurring in various parts of the city, as political efforts to defuse the situation have thus far failed to produce tangible results. American citizens continue to be safe, although a car belonging to an Embassy employee was bombed last night and foreigners, as well as Lebanese, are unable to move freely in the city. Tourists should be discouraged from visiting Lebanon at this time. Palestinians — the main elements of which have not yet thrown their full weight into the balance — and leftist parties continue to call for punishment of the Phalange. Strife over the last 24 hours has spread to other parts of the country and shown the first signs of assuming a confessional [sectarian] character.[1]

1.
The country passed a night described by a Beirut police official as “bad” and awoke to continued explosions and clashes in various localities. As casualty reports continue to rise — we would place the number killed to date at 80 to 90 —[2] and the Health Ministry issued appeals for blood donors and for doctors and nurses to report immediately to their hospitals, the major portion of the population in the capital at least remains hunkered down in their homes.

2.
Dekwaneh, Tel Zaatar, and Ain Ramanneh remained the primary Beirut centers of incidents (sniping, bombings, and shelling) during the evening and morning, but Sinn al Fil reportedly sustained considerable material damage. Palestinians are said to have occupied Quarantina Hospital[3] during the afternoon of April 14 and were reportedly rocketing Phalangist targets in Ashrafiye[4] from that position. Three large explosions (possibly rockets) have occurred in the Quarantine district near the port since dawn, the first killing one civilian and injuring three. A 4-kilogram package of plastique with a timer was removed from the Napoleon Hotel (one block off Hamra) around noon April 15.

3.
Security forces (police and gendarmerie with armored vehicles) have moved in strength in troubled areas, but their mission is not yet clear. One reasonably reliable report stated that Squad 16[5] moved against Phalange offices in Rue George Picot early this morning. Prime Minister Solh has again repeated his intention to apply the law and punish the culpable, but rejected proclamation of a state of emergency. PSP leader Kamal Jumblatt, who continues to play a key role among both the GOL leadership and those highly critical of the GOL, opposed calling out the army. Military sources inform us they are preparing for the possibility that the army might be called out at approximately 1900Z tonight.

4.
All American citizens [AMCITS] remain safe at the moment. Clashes, however, are beginning to affect the foreign community. A vehicle belonging to a member of the Embassy staff (DEA) was destroyed at approximately 0200Z while parked near his residence in Raouche. The explosive was apparently a single stick of dynamite tossed under the car, which bore white diplomatic plates. The French ambassador was awakened last evening when a stray bullet entered his bedroom; the Italian ambassador has moved into the city from his residence in an affected area.

5.
The airport is still operating and the access road is open. An Embassy officer returning from the airport this morning reported sighting several armed and uniformed fedayeen in the airport circle, but they made no effort to disrupt traffic. Pan American, however, reports it will run out of jet fuel as of 1100Z today, which will probably result in the cancellation or diversion of some flights. Refineries in Sidon and Tripoli have supplies but cannot deliver: tank trucks cannot pass Sidon; workers in Tripoli report high absenteeism and evidence of unknown elements preventing them from reaching the plant. Industry sources estimate local gas stations will begin to run out of gasoline by Wednesday or Thursday if resupply is not possible.[6]

6.
In view of unsettled conditions in Lebanon, the Embassy has informed the Department by separate cable that tourists should be discouraged from coming at this time. Most diplomatic and other social functions are being cancelled.

7.
On the political scene, there is considerable activity but no concrete results yet apparent. Arab League Secretary-General Riad arrived Beirut last evening and began a series of conciliation meetings, including sessions with Arab ambassadors in Beirut and with Abu Iyad (Fatah)[7] and Nayif Hawatmeh (PDFLP).[8] Arafat was not present and, to the best we can determine, has remained aloof from consultations. Latest reports place him in Damascus today after having spent part of yesterday in South Lebanon.[9] Riad has met with Solh and Frangieh and saw Jumblatt at the St. George Hotel this morning. A meeting between Riad and Gemayel has not yet been scheduled.

8.
Prime Minister Solh held a series of sessions during yesterday afternoon, including one with the cabinet that is reliably reported to have degenerated into a shouting match. Two ministers affiliated with Kamal Jumblatt’s PSP — Abbas Khalaf and Khaled Jumblatt — have announced their resignations, made contingent on Solh meeting their demands that those guilty (read: Phalange) in Sunday’s incidents be brought to “justice” and that the government restore order. As we noted in Beirut 4749, loss of Jumblatt’s support would knock the remaining prop from under the Solh government, which is already staggering.

9.
Fatah and other major fedayeen spokesmen are claiming their forces are showing real restraint in the face of Phalange “provocations.” We are unable to confirm this claim, but it is quite possibly true, since the head count to date appears to be in favor of the Phalange.[10] Splinter groups among the fedayeen and probably undisciplined elements of the more “moderate” organizations, however, do not appear to be exercising restraint, and it is questionable whether the major Palestinian groups can avoid a more aggressive stance indefinitely if the basic situation is not contained soon. Should PLO/Fatah take the decision to go all-out, residents of Beirut and other parts of the country will probably have little choice but to batten hatches and try to hold on. As we have reported over the last several years, we believe the fedayeen are well situated, particularly in the capital, for sustained urban warfare and terrorism which would take maximum toll in lives and property.[11]

10.
In addition to the prolongation of the current crisis — with the outlines of a denouement still not in sight — a disturbing element is the apparent spread of clashes geographically and indications that the strife may be taking a turn toward a Christian vs. Muslim/Palestinian conflict. Tripoli[12] witnessed two major demonstrations on April 14, in which at least two were killed and seven wounded, and three Christian churches were bombed last evening.[13] Yesterday’s events also included the gratuitous sacking of Chamoun’s NLP headquarters in Tripoli. NLP offices near the Foreign Ministry in Beirut were the object of an attempted rocketing this morning.

11.
Phalangist offices in Zahle and nearby Sadnail were bombed last evening. The Phalange office in Sidon was attacked and later rocketed from refugee camps outside Sidon. Three Christian — not Phalangist — villages in the Mieh Mieh region near Sidon were rocketed from camps.[14] According to one usually reliable Lebanese source, when the villagers did not return fire, Palestinians advanced into them and, at one village, seized the father and brother of a Lebanese security official and shot them.[15] Just as continuing Palestinian or Muslim casualties must eventually inflame the sentiment of even more moderate compatriots, so Christian losses will tend to engage the emotions of Christians who have thus far not supported the Phalange.[16]

Editorial Footnotes
[1]"Confessional coloration": diplomatic language for violence that has begun to divide along religious community lines rather than remaining a dispute between specific armed groups. The cable uses this phrasing cautiously: the violence is showing "first signs" of assuming this character, not that it has fully become sectarian. This distinction matters — the Embassy is documenting a trajectory, not a completed transformation.
[2]By noon on April 15 — less than 48 hours after April 13 — the Embassy estimated 80 to 90 dead throughout Lebanon. This figure, documented in this cable filed before 2:00 PM on April 15, 1975, does not appear in the standard account of the first days of the Lebanese Civil War.
[3]Quarantina Hospital: the American University of Beirut-affiliated hospital in the Quarantine district near the Beirut port. The cable records that it was occupied by Palestinian forces on the afternoon of April 14 and used as a firing position against Ashrafiye, a predominantly Christian neighborhood. The Quarantina district itself — a mixed working-class neighborhood of Lebanese Christians, Kurds, and Armenians — would be overrun by Maronite militias in January 1976, approximately nine months after this cable.
[4]Ashrafiye: predominantly Christian neighborhood in East Beirut. The cable documents that it was being rocketed from Quarantina Hospital — a medical facility under Geneva Convention protection — on April 14–15. This specific detail appears in this Embassy cable and does not figure in the standard account of these events.
[5]Squad 16: an internal security unit of the Lebanese Army. The cable notes it was reportedly deployed against Phalange offices — a detail that, taken with the prior cable (1975BEIRUT04749, para 6B), documents the Lebanese Army taking some limited action against one side. Whether this represents state enforcement or political positioning is recorded as unclear by the Embassy.
[6]The cable documents significant civilian infrastructure failure by noon on April 15 — less than 48 hours after April 13: airport fuel nearly exhausted, refineries blocked, civilian gasoline supply projected to fail by April 17–18. These logistics details appear in this cable and establish the speed at which the April 13 incidents produced country-wide disruption.
[7]Abu Iyad: Salah Khalaf (1933–1991), Fatah’s security chief and Arafat’s designated deputy. This cable documents that on April 15, Riad was meeting with Abu Iyad directly. The subsequent cable (1975BEIRUT04860) records an unconfirmed report that Abu Iyad had been opposing a ceasefire for 36 hours before Arafat returned from Damascus. Read together, these two cables document Abu Iyad as the primary PLO interlocutor in Beirut during the critical April 14–15 period.
[8]Nayif Hawatmeh (b. 1935): founder and leader of the PDFLP (Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine). The cable documents him in direct consultations with Arab League Secretary-General Riad on April 15, while Arafat was in Damascus. This is consistent with the PDFLP’s documented involvement in the April 13 events (cable 1975BEIRUT04749 attributes the initial Volkswagen driver to the PDFLP).
[9]This cable documents that Arafat had "remained aloof from consultations" on April 15 and was in Damascus. This is consistent with and provides context for the subsequent cable (1975BEIRUT04860, paragraph 3), which records an unconfirmed report that Arafat had been in Damascus while Abu Iyad opposed a ceasefire in Beirut. The two cables, read sequentially, document Arafat’s physical absence from Beirut during the critical early-crisis period and provide the context in which the internal PLO command dispute may have occurred.
[10]"Head count to date appears to be in favor of the Phalange": the Embassy’s own assessment on April 15, 1975, was that the casualty balance ran against the Palestinian/Muslim side, not in their favor. The Embassy presents this as partial corroboration of the Fatah restraint claim. This assessment appears in this cable. It has not been cited in the standard account of the Lebanese Civil War’s first days, which typically frames the April 13 bus attack as the initiating act of unprovoked Phalange violence.
[11]This paragraph documents the Embassy’s long-standing assessment — based on "several years" of reporting — that the Palestinian armed forces were well positioned in Beirut for sustained urban warfare and terrorism. This assessment predates April 13, 1975, and contextualizes the structural military reality that made the April 13 incidents so significant: the PLO had the capacity to escalate to full urban warfare, and the Lebanese state did not have the capacity to prevent it.
[12]Tripoli: Lebanon’s second-largest city, located in the north. With a predominantly Sunni Muslim population, Tripoli was a base for pro-Palestinian and leftist forces. The cable documents the spread of violence to Tripoli within 48 hours of April 13, including attacks on specifically Christian targets.
[13]The bombing of three Christian churches in Tripoli on the evening of April 14 is documented in this cable. This event — representing targeted attacks on places of Christian worship within 24 hours of April 13 — appears in this Embassy cable. It does not appear in the standard account of the first days of the Lebanese Civil War.
[14]The rocketing of three Christian villages in the Mieh Mieh region near Sidon — explicitly described as "not Phalangist" — is documented in this cable as occurring on April 14–15. The cable distinguishes these villages from Phalangist targets; they were not party combatants. This event appears in this cable. It does not appear in the standard account of these events.
[15]The cable reports, on the basis of one usually reliable source, that when the villagers of one of the Mieh Mieh region villages did not return fire, Palestinian forces advanced into the village and seized and shot a Lebanese security official’s father and brother. The Embassy sources this as a single-source report and presents it with appropriate qualification. The event — if accurate as reported — documents the taking and shooting of civilian family members in a non-combatant village that had declined to return fire. This appears in this cable.
[16]Paragraph 11’s final sentence is one of the cable’s most analytically significant: "Just as continuing Palestinian or Muslim casualties must eventually inflame the sentiment of even more moderate compatriots, so Christian losses will tend to engage the emotions of Christians who have thus far not supported the Phalange." The Embassy is documenting, as of noon April 15, the dynamic by which a militia conflict was becoming a community conflict — and doing so symmetrically, attributing the same dynamic to both sides.
[17]G. McMurtrie Godley (1917–1999), U.S. Ambassador to Lebanon. This is the third major cable filed by Godley in the April 13 crisis, following 1975BEIRUT04749 (April 14) and preceding 1975BEIRUT04860 (April 16). Together, the three cables document the continuous Embassy reporting from the first hours of the civil war through the first attempted ceasefire.
